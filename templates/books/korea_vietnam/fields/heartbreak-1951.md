### The campaign
Armistice talks opened at Kaesong in July 1951. The front stayed still while they went on, but neither side stopped fighting. Hermes says the Communist delegates called off the meetings on the night of 22 August, and early on the 23rd they said a UN aircraft had bombed the conference site. The UN investigators did not believe it, and Ridgway thought the break had been planned in advance.

In the east, X Corps under Major General Clovis E. Byers held the line round the Punchbowl. The 2nd Infantry Division attacked Bloody Ridge from 18 August. The ROK 36th Regiment took it and was driven off. Attacks by the US 9th Infantry failed, and the ridge fell only when the 23rd and 38th Infantry worked round both flanks. The North Koreans left on 4 and 5 September, leaving more than 500 dead on the heights. On 5 September Van Fleet dropped a larger offensive in favour of limited attacks to tidy up the line.

The North Koreans dug in again about 1,500 yards north, on a hill mass some 7 miles long. Its peaks were Hill 894 in the south, Hill 931, about 1,300 yards beyond it and the highest, and Hill 851, about 2,100 yards further north. The press came to call it Heartbreak Ridge. Korea's veterans' ministry says the name came from a UPI reporter, Stan Carter, after he saw a wounded soldier at an aid station.

### The places
- **Hill 931**: the highest peak and the 23rd Infantry's objective, taken and lost several times in September.
- **Hill 894**: the southern peak, taken by the 2nd Battalion, 9th Infantry, on 15 September.
- **Hill 851**: the northern peak, the last taken, on 13 October.
- **Hill 520**: a small hump at the western tip of a spur off the crest, taken by G Company, 2nd Battalion, 23rd Infantry, on 10 October.
- **The Sat'ae-ri valley**: east of the ridge, up which the 23rd first attacked.
- **The Mundung-ni valley**: west of the ridge, up which the tanks went from 10 October.
- **Bloody Ridge**: to the south, fought over from 18 August to 5 September.
- **The Punchbowl**: the round valley nearby; the battle took the sag out of X Corps' line there.
- **Panmunjom**: where the talks began again on 25 October 1951.

### The leaders
- **Clovis E. Byers** (UN, X Corps): gave Hill 931 first priority, and with Young called off the September attacks.
- **Thomas E. de Shazo** (UN, 2nd Division, acting): sent in the 23rd Infantry on 13 September. Wikipedia spells him Deshazo.
- **Robert N. Young** (UN, 2nd Division): took command on 20 September and later called the first attacks a "fiasco" (Hermes).
- **James Y. Adams** (UN, 23rd Infantry): told Young on 26 September it was "suicide" to keep to the plan.
- **Henry F. Daniels** and **Virgil E. Craven** (UN, 2nd and 3rd Battalions, 23rd Infantry): led the night attack of 5 October.
- **Ralph Monclar** (UN, French Battalion): the name taken by Raoul Magrin-Vernerey, a general who stepped down to lieutenant-colonel to lead the battalion.
- **John O. Woods** (UN, 72nd Tank Battalion): led the tanks up the Mundung-ni valley.
- **Robert W. Love** (UN, 2nd Engineer Combat Battalion): built the road the tanks used.
- **Hong Nim** (North Korean, 6th Division): sent the fresh 13th Regiment onto the ridge in mid-September (Hermes).

### The battle
The sources give hours without naming a clock; the times here are taken as local. At 05:30 on 13 September a 30-minute bombardment began, and the 23rd Infantry went up from the east, Craven's 3rd Battalion leading and Daniels's 2nd behind. Hidden bunkers held by the 1st Regiment of the North Korean 6th Division stopped them at the foot of the spur by nightfall. The 2nd Battalion, 9th Infantry, took Hill 894 on the 15th and lost over 200 men to counter-attacks in the next two days. Through the rest of the month the 23rd attacked 931 again and again. Its men reached the crest on 23 September, but the 3rd Regiment of the 12th Division threw them off. On 26 September the French Battalion and the 23rd failed against the 15th Regiment. On the 27th Young and Byers stopped the attacks. The 23rd had lost over 950 men.

Hermes says North Korean mortars caused about 85 per cent of the division's casualties up to then. Korean civilian porters often dropped their loads and ran when the enemy came close, so riflemen had to carry the supplies and litters. Bringing a litter case down from the 23rd's forward positions could take up to ten hours. No source read for this page says how many of the ridge's wounded went out by helicopter; across the war, Zimmerman says, helicopters carried more than 20,000 casualties to the surgical hospitals.

Young's new plan, Operation Touchdown, was issued on 2 October. The whole division would attack together, and Love's engineers cleared a road up the Mundung-ni valley for the Shermans, blasting the roadblocks and mines by hand while the bulldozers were under repair. A small task force raided up the Sat'ae-ri valley on 3 and 4 October to draw attention there. Artillery and Marine Corsairs began the preparation in the late afternoon of 5 October, and the attack went in at 21:00. Hermes says the North Korean regiments facing it were all far under strength. By 03:00 on the 6th the 2nd and 3rd Battalions of the 23rd held the southern half of Hill 931. They beat off a counter-attack, and before noon they and the French had the whole hill.

{{picture:heartbreak-from-north}}

On 10 October the 72nd Tank Battalion, with L Company of the 38th Infantry and a platoon of engineers, drove up the valley to Mundung-ni. It caught the Chinese 204th Division, of the 68th Army, in the open as it came in to relieve the North Koreans, and cut its supply routes. The same day G Company of the 2nd Battalion, 23rd, took Hill 520. Gugeler's account says it had about 20 men a platoon; it took three hours and over 30 casualties, mostly light grenade wounds, and flame throwers were brought up to clear the bunkers. The 1st Battalion and the French then worked north from bunker to bunker, and at daybreak on 13 October the French took Hill 851. The 38th Infantry took its last objective, Hill 1220, on 15 October.

### Where the accounts disagree
- **The American loss.** Hermes, as transcribed online, gives the 2nd Division over 3,800 casualties from 13 September to 15 October, almost half of them in the 23rd Infantry and the French. Wikipedia, citing Hermes, says over 3,700.
- **The enemy loss.** The 2nd Division estimated about 25,000 North Korean and Chinese casualties, about half in Touchdown. Wikipedia calls this a US claim. No North Korean or Chinese figure was found for this page.
- **The French loss.** The French ministry's *Chemins de mémoire* gives 60 killed and nearly 260 wounded; Wikipedia's article on the battalion, 60 and 200.
- **When it began.** Hermes and Wikipedia start the battle on 13 September. *Chemins de mémoire* says the first attacks were on 15 September and the French were engaged from the 26th.
- **The mortar rounds.** Hermes, as transcribed online, gives over 19,000 mortar rounds; Wikipedia gives over 119,000. The online text may have lost a figure.
- **Bloody Ridge.** Hermes gives over 2,800 UN and ROK casualties there; Wikipedia, over 2,700.
- **The defenders.** Wikipedia names Pang Ho San among the North Korean commanders without saying his post. Hermes names Hong Nim as commander of the 6th Division.

!!! story "Company C on 931"
    Private First Class Herbert K. Pililaau was a Native Hawaiian from Waianae, Oahu, the ninth of
    14 children and a singer and ukulele player. On 17 September 1951 his platoon of C Company,
    23rd Infantry, was holding a position on the slope of Hill 931 when it was ordered to pull
    back. He stayed behind with his squad leader to cover it. He fired his Browning Automatic Rifle
    until it was empty, threw his grenades, and then fought with a trench knife and his fists until
    he was killed. When the position was retaken, more than 40 enemy dead lay round it. He was the
    first Hawaiian to receive the Medal of Honor.
