### The campaign
In the second half of 1967 the North Vietnamese fought a string of actions close to South Vietnam's borders: at Con Thien, Song Be, Loc Ninh, Dak To and later Khe Sanh. Wikipedia, after the American official histories, calls these the border battles, and says their aim was to draw American and South Vietnamese troops away from the cities before the Tet offensive. Round Dak To, in Kontum province, the 4th Infantry Division under Major General William Peers, the 173rd Airborne Brigade and South Vietnamese units had fought through the summer in Operation Greeley.

In October American intelligence saw North Vietnamese regiments moving north from Pleiku into Kontum, and gathered as the PAVN 1st Division. On 3 November a North Vietnamese sergeant, Vu Hong, gave himself up and described the plan. The 66th Regiment was to make the main attack south-west of Dak To, the 32nd to block to the south, the independent 24th to the north-east, and the 174th to be held in the north-west, with the 40th Artillery Regiment in support. A captured B3 Front document said the aim was to destroy a major American unit. American and South Vietnamese reinforcements came in under Operation MacArthur.

Through early November the Americans fought for one fortified hill after another: Hill 823, Hill 724, Hill 1338 and others. On 15 November mortars hit the ammunition and fuel dumps at the Dak To airstrip, and more than 1,100 tons of ammunition were destroyed. After that the North Vietnamese began to draw off to the south-west. The 174th Regiment, still fresh, moved west past Ben Het to cover the withdrawal of the 66th and 32nd. When a Mobile Strike Force company met strong resistance near Hill 875, Brigadier General Leo Schweiter of the 173rd ordered the 2nd Battalion, 503rd Infantry to take it.

### The places
- **Dak To base and airstrip**: the brigades' base; its ammunition dump blew up on 15 November.
- **Ben Het**: the Special Forces camp near the border, with Fire Support Base 12 close by.
- **Fire Support Base 16**: from which the 4th Battalion, 503rd Infantry was sent to relieve the 2nd.
- **Hill 823**: where B Company, 4th Battalion, 503rd Infantry, about 120 men, was landed alone and held.
- **Hills 724 and 1338**: two of the hills where the 4th Infantry Division fought earlier in November.
- **Hill 875**: a jungle hill well west of Dak To, its crest ringed with bunkers and trenches, with tunnels by which the defenders later got away.
- **The north slope**: the way the 2nd Battalion went up.
- **The new landing zone**: a clearing for one helicopter, cut about two-thirds of the way up the hill on the 21st (Lewis).

### The leaders
- **William R. Peers** (United States, 4th Infantry Division): commanded the American forces round Dak To.
- **Leo H. Schweiter** (United States, 173rd Airborne Brigade): ordered the attack on Hill 875.
- **James Steverson** (United States, 2nd Battalion, 503rd Infantry): directed the battalion from a command helicopter.
- **Harold Kaufman** (United States, captain, C Company): commanded the battalion's companies on the hill, and was killed by the bomb on the 19th.
- **Kiley** (United States, captain, A Company): killed when his company was overrun from behind.
- **Charles J. Watters** (United States, chaplain, 173rd Support Battalion): a Catholic priest, killed on the evening of the 19th.
- **Carlos Lozada** (United States, private first class, A Company): held the rear with a machine gun until he was killed.
- **James Johnson** (United States, 4th Battalion, 503rd Infantry): sent his battalion to the relief.
- **Ron Leonard** (United States, captain, B Company, 4th Battalion): led the first of the relief onto the hill.
- **174th Regiment** (North Vietnamese, the defenders): no source read for this page names its commander. Wikipedia names Hoàng Minh Thảo as the North Vietnamese commander for the whole battle.

### The battle
Times are local, South Vietnam time (GMT+8); Hanoi kept GMT+7. The 2nd Battalion spent the night of 18 November at the foot of the hill. On the morning of the 19th three companies, about 330 men by Wikipedia and about 290 by Scott, moved to their start line at 09:43. C and D Companies led up the north slope, with two platoons of A behind. At 10:30, within 300 m of the crest, machine guns, B-40 rockets and recoilless rifles opened on them from bunkers. D Company's point man was shot, and an aidman was killed. The attacks on the bunkers stalled, and Kaufman pulled the companies into a perimeter on the slope.

At about 14:30 (Scott says about 14:00) a North Vietnamese force waiting at the foot of the hill, by Wikipedia the 2nd Battalion of the 174th, struck A Company from the west along a trail. It overran A Company's command post; Kiley was among the dead. Lozada held the rear with his M60 and was killed; he received the Medal of Honor after his death. The battalion was now surrounded, with the enemy above and below. Six Hueys trying to bring in supplies that afternoon were shot down or badly damaged.

At 18:58, as Kaufman met his officers at dusk under a lone tree, an American aircraft dropped two bombs. One fell outside. The other burst in the trees over the command group, among the wounded and the medics gathered there. It killed 42 men and wounded 45. Kaufman was among the dead, and so was Watters, who had spent the afternoon going out under fire to bring in wounded. Lieutenants and sergeants took over what was left.

The wounded could not be got out. Scott says the medics ran out of dressings and used towels and T-shirts; one Huey hovered long enough to lift out five of the worst hurt, and another was driven off. On the 20th the 4th Battalion came up through sniper and mortar fire, and only reached the perimeter at nightfall. On the 21st a one-helicopter landing zone was cut on the slope, and the wounded began to go out, the 173rd lifting out its wounded from this fight for the first time; the helicopter pilot Lewis says Dustoff helicopters got in on the 22nd. The two battalions attacked on the afternoon of the 21st and reached the trenches but were called back at dark. On the 22nd the hilltop was bombed and shelled bare. At about 11:00 on 23 November they went up again and took the crest in about half an hour. The North Vietnamese had gone, leaving some bodies and weapons.

### Where the accounts disagree
- **The bomb.** Wikipedia, after Murphy, gives a Marine A-4 Skyhawk flown by Lieutenant Colonel Richard Taber, two 250 lb Snakeye bombs, at 18:58. Scott, after the after-action reports, says a propeller-driven aircraft and two 500 lb bombs. Lewis, a helicopter pilot at Dak To, says an F-4 and two 500-pounders. An anonymous 4th Battalion account says an Air Force fighter at about 20:00. The game keeps 18:58.
- **How many went up.** Wikipedia gives 330 men in three companies; Scott about 290; *Columbia* magazine about 300.
- **Who held the hill.** The 2nd Battalion went up expecting one company, says the Virtual Wall, and met two regiments. *Columbia* says a regiment of about 2,000. Wikipedia and Scott name the 174th Regiment.
- **The American losses.** Wikipedia gives the 2nd Battalion 87 killed, 130 wounded and 3 missing, and the 4th 28 killed, 123 wounded and 4 missing. An unofficial count given by the Virtual Wall and the anonymous account has 158 killed, 33 missing and 411 wounded evacuated for the two battalions. The Virtual Wall lists 125 men who died in connection with the battle.
- **The North Vietnamese losses.** Scott says about 22 bodies were found on the crest and the 174th lost more than half its strength. A Vietnamese account in *Sài Gòn Giải Phóng* (2021) says about 200 of the regiment died round Dak To and Tan Canh, and claims more than 4,000 Americans and South Vietnamese put out of action and 13 aircraft shot down. For the whole Dak To battle the US Army claimed 1,644 killed by body count; a PAVN report gives about 450 killed and 1,089 wounded.
- **The attack from behind.** Wikipedia gives 14:30; Scott about 14:00.
- **The relief.** Scott, in a hard-to-read scan, puts the first of the 4th Battalion on the hill at 17:30; the anonymous account gives about 16:00 for B Company and 20:00 to 20:30 for the others, on the 20th.
- **Who led.** Scott faults both battalion commanders for directing the fight from helicopters rather than on the ground.

!!! story "The chaplain"
    Charles Watters, a Catholic priest from New Jersey, was 40. He had made the only mass combat
    jump of the war with the 2nd Battalion, 503rd Infantry, in February 1967, and had stayed on in
    Vietnam beyond his year. On the morning of 19 November he said Mass for the battalion before it
    went up Hill 875. Through the afternoon, unarmed, he went out of the perimeter under fire to
    carry in wounded men and give the last rites to the dying; his citation says he went out three
    times. At dusk he was among the wounded in the middle of the perimeter, helping the medics, when
    the bomb burst overhead. He was killed. In 1969 his brothers received his Medal of Honor, the
    first given to an Army chaplain since the Civil War.
