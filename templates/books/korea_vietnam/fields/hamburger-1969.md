### The campaign
The A Shau valley runs about 45 km along the Laotian border in south-western Thua Thien province. The North Vietnamese had held it since they overran the Special Forces camp there in 1966. It held their Base Area 611 and carried a road from the Ho Chi Minh Trail towards Hue and the coast. In early 1969 the 9th Marines' Operation Dewey Canyon found North Vietnamese roads and supply dumps. By March American intelligence saw the North Vietnamese rebuilding.

Lieutenant General Richard G. Stilwell of XXIV Corps ordered a raid into the valley, not an attempt to hold it. This was Operation Apache Snow. Major General Melvin Zais's 101st Airborne Division (Airmobile) was to destroy the North Vietnamese 29th Regiment. Colonel Joseph B. Conmy Jr.'s 3rd Brigade had three battalions: the 2nd Battalion, 501st Infantry, the 1st Battalion, 506th Infantry and the 3rd Battalion, 187th Infantry. Two battalions of the ARVN 1st Division were under its command. The plan was a reconnaissance in force, and intelligence was poor: the North Vietnamese moved by night and kept off the radio.

On 10 May, after a 74-minute bombardment of about 30 possible landing zones, the first Hueys landed at 07:10 (Willbanks). Lieutenant Colonel Weldon F. Honeycutt's 3rd Battalion, 187th Infantry landed about 2,000 m north-west of Dong Ap Bia, Hill 937, and set off through the jungle to clear it. The hill was held by the 29th Regiment, which the North Vietnamese called the 3rd Regiment of the 324th Division. Its 7th and 8th Battalions were dug in on the mountain in bunkers, trenches and tunnels.

### The places
- **The A Shau valley**: a floor of elephant grass between mountains under double and triple canopy jungle, a mile or two from Laos.
- **Dong Ap Bia (Hill 937)**: a solitary massif about 1.9 km from the border, 937 m high. The Montagnards called it "the mountain of the crouching beast".
- **The landing zone**: about 2,000 m north-west of the summit, where the 3rd Battalion, 187th Infantry came in.
- **Hill 916**: a peak on a ridge running south from the summit. Traas puts it about 1,000 m south-west; bunkers there stopped the 1st Battalion, 506th Infantry on 16 May.
- **Hill 900**: the end of the spur running south-east, where the 1st Battalion, 506th Infantry was pinned on 18 May.
- **Firebase Airborne**: the guns on a ridge about 7 km north-east. Two North Vietnamese battalions attacked it before dawn on 13 May.
- **Laos**: across the border to the west, where the 29th Regiment's reinforcements came from and its survivors went.
- **The game's field**: the summit and its upper slopes, with the north-west spur, the south spur and the eastern slope; the landing zones and Hills 900 and 916 lie off the board.

### The leaders
- **Melvin Zais** (United States, 101st Airborne Division): thought of stopping the attack on 18 May, then reinforced it.
- **Richard G. Stilwell** (United States, XXIV Corps) and **Creighton W. Abrams** (United States, MACV): backed Zais in going on. Afterwards Abrams had to take more care of American lives.
- **Joseph B. Conmy Jr.** (United States, 3rd Brigade): sent the 1st Battalion, 506th Infantry north on 13 May and ordered the two-battalion attack of 18 May.
- **Weldon F. Honeycutt** (United States, 3rd Battalion, 187th Infantry): wounded on 15 May (Traas), he would not be evacuated. On 19 May he asked to keep his battalion in the fight, and Zais let him.
- **Robert J. Bower** (United States, 1st Battalion, 506th Infantry): fought up from the south-west for four days. Wikipedia calls him John Bowers.
- **Robert L. German** (United States, 2nd Battalion, 501st Infantry): landed north-east of the hill on 19 May.
- **Ngô Quang Trưởng** (South Vietnamese, 1st Division): offered his 2nd Battalion, 3rd Regiment for the last attack.
- **Ma Vĩnh Lan** (North Vietnamese, 29th Regiment): its commander by Wikipedia, with Kiều Tam Nguyên as political commissar. No other source read for this page names them.
- **Hồ Thị Đơm** (North Vietnamese, local forces): a Pa Cô woman who, the Vietnamese press says, commanded a local company on Đồi A Bia. It gives no date.

### The battle
On 11 May B Company met camouflaged bunkers; Traas gives 3 killed and 19 wounded. A second assault cleared the slope by 16:45, and helicopters took out the wounded that evening. Then a gunship fired on the battalion command post. It was the first of five times American aircraft hit the battalion. On 14 May three companies attacked. Willbanks counts a dozen killed and more than 80 wounded. On 15 May A and B Companies got within about 150 m of the summit, and a Cobra put rockets into B Company's command group. On 15 or 16 May the Associated Press reporter Jay Sharbutt reached the battalion. His stories gave the mountain its name in print.

On 18 May fighter-bombers struck from 08:00, then the artillery fired for an hour. The 3rd Battalion, 187th Infantry attacked from the north and the 1st Battalion, 506th Infantry from the south. D Company came within about 75 m of the top, fighting at a few metres, and lost all its officers. Then a rainstorm turned the bare slope to mud and the attack came back down. By the next day A and B Companies were at half strength, and C and D had lost 80%. Zais thought of calling off the attack. Instead, backed by Stilwell and Abrams, he brought in the 2nd Battalion, 501st Infantry and the ARVN 2nd Battalion, 3rd Regiment. He meant to relieve Honeycutt's battalion, but Honeycutt asked for one more company and got A Company of the 2nd Battalion, 506th Infantry. On 19 May helicopters put the new battalions down about 1,200 m north-east and south-east of the hill. By dark the 1st Battalion, 506th Infantry was within 200 m of the summit.

No account read says which clock the times on 20 May keep; South Vietnam kept UTC+8 and the North UTC+7. Air strikes and artillery pounded the summit for about two hours. Traas has the 3rd Battalion, 187th Infantry move off at 11:00. Mira says it met no fire until the second line of bunkers, about 75 m from the top, where rifles and rocket-propelled grenades opened on it. Specialist 4 Johnny Jackson went forward alone with his M60, and A Company followed him over the last 20 m. Traas says A Company was on the summit about 55 minutes after the start. Most of the North Vietnamese had gone. Willbanks says about two platoons, apparently ordered to stay, fought to the end. The others went down the western slopes towards Laos. The hill was secure by 17:00.

{{picture:hamburger-1969-wounded}}

The wounded went out by helicopter. Where no helicopter could land, the 326th Medical Battalion's Eagle Dustoff crews lowered litters on hoists through the trees. Galloway puts about half the American losses down to fragments and 30% to bullets. Friendly fire killed 5 and wounded 53. On 21 May the 3rd Battalion, 187th Infantry flew out to rest at Eagle Beach, on the coast. Traas gives the cost as 70 Americans killed and 372 wounded, against 633 North Vietnamese dead counted.

The fight was news before it ended. On 20 May Senator Edward Kennedy called the attack "senseless and irresponsible" on the floor of the Senate; Senators George McGovern and Stephen Young joined him. Zais answered that the hill mattered only because the enemy was on it. On 5 June the order came to leave Dong Ap Bia, and about two weeks later North Vietnamese troops were reported back on it. Nixon told Abrams to fight with fewer American losses. On 8 June, at Midway, he announced the first withdrawal of 25,000 men.

### Where the accounts disagree
- **The clock.** No account read for this page says which clock its times keep. South Vietnam kept UTC+8 in 1969, North Vietnam UTC+7 (Wikipedia). The game uses the times as given.
- **When the last attack went in.** Traas has the 3rd Battalion, 187th Infantry move off at 11:00 and reach the top 55 minutes later. Wikipedia, after the 101st's report, also starts it at 11:00, with the crest reached by noon. Willbanks starts it at 10:00 and has the summit at 11:45. All agree it was secure by 17:00.
- **Who was first on top.** Traas, Willbanks and Mira put A Company of the 3rd Battalion, 187th Infantry first. Wiest, as Wikipedia gives him, says the ARVN 2nd Battalion, 3rd Regiment found a lightly held slope and reached the crest about 10:00, but was ordered off because the American artillery was about to fire on the summit.
- **The American losses.** Traas and Willbanks give 70 killed and 372 wounded. Wikipedia, after the 101st's report, gives 56 killed, 367 wounded and 7 missing; History.com 72 killed; Galloway about 66 killed. The 3rd Battalion, 187th Infantry lost 39 killed and 290 wounded (Willbanks); Mira gives it 329 killed and wounded together.
- **The North Vietnamese losses.** The American count was 633 bodies (Traas, Mira) or 630 (Wikipedia). Willbanks says a Special Forces patrol in Laos reported about 1,100 dead and wounded carried out; Galloway says trail-watchers counted over 1,200. The Vietnamese newspaper *Sài Gòn Giải Phóng* claims about 1,500 Americans killed; Wikipedia gives the PAVN claim as 1,500 killed and wounded.
- **The defenders.** Wikipedia gives about 800 men in two battalions. Mira estimates the 29th Regiment at 1,200 to 1,800. *Sài Gòn Giải Phóng* names the defenders as the 3rd Regiment of the 324th Division, with local forces and militia, and says the Americans and South Vietnamese brought 13 battalions.
- **The fire on the command post.** Traas says the gunship killed 1 and wounded 35 on 11 May; Willbanks 2 killed and 35 wounded; the HistoryNet timeline 2 killed and 25 wounded. In all, Wikipedia says, friendly fire killed 7 and wounded 53; Galloway says 5 and 53.
- **The bombardment.** Willbanks and the HistoryNet timeline put over 20,000 shells, 272 air strikes, over 1 million pounds of bombs and 152,000 pounds of napalm before the attack of 20 May. Wikipedia gives the 20,000 rounds and 272 missions for the whole battle.
- **Hill 916.** Wikipedia has the 1st Battalion, 506th Infantry take it on 15 May, Willbanks on the 16th; Traas has the battalion fired on from it on the 16th.
- **Was it worth it?** Zais said the hill had no military value in itself; the enemy was there. Honeycutt argued that it overlooked a main North Vietnamese supply and staging area.

!!! story "Dustoff 927"
    On 13 May wounded men of D Company, 3rd Battalion, 187th Infantry were pinned near a river at
    the foot of Hill 937. Dustoff 927, a Huey of the 326th Medical Battalion, came in over the trees
    and let down its hoist. First Lieutenant Gerald Torba was flying. The worst hurt, Private First
    Class George Pickel, was halfway up in a litter basket when a rocket-propelled grenade hit
    the rotor. The helicopter fell onto the men below and caught fire. The co-pilot, First
    Lieutenant Jerry Lee, the crew chief, Specialist 4 James Walters, and the medic, Specialist 4
    James Margro, were killed, and so was Pickel. Torba was left on the hill and found about 17
    hours later. He lost his left leg below the knee, stayed in the Army, and retired a
    lieutenant colonel in 1992.
