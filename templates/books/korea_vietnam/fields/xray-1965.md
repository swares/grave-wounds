### The campaign
By 1965 the Viet Cong held much of the South Vietnamese countryside, with North Vietnamese regulars of the People's Army of Vietnam (PAVN) behind them. General Westmoreland had won promises of about 300,000 American troops, and the build-up came that summer. Among the first to arrive was the 1st Cavalry Division (Airmobile), formed from the 11th Air Assault Division (Test) to try out war by helicopter. It set up its base at An Khe.

In the Central Highlands the PAVN's B3 Front, under Major General Chu Huy Mân, had the 32nd, 33rd and 66th Regiments. On 19 October 1965 the 33rd attacked the Special Forces camp at Plei Me while the 32nd lay in wait for the relief column. The camp held with air support. A South Vietnamese column was ambushed on the evening of 23 October, fought its way on, and reached the camp at dusk on the 25th. The North Vietnamese fell back west, towards the Chu Pong massif and Cambodia.

The 1st Cavalry Division went after them. On 13 November Colonel Thomas Brown of the 3rd Brigade ordered Lieutenant Colonel Harold Moore to make an air assault at the foot of the Chu Pong the next morning. Moore's 1st Battalion, 7th Cavalry should have had about 750 to 765 men; it had about 450. Sixteen Hueys would carry it in from Plei Me, about a company at a time, on a round trip that Galloway puts at 35 minutes. Waiting on the massif, though the Americans did not know it, were the 7th and 9th Battalions of the 66th Regiment.

### The places
- **Landing zone X-Ray**: a clearing about 100 m across that could take about eight helicopters at once.
- **The dry creek bed**: beside the clearing, where A Company met the North Vietnamese coming down.
- **The termite hill**: the bare termite mound on which Moore set his command post.
- **The knoll**: a rise about 100 m beyond B Company's line, where its 2nd Platoon was cut off.
- **The Chu Pong massif**: the high ground to the west, from which the North Vietnamese came down.
- **Landing zone Falcon**: the guns that fired for X-Ray; Crandall's pilots shifted their supply runs there to shorten them.
- **Plei Me**: the Special Forces camp besieged in October, and the pick-up point for the lift.
- **Landing zone Victor**: where the 2nd Battalion, 5th Cavalry landed on the 15th before marching to X-Ray.
- **Landing zone Albany**: about 4 km north-north-east, where the 2nd Battalion, 7th Cavalry was ambushed after leaving X-Ray.

### The leaders
- **Harold G. Moore** (United States, 1st Battalion, 7th Cavalry): refused to leave his battalion when ordered to Saigon to brief Westmoreland.
- **John Herren** (United States, B Company): led the first company in.
- **Henry Herrick** (United States, 2nd Platoon, B Company): followed North Vietnamese into the scrub, was cut off, and was killed.
- **Clyde E. "Ernie" Savage** (United States, sergeant, 2nd Platoon): a squad leader who took command of the cut-off platoon.
- **Ramon "Tony" Nadal** (United States, A Company): fought along the creek bed.
- **Robert Edwards** (United States, C Company): held the south side; badly wounded on the 15th.
- **Bruce Crandall** (United States, major, A Company, 229th Assault Helicopter Battalion): led the lift, call sign "Ancient Serpent 6".
- **Myron Diduryk** (United States, B Company, 2nd Battalion, 7th Cavalry): flew in at dusk.
- **Chu Huy Mân** (North Vietnamese, B3 Front): commanded the PAVN in the highlands.
- **Nguyễn Hữu An** (North Vietnamese, B3 Front forward command post): commanded the front's forward post and wrote a memoir of the campaign (2005).

### The battle
Times here are local, South Vietnam time (GMT+8); no source read says which clock the Americans used, but their accounts read as local time. Hanoi kept GMT+7. After about 30 minutes of artillery, rockets and air strikes, the first of B Company landed at 10:48 on 14 November. The second lift, the rest of B and a platoon of A, came at 11:20. At about noon a squad caught an unarmed North Vietnamese deserter, who said there were three battalions on the mountain. At about 12:15 the first shots hit B Company's patrols beyond the creek bed.

At about 13:00 Herrick's 2nd Platoon followed North Vietnamese into the scrub and was cut off about 100 m from the rest of the company. Herrick was killed after passing command to Sergeant Carl Palmer, who was killed in turn. Savage took over because he was beside the radio, and he brought artillery down round the platoon. C Company landed at 13:32 and took the south and south-west. A Company's platoons met North Vietnamese coming down the creek bed. At 14:30 the last of C Company and the lead of D Company landed under fire, as 175 to 200 North Vietnamese charged C Company; that attack was over by 15:00. The rest of the battalion was in by 15:20.

At 15:45 Moore sent A and B Companies to bring in their wounded and reach the cut-off platoon. They were stopped by a machine gun on a termite hill. Lieutenant Walter Marm knocked it out with a light anti-tank rocket, grenades and his rifle, and was wounded; he later received the Medal of Honor. The push got no further. B Company of the 2nd Battalion, 7th Cavalry, under Diduryk, began landing at about 17:00 and was in by 18:00. In the night the North Vietnamese probed the perimeter in squads, and attacked the cut-off platoon three times, before midnight, at 03:15 and at 04:30.

The wounded went out on the helicopters that brought the men in. The medical evacuation ships came in on the first afternoon and pulled out under fire with only two casualties. Freeman later said that their rules let them land only at a landing zone that had been quiet for five minutes. The lift Hueys carried the wounded out instead, Crandall and Freeman flying in ammunition and water and taking out about 70 wounded that day. Captain Tom Metsker was hit and killed while helping the wounded D Company commander, Captain Ray Lefebvre, onto a Huey.

At about 06:50 on the 15th some 200 North Vietnamese attacked C Company's side. Soon after 07:55, when the perimeter was marked with coloured smoke, an American F-100 dropped napalm close to the command post. Galloway saw two engineers badly burned, and helped carry Private First Class Jimmy Nakayama, burned by it, to the aid station; Nakayama died two days later. A Company of the 2nd Battalion, 7th Cavalry came in at 09:10, and the attacks died down at about 10:00. The 2nd Battalion, 5th Cavalry marched in from Victor at 12:05, and that afternoon the cut-off platoon was reached. Before dawn on the 16th Diduryk's company beat off four attacks. That morning Moore's battalion pulled out to Pleiku.

### Where the accounts disagree
- **The losses at X-Ray.** Wikipedia, after Carland, gives 79 Americans killed and 121 wounded, as does Galloway in *Stars and Stripes* (79 killed, more than 120 wounded). Wikipedia gives Galloway elsewhere as 80 killed and 124 wounded. Seabrooke gives 70 killed.
- **The North Vietnamese dead.** Moore's count was 634 bodies, which he revised down from 834, with 1,215 more estimated killed by artillery and air; he later said his count was too high. The PAVN's Central Highlands Front report of 1966, as Wikipedia gives it, admits 559 killed and 669 wounded across five engagements of the campaign.
- **The whole campaign.** Hanyok, for the National Security Agency, gives about 3,500 North Vietnamese and about 300 Americans killed. Galloway, on army.mil, gives 234 Americans killed and over 1,200 North Vietnamese for X-Ray and Albany together. The official PAVN history claims 1,700 Americans put out of action.
- **The cut-off platoon.** Wikipedia gives 29 men, of whom 9 were killed and 13 wounded. Savage's citation says it held for 26 hours, which would put the relief at about 15:30 on the 15th, as Wikipedia does.
- **The enemy.** The deserter spoke of three battalions on the massif; Freeman remembered three regiments. Seabrooke names the 32nd, 33rd and 66th Regiments; Wikipedia, after Carland, the 9th Battalion of the 66th close to the landing zone.
- **The wounded flown out.** Crandall's Medal of Honor citation gives 22 flights but no count of wounded. Military Times and Seabrooke say about 70 or more than 70 for Crandall and Freeman together; HistoryLink says more than 75; Freeman's citation credits him with about 30.
- **When the shooting began.** Wikipedia has the first shots at about 12:15; Seabrooke, after Freeman, has the shooting begin about 90 minutes after the landing.

!!! story "Ancient Serpent 6"
    Major Bruce Crandall led the 16 Hueys that put Moore's battalion into X-Ray. When the landing
    zone came under fire, the medical evacuation helicopters would not go in, and Captain Tony Nadal
    radioed that he had 12 wounded and nobody to take them. Crandall took out his door gunner and
    weapons to save weight and went in unarmed. Captain Ed Freeman, his wingman, was the one
    volunteer to go with him. Crandall flew 22 flights that day, Freeman 14, bringing in ammunition and
    water and taking out the wounded. Freeman later reckoned his helicopter took more than 50 rounds,
    and that he flew for fourteen and a half hours. Both received the Distinguished Service Cross,
    later raised to the Medal of Honor: Freeman in 2001, Crandall in 2007.
