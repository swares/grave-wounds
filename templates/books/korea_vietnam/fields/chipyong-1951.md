### The campaign
The Chinese People's Volunteers entered the war in November 1950 and drove the United Nations armies out of North Korea. On 23 December the Eighth Army's commander, General Walton Walker, was killed in a road accident, and Lieutenant General Matthew B. Ridgway took command on the 26th. The Chinese opened their third phase offensive on the evening of 31 December. Seoul was given up on 3 January 1951, and by about 7 January Peng Dehuai, the Chinese commander, had halted his worn-out troops.

Ridgway then went back onto the attack with Operations Thunderbolt and Roundup. Colonel Paul L. Freeman's 23rd Regimental Combat Team, of the 2nd Infantry Division, beat off a Chinese attack at the Twin Tunnels at the start of February and reached Chipyong-ni on 3 February. On the night of 11 February the Chinese opened their fourth phase offensive. The XIII Army Group struck X Corps at Hoengsong; the ROK 8th Division was broken, with 323 officers and 7,142 men dead or missing. X Corps fell back to a line anchored on Chipyong-ni and Wonju, and the 23rd was left out on a limb.

On 13 February Lieutenant General Edward Almond, of X Corps, decided to pull the 23rd back about 15 miles to Yoju. After meeting MacArthur at Suwon that noon, Ridgway judged that giving up Chipyong-ni would open the Han valley, and ordered it held. The roads behind the regiment were already being cut.

### The places
- **Chipyong-ni**: a crossroads town on Route 24 and a single-track railway, about 40 miles east of Seoul.
- **The perimeter**: a ring about a mile across on the low ground round the town. Freeman left the higher hills outside it, which would have needed a line about 12 miles long.
- **Hill 397 (Mangmi-san)**: the height a mile south of the town that overlooked it, from which the Chinese came at G Company.
- **G Company's hill**: the southern edge of the perimeter, held by the 2nd Battalion, where the Chinese broke in on 15 February.
- **Pongmi-san and the other hills**: Pongmi-san to the north-east, and Hills 248, 345, 348, 506 and 319, all outside the perimeter.
- **Route 24A and Koksu-ri**: the road south to Yoju, blocked at Koksu-ri on 12 February, by which Task Force Crombez came.
- **The road cut**: a deep cutting on the western slopes of Hill 397, a mile below the town, where the relief column was hit.

### The leaders
- **Paul L. Freeman** (UN, 23rd Infantry): chose the tight perimeter. Wounded in the leg by a mortar fragment on the first night, he refused to be taken out and kept command until the morning of 15 February.
- **Raoul Magrin-Vernerey, "Monclar"** (UN, French Battalion): 58, a former général de corps d'armée who took the rank of lieutenant-colonel to lead the battalion, which held the west side.
- **James W. Edwards** (UN, 2nd Battalion, 23rd Infantry): held the south and led the counter-attacks for G Company's hill.
- **John H. Chiles** (UN, X Corps G-3): flown in on 14 February to take over; he took command when Freeman went out.
- **Matthew B. Ridgway** (UN, Eighth Army): overruled Almond and ordered the town held.
- **Marcel G. Crombez** (UN, 5th Cavalry): sent his tanks ahead when the infantry could not get through in time.
- **Edgar J. Treacy** (UN, 3rd Battalion, 5th Cavalry): rode with the column, was left behind at Koksu-ri and taken prisoner; he died in captivity.
- **Xu Guofu** (Chinese, 119th Division): the field commander at Chipyong-ni, whose memoirs give the Chinese account.
- **Wen Yucheng** (Chinese, 40th Army): ordered the withdrawal when it was learned that the garrison was much larger than thought.

### The battle
The sources do not say which clock they keep; the times here are taken as local. Freeman had about 4,500 men: the 23rd Infantry, the French Battalion, the 1st Ranger Company, the 37th Field Artillery Battalion, a battery of 155 mm howitzers, anti-aircraft guns of the 82nd AAA Battalion and B Company of the 2nd Engineers. The 1st Battalion held the north, the 3rd the east, the 2nd the south and the French the west. B Company and the Rangers were the reserve.

At about 22:00 on 13 February machine guns and mortars opened on C Company, astride Route 24. At about 23:30 the Chinese attacked C Company, then G Company in the south, the French and the 3rd Battalion. They got into G Company's line once, but F Company and the tank company threw them out. Attacks at 06:30 on the 3rd Battalion and an hour later on the French failed, and the Chinese drew back into the hills. About 100 of Freeman's men had been hit.

On 14 February wind and mortar fire stopped the helicopters taking out the wounded. From about 15:00 two dozen C-119s dropped supplies for three hours. Near midnight a barrage of about an hour fell on the command post. A heavy assault from Hill 397 struck G Company, then A and C Companies in the north and I and K in the east. I Company's line was broken once and restored. In the south the pressure did not stop, and at about 03:00 on the 15th G Company gave up the rest of its hill. The gunners of B Battery, 503rd, fell back with it, leaving their 155 mm howitzers between the lines.

At about 04:00 Edwards sent in a platoon of F Company and a platoon of Rangers with G Company's survivors. They took back part of the hill and were driven off. At 10:00 B Company attacked alone behind mortar, tank and anti-aircraft fire and was kept off the crest. From noon fighter-bombers struck the Chinese on the southern slopes. At about 16:30 B Company took the rest of the hill.

Crombez had come up from Yoju with the 5th Cavalry, but saw that his infantry could not reach the town by dark. At 15:45 he left them and drove on with 23 tanks, of D Company, 6th Tank Battalion, and a platoon of A Company, 70th Tank Battalion, with about 160 men of L Company, 5th Cavalry, riding on the decks. At the halts near Koksu-ri the tanks moved off before all the riders were back on, and about 90 were left behind. In the road cut the Chinese fired rockets and threw satchel charges; a rocket hit Captain Johnnie M. Hiers's tank and killed him and the turret crew, but the burned driver took it through. Crombez entered the perimeter at about 17:15, and the Chinese on Hill 397 ran. Of L Company, 23 men were left with the column, 13 of them wounded.

The wounded of the 23rd lay inside the perimeter, with a platoon of the 2nd Medical Battalion, until the road opened. On 16 February fog and snow grounded the helicopters again; a supply train of 28 lorries and 19 ambulances came up from the south, and the wounded went out to the 5th Cavalry's clearing station at Koksu-ri before dark.

### Where the accounts disagree
- **The garrison.** Mossman and Gugeler give about 4,500 men, of them 2,500 front-line infantry. Wikipedia, after Xu, says the Chinese expected about 1,000 and found over 6,000. The military.com account puts the French at about 800, 180 of them South Koreans.
- **The Chinese strength.** Wikipedia gives a US estimate of 25,000 and counts eight regiments, about 8,000 men, in the area. Mossman's prisoners named six regiments in the attacks, from the 115th, 116th, 119th, 120th and 126th Divisions of the 39th, 40th and 42nd Armies. Xu says only three regiments, the 356th, 357th and 359th, about 3,000 men, attacked; two more, the 343rd and 376th, lost their way.
- **The UN losses.** Mossman gives 52 killed, 259 wounded and 42 missing. Wikipedia gives 51 killed and 250 wounded. A 2009 Army article adds 51 non-battle casualties.
- **The Chinese losses.** Mossman has the 23rd count and estimate 4,946 Chinese casualties, and Crombez over 500 more. Wikipedia, after US sources, gives about 2,000 killed and 3,000 wounded. Xu admits "a little over 900" casualties, a third of them dead. The 2009 Army article claims over 7,000 counted dead.
- **The counter-attacks.** Mossman has Edwards's composite force attack at about 04:00 and B Company at 10:00. Wikipedia, after Gugeler, has the Rangers, an F Company platoon and 14 men of G Company driven back at 06:15, and B Company attacking at noon.
- **Crombez's arrival.** Mossman says about 17:15, Gugeler 17:00, Wikipedia 17:25 and military.com 17:10. Military.com has the column leave at 15:00, the others at 15:45. Wikipedia says nearly 20 tanks arrived; the captured Chinese XIX Army Group critique, as Ryan gives it, says more than 20, at about 16:00.
- **L Company's loss.** Mossman and Gugeler give 12 killed, about 40 wounded and 19 missing. Military.com says more than 130 riders were killed, wounded, captured or stranded.
- **Hill 397.** Mossman puts it south of the town; Wikipedia says north. The accounts of the fighting agree with Mossman.

!!! story "Sergeant Sitman's grenade"
    Sergeant First Class William S. Sitman, of Pennsylvania, led a machine-gun section of M
    Company, 23rd Infantry, attached to I Company on the eastern side of the perimeter. On the
    night of 14 February the Chinese came at his position. A grenade put his own gun out of action,
    and he stayed on to protect the crew of a light machine gun that had moved in. When a grenade
    landed among them, his Medal of Honor citation says, he threw himself on it, "absorbing the
    full force of the explosion with his body." He died of his wounds. Five men lived. He was 27.
