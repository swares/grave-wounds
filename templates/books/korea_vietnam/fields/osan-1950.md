### The campaign
Korea had been divided at the 38th parallel since 1945. At 04:00 on 25 June 1950 the North Korean People's Army attacked across it from coast to coast. Half the force went down the Uijongbu corridor towards Seoul. The Center of Military History puts the assault force at about 90,000 men: seven infantry divisions, an armoured brigade and smaller units, with about 150 Soviet T-34 tanks. The South Korean army had 95,000 men and no tanks. By the end of June it could account for only 22,000.

Seoul fell on 28 June to the North Korean 3rd and 4th Divisions; the Han River bridges were blown early, cutting off much of the South Korean army. The UN Security Council called for a cease-fire on 25 June and on 27 June asked its members to help. On 29 June President Truman let the Army secure a port and airfield at Pusan and agreed to send two divisions from Japan.

On 30 June MacArthur ordered the 24th Infantry Division to Korea. At 20:00 on 1 July Lieutenant Colonel Charles B. Smith, commanding the 1st Battalion, 21st Infantry, was alerted at Camp Wood, near Kumamoto. He took two understrength rifle companies, B and C, with recoilless rifles and mortars. 406 of his men flew from Itazuke to Pusan that day. Appleman says about one man in six had been in combat. They went by train to Taejon, arriving at 08:00 on 2 July, and then north to P'yongt'aek and Ansong.

On 4 July A Battery, 52nd Field Artillery Battalion, under Lieutenant Colonel Miller O. Perry, joined Smith at P'yongt'aek. Brigadier General George B. Barth told Smith to take up the positions he had chosen near Osan. The force left a little after midnight in commandeered trucks; the 12 miles took two and a half hours in blackout. At about 03:00 on 5 July the men began to dig in. It was raining.

### The places
- **Suwon**: the town to the north, down whose road the North Koreans came.
- **The ridge north of Osan**: about three miles north of the town, across the road at right angles; its highest point about 300 feet above the paddies.
- **The saddle**: the low point where the highway crossed the ridge. The memorial there takes its name, Jukmiryeong, from the pass.
- **The knob west of the highway**: held by one platoon of B Company, and taken by the North Koreans at 12:30.
- **The railroad**: on the right, bending away to the east almost a mile from the road. C Company's right was bent back along a finger ridge west of it.
- **The battery position**: about 2,000 yards behind the infantry, four howitzers 150 yards west of the highway, with a fifth halfway forward to fire on the saddle.
- **Osan**: the town behind the line, where the vehicles waited and where three tanks blocked the road south in the afternoon.
- **P'yongt'aek and Ansong**: 15 and 20 miles to the south and south-east, where the force came from and its survivors went.
- **The UN Forces First Battle Memorial**: on the ridge today, with the Jukmiryeong Peace Park and museum, opened in July 2020.

### The leaders
- **Charles B. Smith** (American, 1st Battalion, 21st Infantry): commanded the task force. He later said he stayed in the position too long.
- **Miller O. Perry** (American, 52nd Field Artillery Battalion): commanded the battery. Wounded in the right leg, he refused to be evacuated.
- **George B. Barth** (American, 24th Infantry Division): sent Smith forward from P'yongt'aek and recorded the first round at 08:16.
- **William F. Dean** (American, 24th Infantry Division): chose Smith's battalion to go first.
- **Ollie D. Connor** (American, second lieutenant): fired 22 bazooka rockets at the tanks from about 15 yards.
- **Raymond "Bodie" Adams** (American, first lieutenant): knocked out a machine gun with a grenade thrown about 40 yards during the withdrawal.
- **Lee Kwon Mu** (North Korean, 4th Division): led the infantry column, by Wikipedia's account.
- **107th Tank Regiment, 105th Armored Division** (North Korean, the tanks): no source read for this page names its commander. Wikipedia lists a Ryu Kyong-su among the North Korean commanders without saying which unit he led.

### The battle
Times here are local, as Appleman gives them. At the ridge were 540 Americans: 17 officers and 389 men of infantry, and 9 officers and 125 men of artillery. The battery had about 1,200 rounds but only 6 anti-tank (HEAT) rounds. A little after 07:00 Smith saw movement near Suwon. Eight tanks came first and then others in groups of about four, 33 in all. At 08:16 the howitzers opened fire at about 4,000 yards, without effect. The recoilless rifles held their fire until 700 yards and hit the leading tanks, which kept on. The forward howitzer, firing HEAT, stopped the two leading tanks, and one burned. A later tank destroyed that gun. By 09:00 the last tank had passed the infantry, and about 20 infantrymen had been killed or wounded.

The tanks cut the telephone wire to the guns. The radios were wet, and by 11:00 the battery had lost touch with the infantry. The tanks drove past the battery, the last by 10:15. One was stopped by a shell in its tracks. Sergeant Edwin A. Eversole's bazooka round bounced off another. In all four tanks were destroyed or stopped and three slightly damaged. Perry and one other man were the only wounded at the battery.

About an hour after the tanks, a column some six miles long came out of Suwon: three tanks, then trucks, then marching infantry. It was the 16th and 18th Regiments of the North Korean 4th Division. At about 11:45, when the trucks were about 1,000 yards off, the mortars and machine guns opened fire and set them burning. About 1,000 men got down and spread out to both flanks. At about 12:30 they took the knob west of the highway, and the B Company platoon there moved east of the road.

At about 14:30 Smith decided to go. The enemy was on both flanks with a large reserve on the road, small-arms ammunition was nearly gone, he had no communications and no help was coming. The companies leapfrogged off the ridge and down the finger ridge west of the railroad: C Company first, then the medics, battalion headquarters and B Company. B Company's 2nd Platoon never got the order. The men left the recoilless rifles, mortars and machine guns and carried only rifles with two or three clips. Most of the losses came now, as the groups crossed the open ground under fire.

About 25 to 30 men on litters had to be left behind. A medical sergeant volunteered to stay with them. The walking wounded went with the companies, but many fell behind when fire broke up the groups. Smith found the battery with all its guns still working. The gunners took out the sights and breech locks and drove off. At the south edge of Osan three tanks blocked the road, so the column turned back through the town and went east on a dirt road, with about 100 infantrymen on the trucks. It reached Ansong after dark. The North Koreans took the ridge and did not follow. Survivors counted at Ch'onan on 6 July came to 185, and 65 more came in with Captain Richard Dashmer.

### Where the accounts disagree
- **The clock.** Appleman does not say which clock. Korea kept GMT+9, but South Korea tried a summer time of GMT+10 in 1948 to 1951; no source read says whether the Army used it.
- **When the tanks were seen.** Appleman notes that Smith told him about 07:00 but said 07:45 in an interview on 7 July; Eversole recalled 07:45, Barth about 08:00, and a telephone report 08:18. Wikipedia gives about 07:30.
- **How many men.** Appleman gives 540; the Center of Military History's brochure, about 500; the Army's 2019 article, the 406 infantry.
- **The howitzers.** Wikipedia gives the battery six, "five remaining" after one was lost. Appleman says the sixth was left at P'yongt'aek with a broken prime mover, so five were at Osan; he also says several reports wrongly had all of them destroyed. The game gives six.
- **The tanks hit.** Appleman counts four destroyed or immobilised and three damaged; Wikipedia's summary, one destroyed and three disabled.
- **The North Korean infantry.** Wikipedia puts the column at nearly 5,000 men; Appleman says about 1,000 got off the trucks and deployed in front of the ridge.
- **The American losses.** Smith reckoned about 150 of the infantry, 155 by one estimate; the division's first report said 148 men and 5 officers missing. On 7 July 5 officers and 26 men of the artillery were still missing. Wikipedia gives a later count of 60 dead, 21 wounded and 82 captured, of whom 32 died in captivity. Wikipedia's *Korean War*, after Stokesbury, gives 180 casualties. The Army's 2019 article says 150 killed or missing. Appleman counts 250 survivors by 6 July.
- **The North Korean losses.** Appleman gives about 42 killed and 85 wounded, and warns that some of these may have been lost at P'yongt'aek the next day.
- **The wounded left behind.** Appleman could not find the medical sergeant's name. Wikipedia says the litter cases were later found shot where they lay and the medic was never seen again. Appleman records 36 men under Second Lieutenant Jansen C. Cox captured on 6 July; Seoul radio claimed 72.
