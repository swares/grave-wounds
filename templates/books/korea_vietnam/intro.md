## The period's fighting {#fighting}

This book covers two wars in Asia: Korea, from 1950 to 1953, and Vietnam, from 1965 to 1973, the
years when American and Australian armies fought there. Its battles run from Task Force Smith at
Osan in July 1950 to Hamburger Hill in May 1969. Both wars were fought for hills and ridges by
infantry with the full weight of guns and aircraft behind them, and in both the helicopter carried
the wounded to the surgeon.

### Armies

The United States fought both wars with the **draft**. Between June 1950 and the armistice of 1953
Selective Service inducted over 1.5 million men; about 1.3 million more volunteered, most of them
for the Navy or the Air Force. The act of 1951 lowered the age of induction to 18 and a half and
made service 24 months. For Vietnam, from August 1964 to February 1973, the tables count 1,857,304
men inducted. College students could be deferred. On 1 December 1969 a lottery by birthday set the
order of call for men born from 1944 to 1950. The last draft call went out on 7 December 1972, and
in January 1973 the draft ended.

Australia also sent conscripts. Its **national servicemen** were chosen by a birthday ballot of
twenty-year-olds: marbles, each standing for two birthdays, were drawn from a barrel. The scheme ran
from November 1964 to December 1972. Of 804,286 young men registered, the Australian War Memorial
counts 63,735 who served in the Army. The Department of Veterans' Affairs says more than 15,300 of
them served in Vietnam, and more than 200 national servicemen died overseas.

In Korea the American divisions of 1950 were short of men. In August 1950 President Syngman Rhee and
General Douglas MacArthur agreed, by word of mouth only, to put Korean soldiers into American units.
These were the **KATUSA**, the Korean Augmentation to the United States Army. Some volunteered and
some were taken by force; before the Inchon landing the 7th Infantry Division took 313 men from
Busan. In all 43,660 served with the Americans, and 11,365 were killed or missing.

In October 1950 the Chinese came into the war as the **People's Volunteer Army** (PVA). The Center
of Military History's account says over 300,000 men in 30 divisions were in Korea by 6 November,
while MacArthur's intelligence staff counted 34,500. Experts had expected them to be poorly armed,
poorly led and badly supplied. They were short of supplies but well motivated and hardened by years
of war. They attacked at night, often to bugles, and set roadblocks behind the Americans.

In Vietnam the enemy came in two forms. The National Liberation Front (NLF), founded in the South on
20 December 1960, was the **Viet Cong** of the Americans; its army was the Liberation Army of South
Vietnam. It fought under Hanoi's direction, and Vietnam's official history calls it a part of the
North's army. The **People's Army of Vietnam** (PAVN) sent its regulars south down the Ho Chi Minh
Trail, run from 1959 by Group 559; about 200,000 went south before the Tet offensive of 1968. Tet
cost the Front dearly. "Tet cost us half our forces," one of its leaders, Trương Như Tảng, later
wrote.

### Arms

The American in Korea carried the **M1 Garand**, the self-loading rifle of 1939-45; a "winter
trigger" let him fire it in gloves. By Vietnam it had given way to the M14, and from March 1965 the
Army began to issue the **M16**. It was light, fired a small 5.56 mm round from a 20-round magazine,
and in its first years jammed. The powder fouled it, it came without cleaning kits, and its chamber
was not chromed, so spent cases stuck. A newspaper story of 26 March 1967 led to a Congressional
inquiry. The M16A1, standardised in February 1967, had a chromed chamber and came with cleaning
kits. Against it the Viet Cong and the PAVN carried the **Kalashnikov**, most often the Chinese
copy, the Type 56. American reports had said early on that men with the heavier M14 could not carry
enough rounds to match its fire.

In Korea the Americans had the Browning Automatic Rifle and the belt-fed Browning machine guns of
the last war. In Vietnam the **M60** machine gun, the "Pig", was meant to replace both. It fired 550
to 650 rounds a minute and weighed over 10 kg, and riflemen carried belts and spare barrels for it.

Fragments of **mortar** bombs, **artillery** shells, grenades and mines did much of the wounding; on
Hamburger Hill, Galloway says, about half the American losses. "We must expend steel and fire, not
men," said General James Van Fleet in May 1951. On Hamburger Hill in 1969 the guns fired some 20,000
rounds at one mountain. **Napalm**, a jellied petrol, came from the air. The US Air Force dropped
more than 30,000 tons of it on Korea, and some 388,000 short tons of American napalm bombs fell in
South-East Asia from 1963 to 1973.

The **helicopter** changed both wars. In Korea small Bell H-13s carried two patients in litters
outside the cabin. By Vietnam the United States had some 4,000 helicopters in the theatre, and the
Bell UH-1, the Huey, carried the infantry into battle and the wounded out.

### Battles

The first year in Korea was a war of movement, south to Pusan, north to the Yalu and back. Talks for
a cease-fire opened at Kaesong on 10 July 1951, and while they went on, the line settled across the
peninsula. Both sides dug in along the **ridges**: trenches, bunkers and caves on the Main Line of
Resistance, with **outposts** on the hills in front. Each outpost of the Nevada Cities in March 1953
was held by a platoon of about 40 Marines and two Navy corpsmen. Vegas, the tallest, stood about
1,200 m beyond the line. Patrols and raids went out by night; the big fights were for single hills,
held for the view they gave. The armistice was signed on 27 July 1953 and set a demilitarised zone 4
km wide along the front.

!!! story "Reckless"
    In October 1952 Lieutenant Eric Pedersen of the 5th Marines' recoilless rifle platoon paid
    $250 of his own money for a chestnut mare at the Seoul racecourse. The Marines named her
    Reckless, after the rifle, and trained her to carry its 24-pound shells up to the guns and
    the wounded down. The figures usually given for Outpost Vegas in March 1953, and repeated by
    the National Museum of the Marine Corps, are 51 trips alone in one day, 386 rounds and more
    than 35 miles; the museum ties them to no source, and much else told of her is unit legend.
    What is sure is that she was wounded twice, was made a sergeant in 1954 and a staff sergeant
    by General Randolph Pate in 1959, and died in May 1968.

In Vietnam there was no front. Under General William Westmoreland, in 1966 and 1967, American units
went out on **search and destroy**: companies and battalions moved out from their bases to find the
enemy, fight him and count his dead. The **body count** became the measure of success. Units went in
by helicopter to a **landing zone**, often a clearing just big enough for a few Hueys, and sometimes
came under fire as they landed. The guns that supported them sat on a **fire support base**: a
battery of six howitzers, two helicopter pads, an aid station and an infantry company to guard them,
out in the country. At first they moved every two days or so; later many became small forts. After
Hamburger Hill the Americans were told to spare their own men, and the war passed slowly to the
South Vietnamese.

### Wounds and medicine

The **Mobile Army Surgical Hospital** (MASH) was the new hospital of Korea: 60 beds under canvas, 5
to 40 km behind the front. The 8076th had 10 doctors, 12 nurses and 89 enlisted men, and once took
in over 600 casualties in a day. Most wounded still came by jeep and truck ambulance. A seriously
wounded man who reached a MASH alive, King and Jatoi write, had a better than 97% chance of living.

{{picture:korea_vietnam-helicopter-1951}}

The Air Force flew the first helicopter ambulances in July 1950. The Army's 2nd Helicopter
Detachment began work on 1 January 1951. By the armistice, Dorland and Nanney count, Army
helicopters had flown about 17,700 casualties, with only eleven aircraft at the peak. They brought
10 to 20% of those who reached the MASHs.

In Vietnam the helicopter ambulance became the **Dustoff**, from the call sign the 57th Medical
Detachment chose in 1963. Unarmed Hueys marked with red crosses flew in to the fighting, and where
they could not land they lowered a hoist through the trees. Dorland and Nanney say they carried
almost 900,000 American and allied sick and wounded. The Vietnam Archive at Texas Tech says most
wounded men were in a hospital within 35 minutes; another account gives an average of 2.8 hours.
Either way the time from wound to surgeon was shorter than in any war before. Of the wounded who
reached treatment, 2.6% died, against 4.5% in 1939-45; the figure for Korea is given as 2.5%.

!!! story "When I have your wounded"
    Major Charles Kelly took command of the 57th Medical Detachment in January 1964 and moved
    south to Soc Trang, in the Mekong Delta. He held that ambulance helicopters should fly
    unarmed and go in under fire. On 1 July 1964 he flew to a field south of Vinh Long to pick
    up a wounded American adviser. The men on the ground told him to leave: the landing zone was
    under fire. Most accounts give his answer as "When I have your wounded"; Peter Arnett had it
    as "I'll move when I have your wounded with me." A single bullet came through the open cargo
    door and struck him in the heart. He was the 149th American to die in Vietnam. His unit flew
    on as he had taught it, and it became the Dustoff way.

Surgeons learned to save legs. In 1939-45 a torn main artery was tied off, and about half of those
limbs were lost. In Korea, from December 1951, John Howard's surgical research team at the 46th MASH
and the young surgeon Edward Jahnke began to repair arteries instead. They got six Potts clamps, one
for each MASH, and taught a pair of surgeons from each how to use them in a day. Carl Hughes
reported an amputation rate of 13% over 304 repairs. In Vietnam limbs were saved in 87% of cases,
Rich found.

Men began to wear **body armour** again. In Korea the M1951 vest, nylon with plates of a fibreglass
called Doron, weighed just under 8 pounds. The Army's nylon M1952A reached the front only late in
1952, about 26,161 of them by July 1953. In a field test in Korea in 1952 a trial vest stopped 75.7%
of fragments but only 24.4% of bullets. The Vietnam vests could not stop a Kalashnikov round either,
and in the heat men mostly wore them in fixed positions and convoys.

Disease came with the country. In Vietnam malaria put an average of 26.7 men in every 1,000 into
hospital each year, against 11.2 in Korea; in December 1965 the Army's rate reached 98.4, and in
some units in the Ia Drang valley 600. In Korea the enemy was the **cold**. At the Chosin Reservoir
in November and December 1950 the temperature fell to minus 30 °F (about minus 34 °C) and below.
About 8,000 men there were frostbitten. Canteens and rations froze, and fires were forbidden. Of the
frostbite cases at Percy Jones hospital in Michigan, about 90% were of the feet. Surgeons tried to
save what they could, often taking only fingertips rather than whole hands.

### Women

In both wars American women served as **nurses**. Some 540 nurses of the Army Nurse Corps served in
Korea, in the MASHs and the hospitals of Korea and Japan. They triaged, started transfusions and
penicillin, and sutured wounds. The Navy had 3,081 active and 1,702 reserve nurses in the war, on
hospital ships and evacuation aircraft. One Army nurse, Genevieve Smith, died on her way to her
post; the National Archives finds no woman nurse killed by the enemy.

Over 11,000 American servicewomen served in the Vietnam war, most of them nurses. Eight died: seven
Army nurses and one of the Air Force. Over 340 Australian nurses, military and civilian, served with
their forces, 43 of them from the Royal Australian Army Nursing Corps.

!!! story "Ward 4"
    Sharon Lane trained as a nurse at Aultman Hospital in Canton, Ohio, and joined the Army
    Nurse Corps Reserve in April 1968. After a year on the tuberculosis and cardiac wards at
    Fitzsimons General Hospital in Denver, she reached the 312th Evacuation Hospital at Chu Lai
    on 29 April 1969. She worked on the Vietnamese ward, which took civilians, soldiers and
    prisoners, and in her off-duty hours in the surgical intensive care unit. On 8 June 1969 a
    122 mm rocket struck between the huts of Ward 4. The fragments killed her and a Vietnamese
    child. She was 25, and had been in Vietnam 41 days. She was the only American servicewoman
    killed by enemy fire in the war.

On the other side women also fought. At least 1.5 million women served in the North's forces. Women
made up as much as 70% of the Youth Shock Brigades on the Ho Chi Minh Trail, who built and mended
the roads, worked as guides, defused bombs and nursed the wounded. In the South, Nguyễn Thị Định, a
co-founder of the NLF, became its deputy commander. Bến Tre province alone counted over 3,000 women
guerrillas in 1961, the "long-haired army". Võ Thị Mô was second in command of a battalion near Cu
Chi. Đặng Thùy Trâm, a surgeon, treated the wounded of the Front and the PAVN from 1966 until she
was killed in 1970; her diaries, published in 2005, sold over 300,000 copies in a year.

!!! gm "For the GM"
    Expect a fight in Korea or Vietnam to be for a hill, and to be decided by fire. Fragments
    from mortars, shells and grenades do most of the wounding, and a man in a bunker or a deep
    hole is far safer than one in the open. The enemy fights at night and close, where the guns
    cannot help the Americans. A wounded American's chances are better than in any earlier war:
    the aidman, then a helicopter, then a surgeon within an hour or two, with whole blood,
    antibiotics and, from 1952, a repaired artery. A wounded Chinese, North Korean or North
    Vietnamese soldier has no helicopter, and must be carried. In Korea cold will take as many
    men as the enemy; in Vietnam, mosquitoes and the heat.
