## Why the team marches {#frames}

Four reasons for a team to be in Korea or Vietnam. A frame adds no rules: it says who pays, what the
team is after and what ends it, and uses the [patrons](rules.html#patrons), [pay](rules.html#rank) and
[work](rules.html#work) in the Rules. The history in each frame is sourced below. The goals, first jobs
and complications are ours, written for play.

### The first to go {#frame-first}

When North Korea invaded on 25 June 1950, the nearest American troops were the occupation divisions in
Japan, under strength and out of practice. Task Force Smith, about 540 men of the 1st Battalion, 21st
Infantry, was flown in to stand in the road at Osan, and met the North Korean tanks there on 5 July.
That winter the 1st Marine Division, filled out with called-up reservists, fought its way out of the
Chosin Reservoir.

- **Who pays:** the army, as in Japan, with combat pay still to come.
- **The goal:** hold the road until more come, and get your men out.
- **What ends it:** relief, a wound, or the unit overrun.
- **First job:** dig in across the road north of town, and wait.
- **What goes wrong:** the bazooka rounds bounce off the tanks. The flank is open. It is colder than anyone has ever been.
- **Maps:** Osan, Chosin Reservoir.

### Under the United Nations flag {#frame-un}

Sixteen nations sent fighting units to Korea under the United Nations Command. At Kapyong in April 1951
the 27th British Commonwealth Brigade's Australians and Canadians held the valley against a Chinese
offensive, and were awarded the US Presidential Unit Citation. A French battalion fought beside the
Americans at Chipyong-ni that February.

- **Who pays:** your own country, at your own army's rates.
- **The goal:** do your part, and be seen to do it.
- **What ends it:** your battalion's tour, a wound, or the war's end.
- **First job:** a hill on the line, taken over from a battalion of another army.
- **What goes wrong:** no one on the next hill speaks your language. Your supplies come by another nation's road. The Americans pull back on your flank.
- **Maps:** Kapyong, Chipyong-ni.

### Points to go home {#frame-points}

From 1951, as the line stood still, the US Army sent its men home by points: four a month in the close
combat zone, two in the rear areas, and home at 36. Men knew their totals to the day. The outposts on
Heartbreak Ridge and Pork Chop Hill were held by men counting.

- **Who pays:** the army, with combat pay from 1952.
- **The goal:** reach 36 points.
- **What ends it:** your points, a wound, or the armistice.
- **First job:** an outpost on the line, and a patrol into the valley.
- **What goes wrong:** a man with 35 points will not go on patrol. The hill is given up after you take it. The cease-fire is a rumour every week.
- **Maps:** Heartbreak Ridge, Pork Chop Hill.

### A year's tour {#frame-tour}

An American soldier went to Vietnam for twelve months (a Marine for thirteen), alone, not with his unit,
and left on a fixed date. He joined a unit already in the field as a replacement, and left it as one.

- **Who pays:** the government, with combat pay and tax free.
- **The goal:** make it to your date.
- **What ends it:** your date, a wound, or the dust-off.
- **First job:** a landing zone, and a patrol out from it.
- **What goes wrong:** the new man is put on point. The man you came in with is in another company. Your date comes in the middle of an operation.
- **Maps:** Ia Drang, Dak To, Khe Sanh, Hue, Hamburger Hill.
