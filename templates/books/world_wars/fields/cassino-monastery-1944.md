### The campaign
By January 1944 the Fifth Army under General Mark Clark faced the Gustav Line, the German line across Italy along the Garigliano and Rapido rivers. At Cassino it barred the Liri valley, the way to Rome, which was just over 10 kilometres wide and overlooked by Monte Cassino, about 500 metres high, with the Benedictine abbey on top (NZHistory). The Fifth Army reached the line on 15 January after six weeks of fighting that had cost it about 16,000 men (Majdalany).

The first battle was meant to draw German reserves away from the landing at Anzio on 22 January. The British 10 Corps crossed the Garigliano from 17 January. On the night of 20 January the US 36th Division tried to cross the Rapido near Sant'Angelo and was thrown back; the 141st Infantry withdrew at daybreak on the 21st, and a second try on the 21st also failed (Blumenson). From 24 January the 34th Division crossed north of the town, took the Italian barracks and Monte Castellone, and fought along the ridges towards the abbey. One battalion reached Monte Albaneta, a little more than a mile north-west of the abbey, and another came within several hundred yards of it before it was driven back. By 10 February the 168th Infantry's three battalions had 154, 393 and 246 effective men (Blumenson).

Alexander and Clark had agreed that if the Americans had not broken the line by 12 February, a New Zealand Corps under Lieutenant General Bernard Freyberg would take over. It had been formed on 3 February from the 2nd New Zealand Division and the 4th Indian Division (Blumenson). Major General Francis Tuker of the 4th Indian Division judged that the abbey must be destroyed before his men attacked, and Freyberg asked for bombers on 12 February. Clark did not think it a military necessity, but Alexander left the decision to Freyberg, and General Wilson approved it after Generals Eaker and Devers reported seeing soldiers and a radio aerial (Blumenson).

On the morning of 15 February the bombers came. Blumenson has the first of about 250 attack at 09:45 and almost 600 tons dropped, and the whole top of the mountain turned to rubble. Hapgood and Richardson find that the only people killed in the abbey were about 230 Italian civilians sheltering there. The abbot and most of the monks survived in the crypt (Rickard). The bombing came a day and a half before the Indians were ready to attack (NZHistory).

### The places
- **Monte Cassino**: the mountain above the town, with the abbey founded by St Benedict on its top.
- **Snakeshead Ridge**: the narrow crest, about 1,200 yards long with deep ravines on either side, along which the Indians attacked (*The Tiger Triumphs*).
- **Point 593**: the height at the western tip of the ridge, with the ruins of an old fort; the Poles later called it Monte Calvario.
- **Point 444 or 445**: the knoll below the abbey that the Gurkhas made for; *The Tiger Triumphs* calls it Point 445.
- **The copse**: the thorn scrub on the slope below the ridge, mined and wired, where the 1/2nd Gurkhas were caught.
- **Monte Castellone and Albaneta**: the heights to the north and north-west taken or reached by the Americans in early February.
- **Cassino railway station**: south of the town, taken by the 28th (Maori) Battalion on the night of 17 February and lost the next day.
- **The Rapido**: the river in front of the town, flooded by the Germans.

### The leaders
- **Bernard Freyberg** (Allies, New Zealand Corps): asked for the bombing and called it, in Blumenson's report, "his considered opinion that it is a military necessity".
- **Francis Tuker** (Allies, 4th Indian Division): fell ill on 4 February but argued from his bed that the abbey must be destroyed first.
- **Harry Dimoline** (Allies, 4th Indian Division): commanded the division in Tuker's place.
- **O. de T. Lovett** (Allies, 7th Indian Brigade): his brigade, the Royal Sussex, Punjabis and 1/2nd Gurkhas, held the ridge.
- **Lieutenant Colonel Showers** (Allies, 1/2nd Gurkha Rifles): badly wounded in the copse.
- **Mark Clark** (Allies, Fifth Army): opposed the bombing but deferred to Alexander.
- **Fridolin von Senger und Etterlin** (German, XIV Panzer Corps): said after the war that no German troops had been inside the abbey before the bombing.
- **Ernst-Günther Baade** (German, 90th Panzer Grenadier Division): in charge of all troops in the Cassino position from 1 February.

### The battle
The bombing did not take the hill. Bombs fell short on the Indian posts on Snakeshead Ridge, and the forward posts had been drawn back. The two lines were so close that the guns could not fire a barrage for the infantry (*The Tiger Triumphs*). Point 593 was made the first objective. On the night of 15 February one company of the 1st Royal Sussex went for it and was stopped by a palisade of boulders about 70 yards on; it came back with 20 casualties. On the 16th the whole battalion attacked. By 22:00 it was on the approaches, and Lieutenant Dennis Cox led a charge into the ruined fort. Then a German flare was read as an order to withdraw, and before dawn the Sussex gave the place up. Holmes counts the battalion's loss over the two nights at 12 of 15 officers and 162 of 313 men.

The next attack was a divisional one. At midnight on 17 February the 4/6th Rajputana Rifles, with three Sussex companies, went for Point 593. Major Markham Lee reached the crest with a handful of men and died there. By 03:30 the attack had stopped. About 02:00 the 1/2nd and 1/9th Gurkhas went down the slopes for the abbey. The leading companies of the 1/2nd ran into the copse, which no one had scouted. It was sown with mines and trip-wires and covered by machine guns, and two-thirds of the leading companies were hit within five minutes. Men lay dead with four or more trip-wires round their legs. The other companies, with men of the 1/9th, went round the left of the copse, and shortly before dawn a company got a lodgement on Point 445 (*The Tiger Triumphs*).

At 04:30 a fourth Rajputana company was thrown in and failed to take the summit, and the Germans won back part of the crest. To move by day would have been suicide, and the Gurkhas came back from Point 445 after dark. Wikipedia gives the Rajputanas' loss as 196, the 1/9th's as 149 and the 1/2nd's as 96. In the valley the Maori had taken the station, but no bridge could be built behind them, and German tanks drove them out on the afternoon of the 18th. Dimoline and Freyberg called the attack off that day.

The wounded had to be brought back along the ridge under fire. In the copse Naik Bir Bahadur Thapa, hit in about a dozen places, held on beyond the scrub with a few survivors.

### Where the accounts disagree
- **The clock.** None of the accounts says which clock it used. Italy and the German army both kept GMT+1 that winter (tz database).
- **The bombing.** Blumenson has about 250 bombers from 09:45 and almost 600 tons. *The Tiger Triumphs* has 15 waves from 08:00 and 35 tons in the morning and afternoon. Laurie, cited by Wikipedia, gives 142 B-17s, 47 B-25s and 40 B-26s; Wikipedia gives 1,150 tonnes in its text and 1,400 in its introduction.
- **The dead in the abbey.** Hapgood and Richardson give about 230 civilians. Rickard gives 280 to 300 refugees. A civilian witness quoted by Blumenson said some 2,500 people had been inside; the abbot spoke of hundreds of innocent people killed.
- **Were the Germans inside?** Senger said not, and the Fifth Army's Counter Intelligence Corps found it unoccupied on 26 February (Blumenson). Eaker and Devers reported soldiers and an aerial. Paratroops moved into the ruins after the abbot left on 17 February (Rickard).
- **Short bombs.** *The Tiger Triumphs* and Blumenson give 24 Indian casualties; General Walker heard of about 40.
- **The second Sussex night.** *The Tiger Triumphs* has the fort taken and given up over a misread flare. Blumenson has five battalions regain the lost ground and be driven back by counter-attacks at daybreak.
- **The Gurkhas' hill.** *The Tiger Triumphs* names Point 445; other accounts give Point 444.
- **The Rajputana and Gurkha losses.** The figures in Wikipedia have no source given.
