### The campaign
Britain declared war on Germany on 4 August 1914. The British Expeditionary Force (BEF), about 80,000 men in two corps and a cavalry division, began to cross to France on 9 August and marched into Belgium on the left of the French Fifth Army. The German First Army was coming through Belgium on the right wing of the German advance.

On 21 August Private John Parr of the 4th Middlesex was killed on patrol, the first British soldier to die in the war. On 22 August the BEF reached Mons. II Corps under General Sir Horace Smith-Dorrien took the line of the Mons-Condé canal west and north of the town. I Corps stood back on the right, at an angle to it.

North of Mons the canal bends north in a loop round the village of Nimy. Doyle calls it "a most awkward loop": troops in it could be shelled from both sides. The 3rd Division held it: the 4th Royal Fusiliers of the 9th Brigade the western face and the bridges at Nimy, the 4th Middlesex of the 8th Brigade the eastern face as far as Obourg.

The times below are British. The BEF kept Greenwich time in 1914 and German clocks ran an hour ahead; the official historian, Edmonds, converts German times to British.

### The places
- **The Mons-Condé canal**: about 60 feet wide and 7 feet deep, Kendall says, with 18 bridges on this stretch.
- **Nimy**: the village inside the loop of the canal, about 2 miles north of the centre of Mons.
- **The Nimy railway bridge**: on the Paris-Brussels line, where Dease's two machine guns stood.
- **The Nimy road bridge**: a swing bridge on the Chaussée de Bruxelles, the road north out of Mons.
- **Lock No. 6 and the park gate bridge**: further west, held by Captain Byng's company of the Fusiliers.
- **Obourg**: the station and bridge on the eastern face of the salient, held by the 4th Middlesex.
- **The Grand Place, Mons**: where the Fusiliers rested on 22 August, and fell back to on 23 August.
- **St Symphorien**: east of Mons. Dease, and the German soldier Oskar Niemeyer, are buried in the military cemetery there.

### The leaders
- **Sir John French** (British, commander of the BEF).
- **Sir Horace Smith-Dorrien** (British, II Corps): held the canal with the 3rd and 5th Divisions.
- **Hubert Hamilton** (British, 3rd Division): killed at La Couture on 14 October.
- **Norman McMahon** (British, 4th Royal Fusiliers): lieutenant colonel. At the School of Musketry he had introduced the standard of 15 aimed rounds a minute. Killed near Hooge on 11 November.
- **Charles Hull** (British, 4th Middlesex): lieutenant colonel, at Obourg and through the retreat.
- **Maurice Dease** (British, 4th Royal Fusiliers): lieutenant, the battalion's machine-gun officer, aged 24.
- **Alexander von Kluck** (German, First Army).
- **Ferdinand von Quast** (German, IX Corps): his 18th Division attacked the salient; its 35th Brigade had Infantry Regiment No. 84 and Fusilier Regiment No. 86.

### The battle
German guns opened on the salient at dawn. Ernest Hamilton, writing in 1916, has the first infantry attack at 08:00 on the north-west corner of the loop. About 09:00 four German battalions, of the 84th Regiment by the visitmons guide, came at the Nimy bridges in close column. Rifle and machine-gun fire at short range brought the leading sections down together. The survivors took cover, and about half an hour later came on again in extended order.

At the railway bridge Captain Ashburner's company held the line with Dease's two guns. Dease was hit below the knee early on, and again as he went between the guns. Captain Forster, beside Ashburner, was shot through the arm and stomach. Ashburner was wounded in the head. Second Lieutenant Mead brought up a platoon. He was hit in the head, had the wound dressed, went back to the line and was killed. Captain Bowden-Smith and Lieutenant Smith brought up another, and both were badly wounded within ten minutes.

The visitmons guide says the Germans attacked in force at 11:00 and brought up more guns. At Obourg the 4th Middlesex, under fire from about 08:00, asked for help around midday, and two companies of the 2nd Royal Irish came up from Hyon. On the German side Musketier Oskar Niemeyer of the 84th swam the canal under fire, came back with a boat, and worked the swing bridge so that his comrades could cross. He was killed.

The order to retire came at 13:10, by the visitmons guide, and the Fusiliers went at 13:40. By then most of the machine-gun crews were down. Dease had been hit a fifth time and could not move. Lieutenant Steele carried him back, and he died of his wounds. Private Sidney Godley took over a gun and kept it firing while the battalion withdrew across about 250 yards of open ground. The Fusiliers fell back along the Chaussée de Bruxelles into Mons, leaving most of their wounded in German hands. The official report gave their loss as 7 officers and 105 other ranks; Ernest Hamilton says the Middlesex lost 15 officers and 353 men.

The Germans crossed that afternoon and took Nimy. They burned 108 houses along the Chaussée, killed 22 civilians, and drove hostages, among them the mayor, ahead of them into Mons. At 02:00 on 24 August II Corps was ordered to retreat. The retreat went on into September, more than 250 miles, through Le Cateau on 26 August to the Marne.

### Where the accounts disagree
- **When it began.** Ernest Hamilton has a first attack at 08:00 and the attack on the Nimy bridge about 09:00; Kendall, attacks at 08:00 and 09:00; the visitmons guide, the 84th advancing from 08:30; Wikipedia, the first assault at 09:00.
- **When the Fusiliers left.** The visitmons guide has the order at 13:10 and the move at 13:40. Kendall says the order came at 14:00. Ernest Hamilton says the 3rd Division's brigades were ordered back at 15:00, as does Wikipedia.
- **Who held the road bridge.** The visitmons guide puts Ashburner's company on both the road and railway bridges, with Byng's company further west at Lock No. 6. Kendall and Luscombe put Captain Forster with two platoons at the road bridge. Ernest Hamilton puts Byng's company on the Ghlin bridge, and Godley's VC there too.
- **The attackers.** Ernest Hamilton counts four battalions in the first charge. Kendall gives six battalions of the 18th Division, about 6,000 men (he calls it III Corps; the Wikipedia articles and the German order of battle give IX Corps).
- **Dease's wounds and death.** His citation says he was hit five times; O'Neill's regimental history, as Wikipedia gives it, makes the third wound fatal. Steele wrote to the family that he was first hit about 09:00. Ashcroft says he died about 15:30; the visitmons guide says in the ambulance, Wikipedia at the battalion aid post.
- **Godley's stand.** Wikipedia says he threw the gun's parts into the canal and was taken there. Kendall and Ashcroft say two Belgians helped him to hospital in Mons, where the Germans found him. O'Neill says both guns were riddled with bullets before the end.
- **The losses.** Edmonds gives the BEF's loss at Mons as just over 1,600. For the Germans, Zuber argues from regimental histories for no more than 2,000; Keegan and Herwig give about 5,000. The German medical history counts 2,145 dead and missing and 4,932 wounded for the whole First Army, 20-31 August.

!!! story "Two hours on the bridge"
    Sidney Godley was a private in the 4th Royal Fusiliers' machine-gun section at the Nimy railway
    bridge. When Dease was hit for the last time, Lieutenant Steele asked for a man to stay on the
    gun and cover the battalion's withdrawal. Godley took it. A shell burst put a fragment into his
    back and a bullet hit him in the head. His citation speaks of "fighting his machine gun under a
    hot fire for two hours" after he was wounded. When the ammunition ran out he broke up the gun and
    threw the pieces into the canal. He was taken prisoner, and spent the war in a camp near Berlin,
    where he heard of his Victoria Cross. The King gave it to him on 15 February 1919.
