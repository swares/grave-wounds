### The campaign
In 1916 the British and French planned an offensive on the Somme, to open on 1 July along a front of about 40 km. The northern flank of the British Fourth Army was VIII Corps under Lieutenant General Aylmer Hunter-Weston. Opposite Beaumont-Hamel stood his 29th Division, back from Gallipoli.

The Newfoundland Regiment, one battalion from the dominion of Newfoundland, had served at Gallipoli from September 1915 to January 1916. It reached France in March in the 88th Brigade. It was made the Royal Newfoundland Regiment only in 1917. Facing the division was Reserve Infantry Regiment 119 of the 26th (Württemberg) Reserve Division, which had held the sector for about 20 months. It had dug deep dugouts, and in Y Ravine shelters for a battalion.

The bombardment began on 24 June. The attack was put back from 29 June to 1 July, partly for the weather and partly to give the guns longer. The German wire survived in many places, and the men in the dugouts were largely unhurt. On the night of 30 June the Newfoundlanders marched up from Louvencourt to St John's Road, a support trench behind the front line.

### The places
- **St John's Road**: the support trench, out of German sight, from which the Newfoundlanders went over.
- **The British front line**: on the crest about 250 m ahead, with bridges over the trench and lanes cut in the wire.
- **No man's land**: open grass falling to the German wire, about 400 m wide opposite Y Ravine and narrower to the north-west.
- **The Danger Tree**: a stripped tree about halfway across. A replica stands on the spot.
- **Y Ravine**: a gully that forked at the German front line and ran back into the German position, its dugouts hidden in its sides.
- **Station Road**: the road to the railway station in the valley behind the German line, the Newfoundlanders' objective.
- **Hawthorn Ridge**: to the north, where a mine blew up the German redoubt ten minutes before zero. Its crater is still there.
- **The Newfoundland Memorial Park**: about 74 acres bought by Newfoundland in 1921, the trenches left in place under a bronze caribou. The game's field is the middle of the park, from St John's Road to the German support trench, with Y Ravine on the left; the Hawthorn crater is off the board to the north.

### The leaders
- **Aylmer Hunter-Weston** (British, VIII Corps): wanted the Hawthorn mine fired early; it went at ten minutes before zero.
- **Henry de Beauvoir de Lisle** (British, 29th Division): sent in two reserve battalions at 08:45.
- **Douglas Edward Cayley** (British, 88th Brigade): ordered the Newfoundlanders forward "as soon as possible".
- **Arthur Lovell Hadow** (British, 1st Newfoundland Regiment): finding the communication trenches choked, he sent the battalion over the open.
- **R. C. Pierce** (British, 1st Royal Inniskilling Fusiliers): killed in the first wave.
- **Ellis** (British, 1st Border): wounded in the second wave and brought in by Private Newcombe.
- **G. E. Malcolm** (British, captain, 1st KOSB): tried to carry the attack on with about 40 men and was wounded.
- **Reserve Infantry Regiment 119** (German, the defenders): no source read for this page names its commander.

### The battle
Times here are British, GMT+1; German clocks were an hour ahead. At 07:20 the Hawthorn Ridge mine went up, and RIR 119 came up from its dugouts. At 07:30 the barrage lifted, moving back 100 yards every two minutes, and the 86th and 87th Brigades went over. On the 87th's front the 2nd South Wales Borderers and 1st Royal Inniskilling Fusiliers led. The Border diary says the Borderers were cut down while still in their own wire. At about 07:35 the 1st KOSB and 1st Border crossed the bridges over their own front trench, ranged by the German gunners the day before, and were stopped in no man's land. Veterans Affairs says only parts of the Inniskillings, on the right, got into the German trenches.

White German signal rockets were taken for British success flares. At 08:45 de Lisle ordered the 1st Newfoundland Regiment and 1st Essex to take the German first trench and clear forward to Station Road. The trenches were full of wounded, so Hadow sent the Newfoundlanders over the top. The Essex, in view from Thiepval, had to use the trenches.

The Newfoundlanders left at 09:15, A and B Companies in front and C and D 100 yards behind. They went alone. Many were hit before they reached their own front line, and the war diary says the heaviest losses came in the gaps in the British wire, where men were "mown down in heaps". The survivors went on down the slope; most who reached the Danger Tree went no further, though the diary believed a few got close enough to throw bombs. Within 15 to 20 minutes most of the battalion was down. At 09:45 Hadow reported to brigade that the attack had failed.

The Essex reached the front line at 10:50 and attacked. A halt order from division had not reached them. Their two leading companies lost about 250 men before Lieutenant Colonel Halahan stopped them. At 14:00 the 88th Brigade took over the line from the 87th.

The 88th Brigade's diary put most losses down to machine guns; at the Danger Tree shrapnel hit many. Few wounded could crawl in before dark. Many died where they lay or were hit again, and some were not brought in for four nights. The tin triangles on their backs, meant to show the infantry to aircraft and gunners, flashed in the sun when a wounded man moved. Private Thomas White McGrath, a stretcher-bearer, brought in wounded in daylight under fire and won the Military Medal. Tony Turco counts 471 Newfoundland wounded evacuated, of whom 33 died of their wounds.

### Where the accounts disagree
- **How many went forward.** Veterans Affairs Canada, after Nicholson, gives 22 officers and 758 other ranks, about 780; elsewhere it says about 800. The National Archives blog says about 720. All agree that 68 answered the roll call.
- **The losses.** The war diary of 7 July gives 684: 14 officers and 296 men killed, died of wounds or missing, and 12 officers and 362 men wounded. The Rooms gives 710: 233 dead, 91 missing and 386 wounded, as does *The Canadian Encyclopedia* (324 dead or missing, 386 wounded). Nicholson has all the officers and just under 658 men hit. The Commonwealth War Graves Commission records 243 deaths on 1 July (National Archives blog). Wikipedia puts the loss second only to the 10th West Yorkshires' 733 at Fricourt.
- **When the Essex went.** The Essex war diary, Veterans Affairs and the Basildon Heritage Society say 10:50. The Royal Newfoundland Regiment Museum gives 09:55; the game follows the war diary.
- **Why the Essex were late.** Veterans Affairs says they were in view from Thiepval and had to use the trenches. The Rooms says their commanding officer chose the trenches to spare his men.
- **When Hadow reported.** The war diary and most accounts say 09:45. Turco gives 10:00.
- **The Inniskillings.** Veterans Affairs says parts of the battalion got into the German first to third lines. Their war diary says they could not pass the German wire.
- **The mine.** Wikipedia gives 40,000 lb of ammonal; the Imperial War Museum's caption 45,000 lb; Veterans Affairs and The Rooms 18,500 kg. The crater was 130 feet across and 58 deep by the museum, 40 m by 18 m by Heritage NL.
- **The distances.** Veterans Affairs says more than 200 m to the British front line and more than 500 m on to the German trenches. Heritage NL puts the lines about 400 m apart opposite Y Ravine.
- **The German loss.** Wikipedia, after the German sources, gives RIR 119's loss as 292 men for the whole day. The 29th Division lost 5,240.

!!! story "Fifteen hours in no man's land"
    Private James McGrath was hit in the first rush on 1 July and hit again in the leg as he lay
    out. He later told the *Newfoundland Quarterly* that he lay in no man's land for fifteen hours.
    Then he crawled what he reckoned was a mile and a quarter back to the British line, only to
    find it empty, with the British guns firing heavily. The German reply kept him in a shell hole
    for another hour. Nearing a trench he was hit again, round the hip. Captain Windeler rescued
    him and carried him on his back two miles to a dressing station. "Thank God my wounds are all
    flesh wounds," McGrath wrote. Heritage NL puts his time out at about 17 hours.
