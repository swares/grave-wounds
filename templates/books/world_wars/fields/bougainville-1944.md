### The campaign
In early November 1943 the 3rd Marine Division and the 37th Infantry Division landed at Empress Augusta Bay, on the west coast of Bougainville. They held a perimeter round Cape Torokina and built airfields there, within reach of Rabaul. By March 1944 the XIV Corps under Major General Oscar Griswold held it with the 37th and Americal Divisions: about 27,000 combat troops, and about 62,000 men in all (Miller).

General Hitoshi Imamura at Rabaul and Lieutenant General Harukichi Hyakutake of the 17th Army planned one blow against it. Hyakutake thought he faced about 30,000 men, a third of them airfield ground crew. His main force was Lieutenant General Masatane Kanda's 6th Division, with battalions of the 17th Division. Guns came along the coast by barge at night; the infantry marched inland with about two weeks' rations. Patrols, prisoners, radio intercepts and captured papers warned the Americans.

The plan had three groups. The Iwasa Unit, the 23rd Infantry and the 2nd Battalion, 13th Infantry, under Major General Shun Iwasa, was to take Hill 700 on 8 March. The Muda and Magata Units were to strike further east and west, and all were to reach the Torokina airstrip by 17 March.

### The places
- **Hill 700**: the highest ground the Americans held, on the north side of the perimeter. Its slopes ran at 65 to 75 per cent (Miller).
- **The saddle**: the low ground between the two knobs of Hill 700, where the 23rd Infantry broke in.
- **Cannon Hill**: the next height, held by the 3rd Battalion, 145th Infantry.
- **McClelland Road**: the supply road behind the hill, under Japanese fire from 9 March.
- **The Piva airfields**: the bomber and fighter strips inside the perimeter.
- **Hill 260**: the Americal Division's outpost to the east, attacked from 10 March.
- **Blue Ridge and Hills 250, 600 and 1111**: Japanese-held heights to the north, with their guns.
- **The 21st Evacuation Hospital**: about 4,000 yards behind the front at its nearest point.

### The leaders
- **Harukichi Hyakutake** (Japanese, 17th Army): planned the attack and underestimated the Americans by about half.
- **Masatane Kanda** (Japanese, 6th Division): his division made up most of the attacking force. His answers after the war are one of Miller's sources.
- **Shun Iwasa** (Japanese, Iwasa Unit): a major general, commander of the force sent against Hill 700.
- **Oscar Griswold** (American, XIV Corps): judged the perimeter as well organised as the men and ground allowed, and sent reserves to the 145th from 10 March.
- **Robert S. Beightler** (American, 37th Division): released the 1st Battalion, 145th, on 9 March and brought in the 2nd Battalion, 148th.
- **Cecil B. Whitcomb** (American, 145th Infantry): relieved on 11 March after the assistant division commander, Brigadier General Charles F. Craig, reported him suffering from "extreme battle fatigue" (Miller).
- **Herb Radcliffe** (American, 2nd Battalion, 148th Infantry): a lieutenant colonel; his battalion retook the saddle.
- **Clinton S. McLaughlin** (American, G Company, 145th): wounded twice, he held his position on 11 March and won the Distinguished Service Cross.

### The battle
Japanese guns opened on 8 March. Miller has the first rounds at 05:45; Frankel, about 06:00. They fell mostly on the Piva airfields. The 23rd Infantry spent the day moving up, and did not attack. American guns fired on its assembly areas for two hours from about noon, and Frankel says the 3rd Battalion, 23rd Infantry, was nearly wiped out.

The 2nd Battalion, 145th, held the hill with E and G Companies in line and F Company in reserve. Shortly after midnight, in rain, about two companies came up the north slope against G Company's 1st Platoon and E Company on the summit. They were driven off. At about 02:30 on 9 March the 23rd came again in column of battalions, the 2nd Battalion leading. It blew the wire with bangalore torpedoes, knocked out a pillbox and reached the saddle. It took seven pillboxes, and by noon held a salient about 100 yards wide and 50 deep.

At about noon C Company, 145th, attacked up the south slope, with two platoons of F Company from the flanks. Two M3 light tanks of the 754th Tank Battalion tried to help that afternoon but could not climb the hill. By 17:35 F Company had retaken five pillboxes. Japanese fire closed McClelland Road, and carrying parties took ammunition up and the wounded down by hand.

The Japanese attacked again at 06:45 on 10 March. One engineer bangalore went off among its own men and killed four. At 17:00 men of the 1st and 2nd Battalions attacked with bangalores, rocket launchers and pole charges. They took several pillboxes and lost them again. That night the Japanese came on in close-packed waves and took one more pillbox at the saddle.

On 11 March Whitcomb was relieved and his executive officer, Colonel Freer, took over. At 13:20 the 2nd Battalion, 148th, attacked the saddle after a short bombardment; one platoon reached the crest but lost eight dead. On 12 March E and F Companies, 148th, went in with grenades, rifles, flamethrowers and rocket launchers. The last pillbox fell at 13:17, and by 15:30 the line was whole. The Americans counted 309 Japanese bodies there, and the Iwasa Unit withdrew on 13 March.

The wounded went down by litter, a carry of 1,200 yards, then by half-track and jeep ambulance to the 21st Evacuation Hospital. A Surgeon General's team under Colonel Ashley W. Oughterson studied the Allied casualties on the island from 15 February to 21 April 1944. It arrived on 22 March, after Hill 700, and worked from graves registration files, hospital records and talks with aidmen; from 23 March it autopsied the dead. Of 1,788 casualties, mortars caused 38.8 per cent and rifles 24.9 per cent, but machine guns killed 57.6 per cent of the men they hit. Head wounds were 21.5 per cent, more than in past wars. The study was printed as chapter V of *Wound Ballistics* (1962). The game's Bougainville tables come from its counts.

### Where the accounts disagree
- **The clock and the first shells.** The times are from American records; no source says which clock was kept, and no Japanese times were found in English. Miller has the Japanese guns open at 05:45 on 8 March; Frankel, 06:00; the Ohio National Guard historian, 06:27.
- **Who held the hill.** Miller has the 2nd Battalion, 145th. The Wikipedia article says the 2nd and 3rd Battalions; Frankel puts the 3rd on Cannon Hill.
- **The salient.** Miller gives about 100 yards wide and 50 deep; Frankel, 70 yards wide and 50 deep; the wound study, 75 yards deep on a 100-yard front.
- **The last attack.** Miller has the last pillbox fall at 13:17 on 12 March and the line restored by 15:30. Frankel has E and F Companies, 148th, start at 08:00 and the hill regained by 16:00. The Ohio National Guard has them go over the top at 12:30.
- **Japanese strength.** Miller gives 15,000 to 19,000 for the whole offensive; Kanda later said 19,000 and about 2,000 sailors. Shindo says only 9,548 were committed. No source read gives the Iwasa Unit's strength.
- **Japanese dead at Hill 700.** Miller gives 309 counted at the saddle. Frankel says about 1,500 were buried on the reverse slope. The wound study gives 2,219: 719 counted and 1,500 estimated.
- **American losses.** Miller gives the 37th Division 5 officers and 73 men killed from 8 to 13 March; the Ohio National Guard adds about 400 wounded. The wound study counts 2,600 men engaged on Hill 700 and 519 casualties, 61 of them dead.
- **The whole offensive.** Miller gives over 5,000 Japanese killed, more than 3,000 wounded and 263 American dead. James estimates 3,500 Japanese killed; Shindo, 2,700.

!!! story "They would not be ordered"
    On 9 March Japanese fire covered the road behind Hill 700. Ambulances of A and B Collecting
    Companies, 112th Medical Battalion, waited at G Company's motor pool with the wounded. Colonel
    Whitcomb told the drivers he would not order them to run the road. Eight went anyway, and brought
    their casualties back. Two drivers, Bob Pittman and "Doc" Davis, were lightly hit by mortar
    fragments. Private Joe Bernard of A Company was hit by two snipers, and his orderly was wounded.
    After that, Frankel says, 17 half-tracks shuttled between the line and the aid stations.
