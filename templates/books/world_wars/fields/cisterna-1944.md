### The campaign
By January 1944 the Fifth Army in Italy was held at the Gustav Line, in front of Cassino. To turn the line, the US VI Corps under Major General John P. Lucas landed behind it at Anzio and Nettuno on 22 January 1944. Its task was to take the Alban Hills and cut the roads that fed the German line. The landing was almost unopposed, and Colonel William O. Darby's 6615th Ranger Force, the 1st, 3rd and 4th Ranger Battalions, took the port of Anzio nearly intact.

The Germans moved faster than the Allies. The Fourteenth Army took over the defence on 25 January, and the Hermann Göring Division blocked the roads inland. The 3rd Infantry Division, under Major General Lucian K. Truscott, attacked towards Cisterna from 25 to 27 January and was stopped about three miles short. With some 71,500 Germans reckoned against 61,332 of his own men ashore, Lucas planned a general attack for 30 January, before the odds grew worse.

In the 3rd Division's plan, the 1st and 3rd Rangers were to slip through the German line in the dark, about four miles across the fields, and seize Cisterna and Highway 7 by surprise. The 4th Rangers and the 3rd Battalion, 15th Infantry were to follow up the road, and the 7th and 15th Infantry to attack on the flanks at 02:00. Patrols suggested the German line in front of the town was not yet firm.

### The places
- **Anzio and Nettuno**: the two small ports where VI Corps came ashore.
- **The Mussolini Canal**: the drainage canal across the reclaimed Pontine marshes; its west branch was the start line.
- **The Pantano ditch**: the drainage ditch that ran north-west across the fields, east of the Conca road, and gave the Rangers cover.
- **The Conca–Cisterna road**: the road north to the town, up which the 4th Rangers attacked.
- **Isola Bella**: the hamlet on the road below Cisterna, held by machine guns in farmhouses.
- **Femina Morta**: the place on the road where the 4th Rangers were stopped.
- **The Calcaprini house**: a farm where German self-propelled guns were in bivouac, which the column had to pass.
- **Cisterna**: the town on Highway 7, the old Via Appia, and the railway. It stayed in German hands until May 1944.
- **Rome**: where captured Rangers were marched past the Colosseum.

### The leaders
- **William O. Darby** (American, Ranger Force): raised the 1st Ranger Battalion in 1942. He followed the fight by radio from his command post near the start line.
- **Jack Dobson** (American, 1st Ranger Battalion): newly in command; he led the column to the edge of the town and was wounded.
- **Alvah Miller** (American, 3rd Ranger Battalion): newly in command; killed by a German tank round near dawn.
- **Roy A. Murray** (American, 4th Ranger Battalion): his attack up the road was held all day short of Isola Bella.
- **Robert E. Ehalt** (American, 3rd Ranger Battalion): the senior NCO who sent the last message from the perimeter.
- **Lucian K. Truscott** (American, 3rd Infantry Division): planned the attack, with the Rangers in the lead.
- **John P. Lucas** (American, VI Corps): ordered the general attack of 30 January.
- **Eberhard von Mackensen** (German, Fourteenth Army): was preparing his own counter-attack from round Cisterna.
- **Paul Conrath** (German, Hermann Göring Division): commanded the division that held the town.

### The battle
The 1st Rangers crossed the start line in the dark and the 3rd followed about 15 minutes behind, each man with two bandoliers of ammunition and many grenades. They went in single file along the Pantano ditch, past German posts they could see and hear. Sentries walked the bank above them more than once. The column broke in two on the way, and four of the 3rd Battalion's radio operators lost themselves. Major Miller was killed as dawn came.

At first light the head of the column was within 800 yards of Cisterna. Stewart has the alarm raised at about 06:10, when a German sentry screamed as he was knifed. The Rangers ran on through a German bivouac. Then a force led by three self-propelled guns opened fire; the Rangers knocked the guns out. As the light grew, machine guns, mortars and snipers in houses, haystacks and dugouts pinned them in the open, treeless fields. By about 07:10, Stewart says, the battalions were in a crescent-shaped hasty defence. At 07:30 the 1st Battalion broke radio silence. At about 08:30 a few Rangers reached the buildings by the railway station.

Behind them a platoon of 43 men of the 3rd Reconnaissance Troop, sent up the road, ran into a road block; one man got back. The 4th Rangers were stopped by machine guns in the farmhouses below Isola Bella. Darby sent two tank destroyers and two half-tracks to break through; two ran onto mines.

The Germans had brought fresh troops in the night before. The 2nd Parachute Lehr Battalion came up during the fight, with tanks, flak wagons and guns firing over open sights. The Rangers ran short of ammunition. Their wounded were carried back as the perimeter shrank. About noon German tanks came down from Highway 7 and drove back and forth through the positions, firing along the ditches, and split the Rangers into small groups. They answered with bazookas and sticky grenades, and Dobson counted 15 armoured vehicles burning. Paratroops then marched captured Rangers towards the perimeter; when Rangers shot two guards, the Germans bayoneted two prisoners. The surrender began to spread. Between 12:15 and 12:30 the last message came, and the line was gone.

Of 767 men in the two battalions, six came back. Most of the rest were taken. The Germans carried away the wounded and sent the others by lorry to collecting points. Lawrence Gilbert, of the 1st Battalion, remembered prisoners paraded past the Colosseum in Rome for German cameras. The 3rd Battalion, 15th Infantry, sent after the Rangers, took Isola Bella once they had surrendered.

### Where the accounts disagree
- **The clock.** None of the accounts says which clock it used. Italy and the German army both kept GMT+1 that winter (tz database). On it the sun rose at Cisterna about 07:23 and civil dawn came about 06:54 (our reckoning), later than the "first light" King gives at about 05:45.
- **The start.** Stewart has the 1st Rangers cross the start line at 01:00. The Army's *Anzio Beachhead* has them cross the west branch of the canal at about 01:30, and Laurie says they moved out at 01:30. King dates the Force command post's move to 23:15 on 30 January, a day late.
- **Who was waiting.** *Anzio Beachhead* says the Germans had brought in fresh troops the night before and had apparently found the column. Laurie says men of the 715th Motorized Infantry Division found it in the night and laid an ambush at first light. Stewart rules out an ambush: the infiltration worked, he says, and the Rangers struck a defence that was already there. King puts the failure down to intelligence; a Polish deserter who warned of the build-up was sent to the rear unheard.
- **The last message.** *Anzio Beachhead* gives 1st Sergeant Ehalt's last message at 12:30. Stewart gives 12:15 and calls him sergeant major, as does King.
- **The losses.** King, Laurie and *Anzio Beachhead* give six of 767 back and no split. Haskew gives 311 killed and 450 captured. World War II Day by Day gives 12 killed, 36 wounded and 743 captured. Stewart says more than 740 were killed or captured.
- **The date.** Wikipedia's article on the battle has the Rangers set out on 31 January; the Army histories say the night of 29/30 January.
