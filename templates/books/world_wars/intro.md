## The period's fighting {#fighting}

This book covers two wars: 1914 to 1918 and 1939 to 1945. Its battles run from the canal at
Mons in August 1914 to the jungle of Bougainville in March 1944. In both, armies of millions
fought, and most of the killing was done by guns the victims never saw.

### Armies

In 1914 France, Germany, Russia and Austria-Hungary went to war with armies of **conscripts**,
young men called up each year and kept on the reserve after their service. Britain had a small
army of regulars, the BEF of 1914, raised by voluntary enlistment. Lord Kitchener asked for
volunteers, and by January 1915, the UK Parliament's history records, over 1 million had come
forward.

Many joined with their friends. In late August 1914 General Sir Henry Rawlinson appealed to the
London stockbrokers, and they filled a battalion of the Royal Fusiliers in a week. A few days
later Lord Derby called for "a battalion of pals" in Liverpool, and 1,500 men came in two days.
These **Pals** went into action together, and were often cut down together: of an estimated 700 Accrington Pals at Serre
on 1 July 1916, 235 were killed and 350 wounded.

Volunteering could not keep up with the losses. The Military Service Act of January 1916 made
single men of 18 to 41 in Great Britain liable for service; a second act in May took in married
men. Conscription was never applied in Ireland. It raised about 2.5 million men over the war.

The empires fought with **colonial and Dominion** troops. Over 1 million Indian soldiers served
overseas, and the first Indian force reached Marseille on 30 September 1914. Marc Michel counts
about 200,000 West Africans raised by France, of whom more than 135,000 fought in Europe and
30,000 were killed. Australia sent 416,809 men, the Australian War Memorial says, and twice
voted down conscription. Newfoundland, Canada, New Zealand and South Africa sent their own.

In 1939 Britain did not wait. The National Service (Armed Forces) Act of 3 September 1939 made
men of 18 to 41 liable at once. The United States began its first peacetime draft on 16
September 1940, and over 10 million men were inducted before it ended in 1947. Germany, the
Soviet Union and Japan fought with conscripts. The Indian Army stayed volunteer: it grew from
under 200,000 men in 1939 to over 2.5 million in 1945, the largest volunteer army in history.
At Cassino in 1944 British, New Zealanders, Indians, Gurkhas, French and the 2nd Polish Corps
all fought for the same mountain.

### Arms

The infantryman of 1914 carried a **bolt-action rifle**. The British Short Magazine Lee-Enfield
held 10 rounds, loaded 5 at a time from a charger, and a trained man could fire 20 to 30 aimed
rounds in a minute. The Germans had the Mauser.

The **machine gun**, belt-fed and water-cooled, such as the British Vickers and the German MG 08,
could be served by a small team from a pit. With barbed wire in front of it, it stopped most of
the attacks of the war. But the great killer was **artillery**. Shell fragments caused about
60% of all wounds, the Imperial War Museum says. The gunners learned to fire a **creeping barrage**, a wall of shells
that moved forward in steps, 50 to 100 yards every few minutes, with the infantry close behind.
By September 1916 it was the standard British method.

Trench war brought back the **grenade**. The British adopted the Mills bomb as the No. 5 in
1915 and made over 33 million of them; its fuse burned for 7 seconds, cut to 4 after 1940. The
**trench mortar** threw a bomb high, to drop into a trench. Stokes mortars were first used in
battle at Loos on 25 September 1915.

On 22 April 1915, near Ypres, the Germans opened over 6,000 cylinders of chlorine against the
French line. This was the first great **gas** attack. Phosgene followed in December 1915 and
mustard gas, at Ypres, on 12 July 1917. Gas wounded far more men than it killed. In the American
Expeditionary Forces it caused 70,522 casualties, nearly a third of the total; 1,221 of them died.

The **tank** first went into action at Flers-Courcelette on the Somme on 15 September 1916.
Fourth Army had 49; many broke down before the start. By 4 July 1918, at Hamel, 60 tanks went in
with the Australians, and carrier tanks brought up ammunition.

The **sub-machine gun**, a light automatic firing pistol rounds, came last. The German MP 18
reached units only in mid-1918. Many accounts arm the storm troops of the spring offensive with
it, but there is no firm evidence that it reached the front so early. By 1939-45 sub-machine
guns were common in every army. The Americans went further. The **M1 Garand**, a **self-loading
rifle** with an 8-round clip, became their service rifle in 1936, and about 5.4 million were
made in the war. The British kept the bolt action, in the No. 4 rifle adopted in 1941.

The **anti-tank gun** was the infantry's answer to the tank. The British 2-pounder gave way in
1942 to the 6-pounder, first used at Gazala that May, which could pierce any enemy tank then in
service (see Snipe). Infantry also carried rocket launchers such as the American bazooka.

**Aircraft** joined the land battle. When the Germans crossed the Meuse in 1940, Stuka
dive-bombers kept the French defenders under cover. In the desert the RAF began to put a
controller with the forward troops, who called down fighter-bombers circling in a "cab rank",
and the method was refined in Italy and Normandy.

### Battles

On the Western Front each side dug a **trench system**: a front line, a support line 200 to 300
yards behind, a reserve line behind that, and communication trenches between them. In front lay
belts of wire 15 metres deep or more. German dugouts went down 4 metres and more, some three
storeys. Attacks failed for the same few reasons. The bombardment rarely cut all the wire or
reached the men in the dugouts; the eight-day bombardment before the Somme did neither. When it
lifted, the defenders came up and manned their machine guns. Where men did get in, their
supplies and guns, drawn by horses, could not follow far beyond the railheads. From 1917 the
Germans held their line in depth, with strongpoints spread over hundreds of metres.

The Germans looked for a way through. From December 1915 Captain Willy Rohr trained **storm
troops**: small groups that worked forward under each other's fire, went round strongpoints and
pushed on through the weak places. At Riga in September 1917 Georg Bruchmüller added a short,
violent bombardment. In March 1918 the German 18th Army advanced more than 50 km in under a
week, then outran its supplies and guns. The Allies' answer was the **combined-arms attack**.
At Hamel on 4 July 1918 Lieutenant-General John Monash planned for 90 minutes and took his
objectives in 93: infantry behind a creeping barrage, with tanks, and aircraft that dropped
112,000 rounds of ammunition to the forward troops.

In 1939-45 the war moved. Tanks, lorries and aircraft carried the **mobile war** through Poland
and France and across Russia. In the **desert** of North Africa there was room to manoeuvre and
little cover, and armies sowed mines: some 3 million were laid round El Alamein, and most were
never cleared. In **Italy** the Allies fought up a narrow peninsula of mountains and rivers. The
Gustav Line ran about 100 miles across it, with Monte Cassino at its heart. Few roads climbed the
mountains; supplies went up by mule and on men's backs, and on the rock the infantry built stone
sangars because they could not dig. In the **jungle** of the Pacific, as at Bougainville, men
held pillbox lines behind belts cleared out of the jungle, and disease put more men in hospital
than the enemy did.

### Wounds and medicine

**Shell wounds** were the commonest: torn, irregular wounds, driven full of dirt. The farmland of
the Western Front was rich in manure, and gas gangrene and tetanus followed.

The wounded went back by a **chain of evacuation**. Each British battalion had a medical officer
and at least 16 stretcher-bearers. They carried a man to the Regimental Aid Post near the front;
then to an Advanced Dressing Station, run by a Field Ambulance; then to a **Casualty Clearing
Station**, a tented hospital, the nearest place to the front where women nurses served. Ambulance
trains and barges took him on to the base hospitals, and hospital ships home.

A thigh bone broken by a bullet or shell fragment had killed most men who suffered it. The
**Thomas splint**, devised by Hugh Owen Thomas and brought to the army by his nephew Robert
Jones, held the leg straight and still. Introduced at the front in 1916, it cut deaths from
fractured femurs from about 80% to about 20% by 1918.

**Blood transfusion** came to the front in 1917. In the spring Lawrence Bruce Robertson, a
Canadian, set up transfusion at a casualty clearing station. Oswald Robertson, an American, kept
blood in bottles with citrate and dextrose, on ice, for up to 26 days: the first **blood bank**.
In 1939-45 the American Red Cross drew more than 13 million pints. The Americans began with
dried plasma, but the British were saving more lives with whole blood, and by 1944 whole blood
was being flown from the United States to Europe and the Pacific.

The **sulpha drugs** came first. American soldiers carried a field dressing coated with
sulphanilamide, and medics shook sulpha powder into wounds. **Penicillin** was scarce in 1943,
and every issue had to be approved, but by the landings in Normandy in June 1944 there was enough
for the Allied wounded.

Men broke as well as bled. Soldiers in the winter of 1914-15 called it **shell shock**. Charles
Myers, an army psychologist, urged that men be treated promptly, close to the front, and expected
to recover; in 1939-45 the British and Americans adopted his methods and spoke of **battle
exhaustion**. **Trench foot**, from standing for days in cold water, cost the British about
75,000 casualties and the Americans about 2,000, R. L. Atenstaedt finds. Whale oil, dry socks
and men paired to check each other's feet had cut it greatly by the winter of 1917-18.

In the Pacific the enemy was **malaria**. In 1943 alone, G. Dennis Shanks writes, over 100,000
cases were reported among American troops in the South and Southwest Pacific, at rates of up to
250 per 1,000 men a year in the Solomons and New Guinea. Atabrine tablets kept it down; a
division taken out of those islands was not fit to fight again for six months.

!!! story "The women of Pervyse"
    Elsie Knocker, a trained nurse, and Mairi Chisholm, an 18-year-old who rode a motorcycle, came
    to Belgium in September 1914 with Dr Hector Munro's Flying Ambulance Corps. They decided they
    could save more men by treating them at the front than at the end of the long road back. In
    November they set up their own first-aid post in a cellar at Pervyse, close behind the
    Belgian trenches. Knocker treated the wounded; Chisholm
    drove them to hospital, often under fire. They raised their own money. King Albert gave them
    the Order of Leopold in 1915, and the British gave them the Military Medal in 1917. A gas shell
    caught them on 17 March 1918, and the post closed. Their book, *The Cellar-House of Pervyse*,
    came out in 1917.

!!! story "The new faces of Sidcup"
    Harold Gillies, a New Zealand surgeon, saw the faces torn away by shell fragments in France
    in 1915. He began to rebuild them. He persuaded the army to open the Queen's Hospital at
    Sidcup, in Kent, for facial and jaw wounds; patients came from June 1917, and it opened
    officially on 16 August. Gillies and his team made the **tubed pedicle**: a flap of skin,
    rolled into a tube and left joined at both ends, swung from the chest or forehead to the face
    while it kept its own blood supply. Henry Tonks, a surgeon turned painter, drew the patients
    before and after. About 11,572 operations were done at Sidcup before it closed in 1925.

### Women

Women served as **nurses** in both wars: in the army's own nursing services and, from Britain, as
**VADs** of the Voluntary Aid Detachments. By 1918 the British Red Cross had over 90,000 VADs.
They had to be between 23 and 38, and served at home and abroad. In 1939-45 over 59,000 women served in the US Army Nurse Corps.

Women also joined the armies as **auxiliaries**, so that men could be sent to the front. The
Women's Army Auxiliary Corps, later Queen Mary's, began recruiting in March 1917; over 57,000
served, 17,000 of them overseas, as cooks, clerks and mechanics. The Auxiliary Territorial
Service was formed on 9 September 1938. From December 1941 unmarried British women of 20 to 30
were called up, and by VE Day the ATS had over 190,000 members. From 1941 ATS women made up
two-thirds of the mixed anti-aircraft batteries, working the instruments that aimed the guns.

In Russia in 1917 Maria Bochkareva raised a Women's Battalion, which went into action in the June
Offensive. In 1941-45 over 800,000 women served in the **Soviet forces**, most of them as medics
and nurses, but also as snipers, machine gunners, tank crew and pilots. The 588th Night Bomber
Regiment, all women, flew over 30,000 sorties.

!!! story "Lady Death"
    Lyudmila Pavlichenko was in her fourth year of history at Kyiv University. When Germany invaded in June 1941 she went
    to the recruiting office in Odessa, where they offered her nursing; she wanted the infantry.
    She became a sniper in the 25th Rifle Division and was credited with 187 kills in the siege of
    Odessa. She fought on at Sevastopol until June 1942, when mortar fragments struck her in the
    face. Her count then stood at 309, 36 of them snipers. In 1942 she toured the United States
    with Eleanor Roosevelt, the first Soviet citizen received at the White House. In Chicago she
    asked the crowd whether the men had not been "hiding behind my back for too long". She was
    made a Hero of the Soviet Union in 1943.

!!! gm "For the GM"
    Expect a world-war fight to be decided by fire, and most of it unseen. Shells and mortar bombs
    did most of the wounding, so a man in the open is in danger even when no enemy is in sight,
    and a man in a trench, a hole or a cellar is far safer. Wire and machine guns stop an attack
    in the open; a barrage, smoke, tanks or the dark get it across. In 1914-18 a wounded man's
    life depends on the stretcher-bearers and the chain behind them: the aid post, the dressing
    station, the casualty clearing station, the Thomas splint and, from 1917, blood. There are
    no antibiotics until sulpha in 1939-45 and penicillin in 1943-44. In the trenches and the
    jungle, cold water and mosquitoes will take as many men as the enemy.
