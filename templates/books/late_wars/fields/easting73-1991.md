### The campaign
Iraq invaded Kuwait in August 1990. The coalition's air campaign began on 17 January 1991, and the ground offensive followed on 24 February, with H-hour at 03:00 Saudi time by the Center of Military History's reckoning. While the Marines and Arab forces pushed into Kuwait, the US VII Corps under Lieutenant General Frederick Franks swung west of the Wadi al Batin to strike the Republican Guard from the flank. Its lead was the 2nd Armored Cavalry Regiment under Colonel Leonard D. Holder, Jr. The regiment had screened the corps' front for weeks, and on the first day it breached the border defences.

The regiment's task was to find the Republican Guard ahead of the 1st Infantry Division and the armoured divisions behind it, without becoming decisively engaged. Each of its ground squadrons had three cavalry troops; each troop, Wikipedia says after the regimental accounts, had about 120 men, nine M1A1 Abrams tanks and twelve or thirteen M3 Bradley cavalry fighting vehicles. McMaster gives his own Eagle Troop, E Troop of the 2nd Squadron, as 130 soldiers, nine M1A1s, twelve Bradleys and two 120 mm mortars.

On the morning of 26 February the regiment's limit of advance was the 60 Easting, a north-south line on the military grid. At 05:22 a corps order moved it to 70 Easting and turned the attack on the Republican Guard. Rain in the night gave way to fog and then a sandstorm that grounded the air cavalry for half the day. Across the regiment's path, along the seam with the Iraqi 12th Armored Division, lay the Tawakalna Division of the Republican Guard. The IDA reconstruction names the unit in front of the 2nd Squadron as its 18th Brigade, guarding the division's flank.

### The places
- **The Eastings**: north-south grid lines 1 km apart used as phase lines; the regiment's limits of advance were the 60, then the 70 Easting.
- **The 67 Easting**: Eagle Troop's own first limit, from which it set out east at 16:07.
- **The village**: a fortified cluster of buildings, a training-area cantonment in the IDA paper, held with anti-aircraft guns and infantry; Eagle Troop's map did not show the road through it.
- **The first rise**: the low ridge near the 70 Easting behind which an Iraqi tank company lay dug in on the reverse slope.
- **The 73 Easting ridge**: about 3 km further east, where McMaster places the Iraqi reserve of 18 T-72s and the command post.
- **The 74 Easting**: the line on which Eagle Troop halted at about 16:40.
- **The wadi at 73 Easting**: a shallow valley to the north where Ghost Troop fought for about six hours into the night.
- **Phase line COLLINS**: the line east of which the regiment advanced in the early afternoon, in the CMH account.

### The leaders
- **H. R. McMaster** (US, captain, Eagle Troop): commanded the troop from a tank in the centre of its wedge, and wrote two accounts of the fight (2016 and 2017).
- **Leonard D. "Don" Holder, Jr.** (US, colonel, 2nd Armored Cavalry Regiment): commanded the regiment.
- **Frederick M. Franks, Jr.** (US, lieutenant general, VII Corps): ordered the regiment to find the Guard but not to become decisively engaged.
- **Mike Kobbe** (US, lieutenant colonel, 2nd Squadron): McMaster's squadron commander.
- **Douglas Macgregor** (US, major, operations officer of the 2nd Squadron): rode with Eagle Troop; his tank struck a mine and was repaired on the move. He later wrote a book on the battle.
- **Craig Koch** (US, staff sergeant, McMaster's gunner): destroyed the first tank seen once the troop crested the rise, then two more.
- **Tim Gauthier** (US, lieutenant, Third Platoon): fired the first TOW into the village; later said the main fight ended in 23 minutes.
- **Joe Sartiano** (US, captain, Ghost Troop): commanded the troop to Eagle's north.
- **Major Mohammed** (Iraqi, commander of the defence in front of Eagle Troop): McMaster names him as a graduate of the Infantry Officer Advanced Course at Fort Benning; no other source read gives his full name or unit.

### The battle
Times are those of the American accounts, which give no clock for this action. The CMH gives the start of the ground war in Saudi time (GMT+3), and the accounts read as that clock. By McMaster's account, Eagle Troop moved off at 16:07 in a modified column. The scout platoons led in Bradleys, the mortars followed, and the nine tanks came behind in a wedge. At 16:07 Staff Sergeant John McReynolds' Bradley drove onto an Iraqi observation bunker and two soldiers came out to surrender. His wingman, Sergeant Maurice Harris, then came under 23 mm and machine-gun fire from the village. Gauthier fired a TOW into its centre, Koch marked it with a tank round, and all nine tanks put high explosive into it. The IDA paper has contact with dismounted Iraqis near the cantonment at 16:15.

A TOW from Staff Sergeant David Lawrence's Bradley hit what turned out to be a T-72. The tanks then crested the rise. Stars and Stripes, after the veterans, gives the time as 16:19; Wikipedia gives about 16:20. Koch reported eight T-72s in prepared positions at close range, killed the first, and then two more. The troop fought through the trenches and bunkers behind them.

While the troop was still clearing the western positions, Lieutenant John Gifford, the executive officer, reported that it had reached the 70 Easting limit. McMaster ordered the attack on, judging that a halt would give the Iraqis time to recover. The tanks crested the next rise as the reserve was starting to move, and destroyed it at close range; McMaster has this from about 16:40. Stars and Stripes says the troop halted just short of the 74 Easting at 16:40. Gauthier put the main fight at 23 minutes. The IDA paper puts the main fight at about 90 minutes, with fighting going on for six hours.

After dark the Iraqis sent a company of T-72s and BMPs at the ridge, which was destroyed at long range. To the north Ghost Troop beat off attacks in the wadi until about 21:00, its fire support team calling down 720 howitzer and rocket rounds. There a BMP-1's 73 mm round hit the turret of a Bradley whose TOW was out of action and whose cannon had jammed, and killed its gunner, Sergeant Nels A. Moller. The 1st Infantry Division passed through the regiment's lines just after 22:00.

Eagle Troop had no casualties. Its medics treated and evacuated Iraqi wounded, and guards held the prisoners, several hundred by McMaster's count, through the night; some had expected to be shot. Wikipedia gives the regiment's losses in the whole battle as 6 killed and 19 wounded, and records an I Troop Bradley hit by a TOW from K Troop, which wounded all three crewmen.

### Where the accounts disagree
- **Eagle Troop's tally.** McMaster, in both his accounts, gives about 50 T-72s, 25 armoured personnel carriers and 40 lorries. Wikipedia gives two figures: 28 tanks, 16 carriers and 30 lorries in 23 minutes, and elsewhere eight tanks in the first position and 18 at the 74 Easting. Stars and Stripes adds 13 more destroyed by a scout patrol.
- **The Iraqi position.** McMaster gives about 40 tanks and 16 BMPs, hundreds of infantry and an 18-tank reserve. The IDA reconstruction's database for the whole 2nd Squadron fight holds 55 T-72s, 33 BMP-1s, 147 dismounted men with 19 RPG-7s, and assumed 21 empty berms. Its actual Iraqi losses are 57 tanks, 28 BMPs, 11 MT-LBs and 45 lorries.
- **The Iraqi units.** The IDA paper names the 18th Armored Brigade of the Tawakalna; Wikipedia calls it the 18th Mechanized Brigade. McMaster adds elements of the 10th Armored Division. The CMH puts the fight at the seam between the Tawakalna and the 12th Armored Division.
- **How long it lasted.** Gauthier and McMaster: 23 minutes for the main fight. IDA: about 90 minutes, with fighting for six hours. The CMH has the regiment find the tanks "just after 1600" and the battle end at 17:15, yet speaks of four hours of fighting.
- **The regiment's total.** The CMH gives at least 29 tanks and 24 carriers destroyed and about 1,300 prisoners. Wikipedia, after the regimental history, gives 159 tanks, 260 other vehicles and about 2,000 prisoners.
- **Why it went so one-sidedly.** The IDA's Janus replication (1992) ran the battle again with changes. With clear weather the exchange came out about even; with thermal sights on both sides it turned about two to one in Iraq's favour. Its authors conclude that American thermal sights were a major cause of the result. They used a 400 m sight limit for the sandstorm.
- **Iraqi accounts.** No Iraqi account of this fight in English was found. McMaster's picture of the Iraqi plan is his own.
