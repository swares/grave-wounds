### The campaign
When the Soviet Union broke up, Chechnya, under its president Dzhokhar Dudayev, declared itself independent of Russia. Through 1994 Moscow backed Chechen opponents of Dudayev, and their attacks on Grozny in August, October and November failed; in November their tank columns were surrounded in the city and destroyed by men with rocket launchers. In December 1994 Russian troops moved into Chechnya. Wikipedia gives the defence minister, Pavel Grachev, as having boasted that he could take Grozny in two hours with one airborne regiment.

The force that gathered round the city was, by Oliker's account for RAND, built of conscripts and units thrown together in haste, few of whom had trained together; the army had held no divisional or regimental field exercise since 1992, and its maps were too few and of the wrong scale. Oliker describes a plan for armoured columns to drive on the city centre from the north, east and west. Wikipedia names the groups and their commanders: North under Major General Konstantin Pulikovsky, North-East under Lieutenant General Lev Rokhlin, West under General Ivan Babichev and East under Major General Nikolai Staskov, with Lieutenant General Anatoly Kvashnin over them. The ring round the city was never closed, and the south stayed open.

The Chechens had had three or four months to prepare, and they knew their city. Their chief of staff, Aslan Maskhadov, a former Soviet officer, commanded the defence; Shamil Basayev and Ruslan Gelayev led groups of fighters. Estimates of their strength run, in Oliker's words, from about 1,000 to ten times that. Before the assault the Chechen headquarters moved out to Shali.

On 31 December 1994 about 6,000 Russian soldiers, Thomas says out of some 38,000 round the city, drove into Grozny. Among them were the 131st Separate Motor Rifle Brigade from Maykop, under Colonel Ivan Savin, about 1,000 men by Thomas's count, and the 81st Guards Motor Rifle Regiment under Colonel Yaroslavtsev.

### The places
- **The railway station**: in the city centre; the 131st Brigade's 1st Battalion went into it on the afternoon of 31 December.
- **The streets round the station**: where the brigade's vehicles parked in columns, the reserve on a side street.
- **The railway line**: the way the brigade's remnants tried to break out after midnight.
- **The presidential palace**: Dudayev's seat in the city centre, the 81st Regiment's objective; it fell on 19 January 1995.
- **The city centre**: blocks of flats of five and nine storeys and older houses, where the Chechen teams waited in basements and upper floors.
- **The southern edge of the city**: never closed, so the Chechens could bring men and supplies in through the battle.
- **Shali**: the town to the south-east where the Chechen headquarters went before the assault.

### The leaders
- **Pavel Grachev** (Russian, defence minister): drew up the plan of three stages for the invasion.
- **Anatoly Kvashnin** (Russian, lieutenant general): commanded the assault force.
- **Konstantin Pulikovsky** (Russian, major general, Group North): Wikipedia places both the 131st Brigade and the 81st Regiment under him.
- **Lev Rokhlin** (Russian, lieutenant general, Group North-East): reached the centre from the north but could not reach the trapped units.
- **Ivan Savin** (Russian, colonel, 131st Separate Motor Rifle Brigade): held the station until 1 or 2 January; killed trying to bring out his wounded.
- **Yaroslavtsev** (Russian, colonel, 81st Guards Motor Rifle Regiment): led the column towards the palace; Wikipedia names Burlakov as his chief of staff.
- **Dzhokhar Dudayev** (Chechen, president): led the breakaway republic.
- **Aslan Maskhadov** (Chechen, chief of staff): planned the defence; Wikipedia gives him the plan of letting the tanks into the centre and striking at nightfall.
- **Shamil Basayev** and **Ruslan Gelayev** (Chechen, field commanders): led groups of fighters in the city.

### The battle
The times here are those the sources give; none says which clock it keeps, but Grozny kept Moscow time (GMT+3), and the sunset at about 16:30 fits. Thomas says the columns entered at noon and met no resistance at first. The vehicles drove straight to the centre. The 131st Brigade's 1st Battalion, the first unit to reach it, got down and took up positions inside the railway station, while other vehicles stood parked on a side street as a reserve. The Russian accounts summarised on Wikipedia have Russian battalions at the station by about 13:00 and close to the palace by about 15:00, almost without a fight, and the columns standing in the streets with no defence organised, no posts and no patrols out.

The Chechen fighters came in from other parts of the city and closed round the station. Oliker describes how they fought: squads of a few men with rocket launchers, machine guns and perhaps a sniper, three squads to a cell of about 25 and three cells to a unit of about 75. Teams of six or seven waited in the lower floors of buildings, keeping 50 to 250 m from the Russian columns. They knocked out the first and last vehicles in a street, and Thomas notes that the tanks' guns could neither depress far enough to reach the basements nor rise high enough to reach the roofs. Oliker says it took three to six hits to destroy a tank. Grau, for the Foreign Military Studies Office, puts the armoured columns' losses at 105 of 120 tanks and carriers.

The 81st Regiment's column, on its way to the palace, was ambushed and lost most of its armour. In the Russian accounts given on Wikipedia, the fighting round the station began at about 19:00 and went on all night, and the troops fell back into the station building, which was poor ground to defend. At about midnight the remnants of the brigade, with two tanks, tried to break out along the railway; from about 01:00 on 1 January there was no more contact with them, and they were reported surrounded and destroyed. Savin, by Thomas's account, waited for help until 2 January, and none came. Wikipedia gives his death as 2 January.

Savin tried to get his wounded out. An armoured carrier took 40 of them; it was ambushed, and 13 men lived to be taken prisoner. Grau and Thomas describe how the Russians moved their wounded in the weeks that followed: in BTR-80 carriers used as ambulances, because the Chechens fired on soft-skinned ones, to aid posts and hospitals dug in or in basements, since those were shelled too. Snipers often kept the wounded from being moved until dark. They write that most of the wounds came from mortars, that burns were more common than in other wars, and that most of the killed were hit in the head or chest by snipers. One participant, Oliker says, put up to 60 per cent of the Russian casualties down to their own side's fire.

By 3 January the brigade had lost most of its men and vehicles. The Russians fell back, brought up artillery and took the city block by block. They took the palace on 19 January. The Chechens left Grozny in February and March.

### Where the accounts disagree
- **The 131st Brigade's losses.** Thomas says that by 3 January it had lost nearly 800 men, 20 of its 26 tanks and 102 of its 120 armoured vehicles. Wikipedia's main article gives about 789 killed and 75 captured. Its article on the palace assault cites Lukin for 189 killed, captured or missing, with the same 20 tanks and 102 vehicles and all six Tunguska anti-aircraft vehicles. Its article on the brigade, after *Krasnaya Zvezda* (2006), gives 85 killed and 72 missing, 157 in all, and 22 T-72 tanks, 45 BMP-2s and 37 other vehicles lost.
- **The Russian losses in the New Year assault.** Carlotta Gall, in the *New York Times* as Wikipedia cites her, put them at least 1,500 and perhaps 2,000 over sixty hours. Oliker says only that they fell by the hundreds.
- **The armour.** Grau gives 105 of 120 tanks and carriers lost in the first columns. Wikipedia gives General Galkin's count of 225 armoured vehicles past repair, 62 of them tanks, in the first six weeks; Maskhadov claimed about 400.
- **The Chechen strength.** Oliker gives estimates from about 1,000 to about 10,000; Grau says about 12,000 fought in Grozny over the whole battle; the Russian account on Wikipedia has up to 3,500 men, 50 guns and tanks and 300 grenade launchers gathered against the centre.
- **The Chechen losses.** Wikipedia gives Russian official figures of 6,690 Chechen fighters killed between December 1994 and April 1995, and Western estimates in the low thousands for the whole battle. No source read gives a figure for the night at the station.
- **Civilians.** Estimates of the civilians killed in Grozny vary widely: Anatol Lieven put them at about 5,000, Sergei Kovalev, President Yeltsin's human rights adviser, at about 27,000 in the five weeks of fighting, many of them ethnic Russians, and the World Peace Foundation at 25,000 to 30,000 by mid-February 1995, as Wikipedia gives them.
- **When the fighting began.** Thomas has the column cut off soon after it arrived; the Russian account on Wikipedia has the fighting begin at about 19:00. Our game starts it at dusk, about 16:30.
- **The end at the station.** The Russian account on Wikipedia has the breakout along the railway at about midnight and contact lost at 01:00 on 1 January. Thomas has Savin waiting for help until 2 January, and Wikipedia gives his death as 2 January. Our game has the survivors break out at about 18:00 on 1 January.
- **Who went to the station.** Oliker gives the station to the force from the west. Wikipedia puts the 131st Brigade in Group North. One passage on Wikipedia has a battalion of the 81st Regiment at the station by 13:00; most accounts send the 81st to the palace.
- **The total Russian losses.** Wikipedia's infobox gives the official figure for the whole battle as 1,834 killed, 4,670 wounded and 96 captured; its text gives 1,376 killed and 408 missing; Western estimates run to more than 5,000 killed.
