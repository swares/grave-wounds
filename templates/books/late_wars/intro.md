## The period's fighting {#fighting}

This book covers the late wars of the twentieth century and the first of the twenty-first: the
Falklands in 1982, the Gulf in 1991, Mogadishu in 1993, Grozny in 1994-95, and Iraq and Afghanistan
from 2001 to 2014. Its battles run from Goose Green in May 1982 to Combat Outpost Keating in October
2009, and the game also has a table for Northern Ireland. Some of these wars were over in weeks.
Others went on for years against an enemy who rarely stood to fight. In all of them a wounded soldier
on the winning side had a better chance of living than in any war before.

### Armies

The British and the Americans fought these wars with **volunteers**. The last British national
serviceman was discharged to the reserve on 14 June 1963, the National Army Museum records, so the
paratroopers and Royal Marines of 1982 were all regulars. The United States had ended the draft in
1973.

Not all of them served full time. The **National Guard** and the reserves were called up for Iraq and
Afghanistan as never before. For much of 2005, the Guard Bureau said, the Army Guard provided half of
the Army's combat forces on the ground in Iraq. In February 2006 General Peter Pace told the Senate
that the Guard and reserves made up about 30% of the American forces in Iraq and Afghanistan. A Guard
military police company from Kentucky fought the action at Salman Pak told below.

Argentina and Russia sent **conscripts**. The Argentine Army of 1982 had about 60,000 men, of whom
20,000 were regular officers and NCOs; most of the ranks were conscripts serving a single year. More
than 10,000 soldiers went to the islands. Corbacho finds that some small Army units and the Navy's 5th
Marine Battalion fought well, where officers, NCOs and conscripts held together. The Russian force
that drove into Grozny on New Year's Eve 1994 was, in Oliker's words, made of conscripts and ad hoc
units put together in haste. The army had held no divisional or regimental field exercise since 1992,
and only five or six of the 151 hours of tactical training required before battle dealt with
fighting in towns.

Against the regular armies stood **insurgents and militias**. The Chechens fought in small teams,
Oliker writes: typically two men with RPG-7 or RPG-18 rocket launchers, two machine gunners and
perhaps a sniper. Three such teams made a cell of about 25, and three cells a unit of about 75. They
let the Russian columns in, then fought them at close range from cellars and the lower floors of
buildings. The Somali militia in Mogadishu, the insurgents of Fallujah and the fighters who attacked
Wanat and Keating fought in much the same way, from houses or hillsides, and wore no armour.

### Arms

The British went to the Falklands with the 7.62 mm **Self-Loading Rifle** and the general-purpose
machine gun, the GPMG; the Argentines carried the FN FAL, a rifle of the same family. In 1985 Britain
accepted the 5.56 mm **SA80**. In the Gulf in 1991 it showed many faults: weak magazine springs, a
magazine catch easily knocked, fragile firing pins. Heckler & Koch rebuilt about 200,000 of them from
2000, and the first rebuilt rifles went to Afghanistan in December 2001. The Americans kept the
5.56 mm M16 family. Against both, the Iraqis, Chechens, Somalis and Afghans carried the
**Kalashnikov**.

American squads carried the **SAW**, the M249 Squad Automatic Weapon, adopted in February 1982 and
issued to the Army from 1984 and the Marines from 1985. It fired the rifle's round from a belt at up
to 850 rounds a minute. The British later gave the same gun, the Minimi, the machine-gun role in the
section.

The **RPG**, the Soviet rocket-propelled grenade, was carried by every irregular army in this book.
The Chechen teams had two each, and an RPG brought down the Black Hawk Super 61 in Mogadishu.

The Western armies had **night-vision goggles** and **thermal sights**, which see heat rather than
light. In the sandstorm at 73 Easting in 1991, one American commander writes, the Iraqi tanks showed
in his thermal sight only as hot spots, dug in with just their turrets showing. Nine M1A1 tanks and
their Bradleys destroyed the position in about 23 minutes without loss. Armour was not safe in a
town. In Grozny, Oliker writes, the Chechens halted Russian columns by knocking out the first and last
vehicles, then fired down on the rest.

In Iraq and Afghanistan the weapon that wounded most was the **improvised explosive device**, the
IED: a home-made bomb hidden by a road. Explosions caused 78% of American combat wounds from 2001 to
2005, Owens and colleagues found, more than in any large war before. The game's tables give the IED
about 60% of all wounds.

Above it all flew **precision weapons**. In the Gulf only 8% of the tonnage dropped was guided, the
US General Accounting Office found, though guided bombs were 84% of the cost. On the first night of
the Afghan war, 7 October 2001, a Predator, a remotely piloted aircraft or **drone**, fired the first
missile ever launched in combat from one; its crew sat in Virginia.

### Battles

Some of these wars were **short wars of manoeuvre**. The Falklands war lasted 74 days, from the
Argentine landing on 2 April 1982 to the surrender on 14 June. It cost 255 British and 649 Argentine
lives. The Gulf war of 1991 was a 43-day air campaign followed by a ground war of 100 hours, in which
American and British armour drove across open desert, as at 73 Easting.

Others were **city fights**. In Mogadishu on 3 October 1993 a raid by Task Force Ranger turned into a
night-long fight for two crashed helicopters; 18 Americans died. In Grozny the Russian columns drove
into the city on 31 December 1994 and lost most of their armour in its streets. At Fallujah in
November 2004 American Marines and soldiers took the city house by house, with tanks firing into the
buildings ahead of them.

Most of the fighting was at **outposts and in counter-insurgency**: patrols, checkpoints and convoys,
the roadside bomb and the sniper, with a battle now and then when the enemy chose. Northern Ireland
was the longest of these. The British Army's Operation Banner ran from 14 August 1969 to 31 July
2007, with about 21,000 troops there at its peak in the 1970s. The Ministry of Defence counts 1,441
service deaths; 722 of them were killed by paramilitaries. The army itself killed some 301 to 307
people, about half of them civilians. In eastern Afghanistan the Americans set small posts in the
valleys, close to the villages. At Wanat in July 2008 and at Keating in October 2009 some 200 and 300
fighters attacked them from the hills at dawn.

### Wounds and medicine

Soldiers wore more armour than ever before, and it stopped more. The Rangers in Mogadishu wore solid
plates over the chest. No bullet or fragment went through those plates, Mabry and colleagues found,
and chest wounds caused only 14% of their deaths from penetrating wounds, against 39% in Vietnam. But
36% of those deaths came from head wounds, all of them through places the helmet did not cover. From
2000 the Americans issued the **Interceptor** vest, with plates front and back rated to stop rifle
bullets. Owens's figures for 2001-05 show the result. Wounds of the chest fell to 6%, against 13% in
the Second World War and Vietnam; wounds of the head and neck rose to a larger share than in the
Second World War, Korea or Vietnam; and 54% of wounds were to the **limbs**.

The **tourniquet** came back. It had long had a bad name, and in 2001 it was all but absent from
American practice. Yet in Vietnam, Maughon found, 193 of 2,600 men killed in action had bled to death
from a wound to a limb alone. In 1996 Butler, Hagmann and Butler published **Tactical Combat Casualty
Care**, rules for care under fire written for special operations, built round the commonest causes
of death that could be prevented. Its later guidelines called for the tourniquet to be used freely.

!!! story "The tourniquet comes back"
    In the summer of 2004 the US Army Institute of Surgical Research in San Antonio tested nine
    tourniquets on 18 volunteers. Three worked best. The researchers chose the Combat Application
    Tourniquet, the CAT, because it was the quickest to learn: a strap tightened by a windlass and
    locked, which a man could put on his own arm with one hand. In January 2005 Central Command
    ordered its soldiers to carry one, and in March the Army Surgeon General had one issued to
    every soldier going to Iraq or Afghanistan. In Baghdad in 2006 John Kragh counted 232
    casualties with tourniquets: 87% lived, and none of the five who needed one and did not get
    it. "There is no pre-hospital device deployed in this war that has saved more lives," said
    Colonel John Holcomb.

Speed mattered as much as the tourniquet. Forward surgical teams of about 20 people, Gawande wrote,
could set up four beds and two operating tables within an hour, close behind the fighting. In 2009
the Pentagon ordered that a critically wounded man in Afghanistan reach a surgeon within the **golden
hour**. Kotwal and colleagues found that the median helicopter flight fell from 90 minutes to 43, and
the share of wounded who died fell from 13.7% to 7.6%; they put the lives saved at nearly 360.

From the combat hospital the chain ran by C-17 aircraft, with a doctor, nurse and respiratory
therapist aboard for the worst cases, to Ramstein air base in Germany and the **Landstuhl** Regional
Medical Center, a short drive away. Landstuhl treated nearly 66,000 patients from Iraq and
Afghanistan from 2004 to 2012, about 14,000 of them with battle injuries. Gawande compared the 45
days a wounded man took to reach home from Vietnam with less than four.

!!! story "The Red and Green Life Machine"
    On 22 May 1982 the British opened a field hospital at Ajax Bay, on San Carlos Water, in an old
    refrigeration plant built to freeze mutton. It stood beside an ammunition dump, and Brigadier
    Julian Thompson would not have a red cross painted on the roof. Two Argentine bombs lodged in
    it and did not go off; the surgeons worked on beneath them. Surgeon Commander Rick Jolly ran
    it, and Sea Kings brought the worst wounded in from the hills. The RAMC Association counts 580
    British and Argentine patients; Thompson gave more than 650, and about 210 operations. Three
    died of their wounds, but none in its care. Its name, the Red and Green Life Machine, is said
    to come from the Paras' red berets and the Marines' green, or from its care for both armies.

The **blast** of the IED brought its own wounds. The Defense Department counted 383,938 first
diagnoses of **traumatic brain injury** in American service members from 2000 to early 2018, 82% of
them mild; the count includes injuries in training and at home. It counted 1,705 major
**amputations** from the wars of 2001-17. Up to July 2011, Krueger and colleagues found, about 30% of
amputees had lost more than one limb.

All this saved more of the wounded than in any war before. Gawande put the share who died at under
30% in the Second World War and about a quarter in Korea, Vietnam and the Gulf; in Iraq, he wrote,
more than 90% of those hit were saved. Speed still depended on the fight: in Mogadishu medics cared
for 39 casualties for more than 14 hours before they could be taken out.

### Women

Women went further forward in these wars than ever before, though the rules still kept them out of
the infantry and armour. No British servicewomen fought ashore in the Falklands. In the Gulf in 1991
the Americans sent between 33,000 and 41,000 women, by different counts. They flew helicopters, drove
trucks, fired Patriot missiles and commanded companies. Thirteen died. Two were taken prisoner: Major
Rhonda Cornum, a flight surgeon, and Melissa Rathbun-Nealy, an Army truck driver.

In Iraq the Americans needed women to search Iraqi women. Women were doing it in Ramadi in 2003-04,
the first of the **Lioness** teams; in 2004-05 the Marines made the scheme formal, with volunteers who
were mechanics, fuel handlers and clerks. On 23 June 2005 a suicide car bomb in Fallujah hit a truck
carrying Lionesses back from the checkpoints. Six died, three of them women, and of 13 wounded, 11
were women. In Afghanistan from about 2009 the Marines formed **Female Engagement Teams**: two or
three women attached to an infantry unit, living and patrolling with it, to talk with the women of
the villages. They were barred from offensive patrols. The rule that kept women out of ground combat
units was lifted on 24 January 2013.

!!! story "Raven 42"
    On 20 March 2005 about 50 insurgents ambushed a supply convoy near Salman Pak, south of
    Baghdad. Its escort was Raven 42, a squad of the 617th Military Police Company, Kentucky Army
    National Guard, in three Humvees. Sergeant Leigh Ann Hester, a team leader, took her team into
    a flanking position. With her squad leader, Staff Sergeant Timothy Nein, she attacked a trench
    line with grenades and grenade-launcher rounds and cleared two trenches; she killed three men
    with her rifle. After about 30 minutes 27 insurgents were dead; none of the squad was killed.
    On 16 June 2005 Hester received the Silver Star, the first woman awarded it since the Second
    World War and the first ever for close combat. Nein and Specialist Jason Mike, the medic,
    received it too.

On the other side the record is thinner. Among Russian troops in Chechnya a story spread of the
"White Tights", women snipers from the Baltic states hired by the Chechens. Regamey, who traced it,
finds no evidence they existed; women taken in Chechnya were often labelled snipers whatever they had
done. The Kurds were different. The PKK formed its first women's guerrilla units in 1995, and in
Syria the Women's Protection Units, the YPJ, founded on 4 April 2013, fought in the defence of Kobanî
in 2014, with American aircraft striking for them from October.

!!! gm "For the GM"
    Expect a fight in these wars to be short and lopsided in the open, and long and close in a town
    or a valley. A Western soldier has armour on his chest, a tourniquet in his pocket, a medic
    nearby and, after 2009 in Afghanistan, a helicopter within the hour: hits to the chest are
    rarer, and a man hit in the leg usually lives if the bleeding is stopped quickly. His face,
    neck, arms and legs are bare, and the roadside bomb takes legs. The Argentine and Russian
    conscript has a steel helmet and little else, and the Chechen, Somali or Afghan fighter has
    nothing; their wounded must be carried. In Mogadishu, Grozny and Fallujah, the wounded may wait
    hours or a night before anyone can reach them.
