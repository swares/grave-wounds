## The period's fighting {#fighting}

The battles in this book run from 1179 to 1499. Most of what armies did in those years was not
battle at all. It was marching, raiding, besieging and waiting.

### Armies

Until the very end of the period, men were raised for a campaign and sent home after it. A great lord brought his **retinue**: his household knights, squires and
servants, and the men who held land from him. In England from the 1340s most of this was done
by **indenture**, a written contract. A captain agreed to bring so many men-at-arms and so many
archers, for so long, at set daily wages. In 1415 most contracts asked for three archers to
every man-at-arms.

Below the retinues were the **levies**: townsmen and peasants called out to defend their own
country. They were the Gotlanders at Visby and the Swiss cantons' men. Beside them served
**mercenaries**, hired by the company: Genoese crossbowmen in French pay, English archers in
Portugal, and by 1499 the German Landsknechte. In the Latin East and the Baltic the **military
orders** kept the nearest thing to a professional army. Templars, Hospitallers and Teutonic
Knights were monks under vows who fought for life.

### Arms and armour

In 1180 a knight wore **mail**, a shirt of riveted iron rings, over a padded coat. Mail turned
a cut well and a thrust less well. Through the 1300s plates were added: first at the knees and
elbows, then a breastplate under cloth. By about 1420 a rich man-at-arms wore a full harness of
plate and needed no shield. A poorer man wore a **jack**, a coat of many layers of linen, and an
iron hat.

The missile weapons changed the battle. The **crossbow** was powerful and easy to learn, but slow.
The English **longbow** shot perhaps 10 arrows a minute, and in massed bands it broke French
charges at Crécy and Agincourt. The Swiss fought with the **halberd**, an axe-blade and spike on
a long shaft. From the 1420s they relied more on the **pike**, about 18 feet (5.5 m) long. Their
dense squares could stand against horse and push other foot off the field.

**Guns** appear in European pictures and records from the 1320s. For a century they were noisy,
slow and few. By the 1450s, though, the French royal artillery was knocking down English-held
castles in Normandy in weeks.

### Battles

Battles were rare, because one could lose a war in an afternoon. Most were short. They were decided by nerve, not
by killing. When one side's line broke, its men ran, and the enemy cut them down from behind. Far
more men died in the rout than in the fighting before it. The crusader graves at Sidon (1253) hold
men cut down from behind, perhaps as they fled, and some perhaps beheaded after capture.

The English learned to fight on foot. Their **men-at-arms dismounted** and stood in line with the
archers on their flanks or in front. They dug pits, set sharpened stakes, or chose ground behind
a hedge or a slope. The enemy had to come to them through the arrows. The Portuguese and English
at Aljubarrota fought the same way, behind a ditch and felled trees.

### Sieges and raids

Most war was the taking and holding of strong places. A castle or walled town was **besieged**:
blockaded, battered with stone-throwers, undermined, or stormed with ladders and towers. Many fell
to hunger or a bargain, not an assault. A garrison that surrendered on terms usually walked out.
One taken by storm could expect no mercy.

The other half of war was the **raid**. An English *chevauchée* burned and looted its way across
France to ruin the enemy's lands and shame his king.

!!! story "Women at the walls"
    On 25 June 1218 Simon de Montfort, leader of the crusade against the south, was besieging
    Toulouse. The townsmen sallied to burn his siege shelter, and he came up to drive them back.
    A stone from an engine on the walls struck him on the helmet and killed him. The *Song of
    the Cathar Wars* says the engine was worked by "ladies and girls and men's wives". A century
    later, in Scotland, Agnes, Countess of Dunbar, held Dunbar Castle against the Earl of
    Salisbury from January to June 1338 while her husband was away. Later Scottish tellings have
    her maids dusting the walls with cloths where the English stones had struck; that part is
    tradition.

### Ransom and quarter

A knight was worth more alive. A captured man of rank could be held for **ransom**, often about
a year's income or more, and the captor's lord took a share. King John II of France, taken at
Poitiers in 1356, was ransomed for 3 million gold crowns. Common soldiers had less to offer and
were more often killed. Quarter was a custom, not a right. Henry V had French prisoners killed at
Agincourt when he feared a new attack. In 1499 the Swiss swore to take no prisoners at all.

### Wounds

The mass graves at Visby (1361) and Towton (1461) show what the weapons did. At Visby most
wounds were to the legs and the head, more on the left side than the right. At Towton most were
to the head, and many skulls bear several blows.

A wounded man's chances depended on reaching help. Great lords had **surgeons**; the rank and file
had **barber-surgeons**, or a comrade. They drew out arrows, stitched and bound wounds, and set
bones. In 1403 John Bradmore drew an arrowhead from deep in the face of
the young Prince Henry, cleaned the wound with wine and honey, and saved him. But no one knew what
caused **infection**. Men who survived the field often died days later of fever and gangrene. A
pierced belly or a deep wound to the chest was almost always fatal.

!!! gm "For the GM"
    Expect a medieval fight to be short and decided by nerve. Most men stop when they are
    hurt: the stop check and the side's morale roll end a skirmish within a minute or two.
    On the field map a melee takes ten or twenty minutes, and the side that breaks loses most
    of its men in the rout and the pursuit. Armour matters: it takes a wound down a step, a
    thrust beats mail better than a cut, and a war bow's arrows go through mail and a jack.
    Against plate, aim for a gap. A knight is worth taking alive. After the fight, wound
    fever kills many who lived through it; a camp and a good leech are the only help.
