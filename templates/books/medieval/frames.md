## Why the team marches {#frames}

Four reasons for a medieval team to be on the road. A frame adds no rules: it says who pays, what the
team is after and what ends it, and uses the [patrons](rules.html#patrons), [pay](rules.html#rank) and
[work](rules.html#work) in the Rules. The history in each frame is sourced below. The goals, first jobs
and complications are ours, written for play.

### A lord's retinue {#frame-retinue}

From the 1340s an English captain served by **indenture**, a written contract. Thomas Tunstall's of
April 1415 bound him to serve a whole year from the muster with 6 men-at-arms and 18 mounted archers,
at 12d a day for a man-at-arms and 6d for an archer in France. For the second quarter's wages the king handed over jewels in
pledge. If no acceptable surety came by mid-quarter, Tunstall was discharged at the quarter's end. The
king took a third of his gains, and a third of a third of what his men won.

- **Who pays:** the captain, by the day and by rank, when the Crown pays him.
- **The goal:** serve out the term and rise in your patron's Standing.
- **What ends it:** the term runs out, the pay stops, or your patron falls.
- **First job:** the muster, and the march to the army.
- **What goes wrong:** the quarter's pay arrives as a promise. A rich prisoner is claimed by the king. Your thirds are disputed.
- **Maps:** Crécy, Agincourt, Towton.

### A free company {#frame-company}

When a war paused, its soldiers did not always go home. Robert Knolles, probably an archer by origin,
led a Great Company of 2,000 to 3,000 men in 1358. The Grand Company devastated Romagna, Umbria and
Tuscany. In Italy such captains served by contract, the *condotta*, and had a name for changing sides.

- **Who pays:** whoever hires you, and, between contracts, the country you stand in.
- **The goal:** a good contract, or enough plunder to leave the trade.
- **What ends it:** the company breaks up, its leader is bought, or it is hunted down.
- **First job:** a town wants its road kept clear, and cannot afford you for long.
- **What goes wrong:** the paymaster fails, and the men want to take what they are owed. A rival company bids for your men. The country has nothing left to take.
- **Maps:** Aljubarrota, Dornach.

### A levy defending home {#frame-levy}

Gotland's farmers met Valdemar IV outside Visby in July 1361 in six units, one for each of the
island's *settings*. A Visby record counts 1,800 of them, and a Lübeck chronicle calls them unarmed and
unused to battle. The town kept its gates locked, and surrendered two days later.

- **Who pays:** no one. You eat what you brought.
- **The goal:** get the enemy off your land and be home for the harvest.
- **What ends it:** the enemy leaves, or you are beaten, or the harvest will not wait.
- **First job:** the call goes round, and the parish must decide who goes.
- **What goes wrong:** the town behind you shuts its gates. Men drift home to their fields. Your neighbour's farm burns, and he wants to go now.
- **Maps:** Gotland, 1361.

### A vow in the East {#frame-vow}

A crusader's vow was a binding promise to God. A man who failed to try to keep it could be
excommunicated. It might be redeemed by a payment, and a crusader could mortgage land that could not
otherwise be sold, and put off his debts while he served. Crusade bulls sometimes set one to three
years of service. Louis IX stayed in the Holy Land until 1254, refortified Acre, Caesarea, Jaffa and
Sidon at his own expense, and left 100 French knights in garrison at Acre.

- **Who pays:** your own purse first, then a lord or a king, if you find one.
- **The goal:** keep the vow and get home alive.
- **What ends it:** the vow is kept, or the money runs out, or the fever takes you.
- **First job:** a garrison needs men, and pays by the month.
- **What goes wrong:** the mortgage at home falls due. Your lord dies, and his pay with him. The sick outnumber the fit.
- **Maps:** Jacob's Ford, Sidon.
