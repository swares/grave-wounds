### The campaign
In 1499 the Emperor Maximilian I tried to bind the Empire closer together, with a common tax and an imperial supreme court. The Swiss Confederates refused both. The quarrel became the Swabian War, mostly border raiding along the Rhine from the Grisons to Basel.

Solothurn had bought the castle of Dorneck in 1490. It stood above the Birs, on the roads south towards Solothurn, Bern and the Jura passes. In May and June Count Heinrich von Fürstenberg gathered an imperial army in the Sundgau to take it. He waited for more horses, and the Swiss had time to gather.

On 19 July the army came up the Birs valley and took the bridge at Dornachbrugg. By the evening of 21 July it was camped below the castle, between Dornach and Arlesheim, with the guns set up to batter the walls. The castle's warden lowered a messenger over the wall that night. He slipped past the sentries and ran over the Gempen to the Confederates at Liestal.

### The places
- **Dorneck castle**: Solothurn's castle on its spur above Dornach, held by about 20 men and besieged from 21 July.
- **Dornach** and **Arlesheim**: the villages at the southern and northern ends of the imperial camp.
- **The fields below Dorneck**: the open ground between Dornach and Arlesheim where the camp stood and the battle was fought.
- **The Birs and Dornachbrugg**: the river behind the camp and its bridge. The rout went over it.
- **Reinach**: the village across the Birs where the Welsche Garde, the Burgundian horse, camped.
- **Gempen and the Gempenstollen**: the wooded heights above the camp. The Swiss gathered here from 20 July and watched the camp on the 22nd.
- **Liestal**: where the Confederates assembled, and where the castle's messenger ran.
- **Basel**: not yet a member of the Confederation. It forbade its people to take part, but men from the upper Basel country joined the Swiss anyway.

### The leaders
- **Niklaus Conrad** (Confederates, Solothurn): led the vanguard. Winkler has him watching the camp from the Schartenfluh before the attack.
- **Benedikt Hugi** (Confederates, Solothurn): warden of Dorneck. He held the unfinished castle and sent the messenger to Liestal.
- **Rudolf von Erlach** (Confederates, Bern): born 1448, a Bernese commander who had fought in the Hegau earlier in the war. He led Bern's main body, and Leutenegger names him overall commander.
- **Kaspar von Stein** and **Kaspar Göldli** (Confederates): led Bern's 500-man reinforcement and Zürich's 400.
- **Petermann Feer** and **Werner Steiner** (Confederates): led the Lucerne and Zug men who came up at the end.
- **Heinrich von Fürstenberg** (Imperial, commander): Maximilian's field marshal. He rode towards the noise of the attack, was pulled from his horse and killed.
- **Louis de Vaudrey** (Imperial, the Welsche Garde): captain of the Burgundian horse. His captured banner is in the Museum Altes Zeughaus in Solothurn.
- **Maximilian I** (Imperial, King of the Romans): not at Dornach. He heard the news at Überlingen.

### The battle
On the morning of 22 July a single imperial gun fired at the castle. Most of the army was still in its tents. Bernese and Solothurners on the Gempenstollen could see the whole camp, but were too few to attack. They waited for Zürich and Bern's main body. The council of war then chose to go in without Lucerne and Zug, still on the march.

Solothurners who knew the ground led them down through the wood. They came out in the middle of the afternoon: "in der vierden stund nach mittag", about 16:00, by the chronicles. The vanguard split. A small party took some field guns. Conrad's larger body took the siege guns and pushed through the camp. Many of the imperial men, the Welsche Garde among them, fled over the Birs. Fürstenberg was killed in the first fight.

The Welsche Garde came back and drove the vanguard towards the wood and the castle. Bern's main body, slowed by the undergrowth, came out of the trees and rescued it. The Landsknechte had formed a large pike block, and the two infantries met on the fields. The imperial guns were laid high on the castle and could not be turned. The Swiss guns had been left behind on the heights. For hours the fight swayed back and forth. A rise on the field was later called the Blood Hill.

About 19:00 some 1,000 men of Lucerne and Zug came out on the Arlesheim side, with horns and shouting. The Swiss attacked again. The tired Landsknechte broke. Most of the imperial dead fell in the flight to the Birs: the Swiss killed whoever they caught, and few prisoners were taken. In the dark some Swiss fired on their own men.

The Swiss took the camp, the war chest, 21 guns and 9 banners. They held the field for some days. The booty was shared among the wounded and the widows and orphans of the dead. On 22 September 1499 the Peace of Basel ended the war.

### Where the accounts disagree
- **The Swiss.** Winkler counts over 5,000 at the start: 1,500 from Solothurn, 3,000 from Bern and 400 from Zürich, with 1,000 from Lucerne and Zug to come. The German Wikipedia gives about 7,200 in all; the English, about 5,000 or 6,000.
- **The imperial army.** Winkler gives 10,000-15,000, including more than 2,000 Landsknechte from Gelderland. The German Wikipedia gives about 10,000, the English 15,000-16,000.
- **The losses.** Most give about 3,000 imperial and 500 Swiss dead; the English Wikipedia 4,000 and 600-900.
- **Days on the field.** Schilling has the Swiss stay three days; Winkler, six.
- **Where the leaders lie.** A plaque in St. Mauritius at Dornach says Fürstenberg and two other lords are buried there (Leutenegger; the Swiss Army monument inventory). Winkler, from a letter of Bern's captains, has Fürstenberg's body taken to the church at Arlesheim. Perhaps it went first to Arlesheim.
- **The Welsche Garde.** Winkler counts about 1,100, but says only about 400 stayed on the field, mostly looting.
- **The reply over the nobles' bodies.** *Die Gartenlaube* (1899) has "Die Edlen müssen bei den Bauern bleiben"; the saying linked with the ossuary today has "liegen". The German Wikipedia says only that this "is supposed to have" been the answer.

!!! story "The nobles must lie with the peasants"
    The day after the battle, monks and envoys came out from Basel. The noble families wanted the
    bodies of their counts and knights, to bury them at home. The Swiss refused. Their answer, as
    later told, was "Die Edlen müssen bei den Bauern liegen": the nobles must lie with the peasants.
    A letter from Bern's captains says much the same; only a few lords were buried in a church.
    The rest were left in the open. When Christine Cooper studied about 105 skulls from the ossuary
    in 2008, she found weathering and the gnawing of animals: the dead had lain out a long time,
    perhaps for years. At least 25 skulls show blows struck after the men were down.
