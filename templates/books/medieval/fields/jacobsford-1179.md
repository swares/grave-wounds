### The campaign
In October 1178 King Baldwin IV of Jerusalem began a castle at Jacob's Ford on the upper Jordan.
The ford was the crossing on the road from Tiberias towards Quneitra and Damascus. William of Tyre
says the work took six months, and that the castle was a square with very thick walls. It was handed
to the Templars, who already held Safed nearby.

Saladin saw the castle as a threat on his frontier. A lost Arabic source preserved by Abu Shama says
he offered the Franks 60,000 dinars to pull it down, then raised the offer to 100,000. They refused.
Taqi al-Din told him to spend the money on troops instead.

The spring and summer of 1179 went badly for the Franks. On 10 April Saladin's nephew Farrukh-Shah
beat Baldwin's army near Banias, and the constable, Humphrey of Toron, was mortally wounded. On
10 June Saladin routed the Franks at Marj Ayyun and took the Templar master, Odo of St Amand,
prisoner. In late August he came to the ford with his army. Baldwin gathered a relief force at
Tiberias, about half a day's march to the south.

### The places
- **Chastellet (the castle at Jacob's Ford)**: the Templar castle on a low rise west of the ford.
  Only its outer wall was finished. Excavation found one square tower at the south-west corner, a
  gate in the south wall and a postern on the east.
- **Jacob's Ford**: the crossing of the Jordan between Lake Hula and the Sea of Galilee. Arab writers
  called the place Bait al-Ahzan.
- **Tiberias**: on the Sea of Galilee, where Baldwin assembled the relief army in August 1179.
- **Safed**: a Templar castle in the hills to the west. Saladin's men cut timber in its district to
  shield their stone-throwers. Imad al-Din called it "a nest of evil".
- **Banias**: the Ayyubid town north of the Hula. Near it, at Belfort, Farrukh-Shah beat Baldwin on
  10 April 1179.
- **Hunin (Chastel Neuf)**: Humphrey of Toron's castle, where he died of his wounds in April 1179.
- **Quneitra**: on the Golan, on the road from the ford to Damascus.

### The leaders
- **Saladin** (Ayyubid, sultan): commanded the siege in person and had the castle razed afterwards.
  His secretaries wrote the fullest accounts of it.
- **Farrukh-Shah** (Ayyubid, Saladin's nephew): won the fight near Banias in April 1179 and later
  governed Damascus for his uncle. One Arabic account has him leading attacks on the castle from
  several sides.
- **Taqi al-Din** (Ayyubid, adviser): urged Saladin to use the money offered for the castle on
  soldiers.
- **Al-Qadi al-Fadil** (Ayyubid, Saladin's chief secretary): his letters give the garrison's
  strength, the wall's width and the commander's death.
- **Baldwin IV** (Kingdom of Jerusalem, king): a teenager, already gravely ill. At Marj Ayyun he could
  not ride and a knight carried him to safety. His relief army arrived too late.
- **Odo of St Amand** (Templars, master): oversaw the building. Captured at Marj Ayyun, he died in
  one of Saladin's prisons in 1180.
- **The castellan** (Templars, commander): his name is not known. Al-Fadil says he threw himself
  into the fire as the castle fell.
- **Humphrey II of Toron** (Kingdom of Jerusalem, constable): wounded near Banias in April 1179; he
  died soon after at Hunin.

### The battle
Saladin arrived at the ford on a Saturday. His men took the outer compound on the first day. Ibn
al-Athir credits a man in a torn tunic with climbing the wall first.

Saladin set up stone-throwers behind timber palisades. His archers shot at the walls from both
sides; hundreds of arrowheads have been found on the east and west fronts. Meanwhile his miners dug
under the north-east corner. Imad al-Din says the mine was 30 cubits deep, under a wall 9 cubits
thick.

The miners propped the tunnel with timber and fired it, but the wall stood. Saladin then paid a
dinar for every goatskin of water brought to put the fire out, so the sappers could go back in and
dig deeper. News of the Frankish army at Tiberias hurried the work.

At daybreak on Thursday the second fire brought the corner down. Behind it the Franks had raised a
timber wall; it caught fire in the wind, and the fire spread to their tents. The castle was stormed.
Al-Fadil says the commander "threw himself into a hole full of fire". About 700 prisoners were
taken. Imad al-Din says the volunteers killed most of them on the road. Saladin had the apostates
and the bowmen put to death and sent the rest to Damascus.

That morning Baldwin set out from Tiberias. By noon his men saw the smoke, and he turned back. Al-Fadil
counted about 1,000 mail shirts taken. The dead were thrown into the great cistern. In the heat a
sickness broke out in Saladin's camp, and ten of his emirs died of it. Saladin stayed 14 days in
all and had the castle pulled down.

### Where the accounts disagree
- **The dates.** Modern accounts give 23 or 24 August for Saladin's arrival and 29 or 30 August for
  the fall (Barber has the 24th and "Thursday, August 29th"). In the calendar of 1179, Saturday was
  25 August and Thursday 30 August, which the game follows.
- **The garrison.** Al-Fadil lists 80 knights with their squires and 15 captains with 50 men each,
  besides craftsmen and 100 Muslim slaves. Some modern summaries say 1,500 men.
- **The dead and the captured.** Imad al-Din gives about 700 prisoners. Wikipedia gives both 800
  killed and 700 taken, and the reverse. A 2011 press report spoke of 800 skeletons; the published
  study covers five, and the cistern has not been found.
- **The wall.** Al-Fadil says it was over 10 cubits wide; Imad al-Din says 9 cubits thick. The
  excavators found it about 4 m thick, two faces of dressed stone round a core of basalt and lime,
  which fits the chroniclers. Ibn
  al-Athir calls the castle "impregnable", though it was unfinished and fell in under a week.
- **Who wanted the castle.** Ernoul says the Templars persuaded Baldwin to break a promise not to
  build there. William of Tyre says the king began the work and gave it to the Templars.

!!! story "Five men from the garrison"
    The Hebrew University's dig found five men of the garrison, aged 20 to 40, in the burnt ruin of
    a building that had fallen on them. One, numbered L.929, had his forehead split by a sword, his
    jaw cut through and his left arm severed at the elbow; three arrows had also struck his neck. A
    second had a sword cut into his left shoulder, a third an arrow in the pelvis. One showed no
    wound in the bone. Every sword wound was on the left side, as from right-handed men facing
    them, and none showed the neck cuts of an execution (Mitchell, Nagar and Ellenblum 2006).
