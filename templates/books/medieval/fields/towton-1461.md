### The campaign
Richard, Duke of York, claimed the throne of Henry VI. The Act of Accord of 1460 made him Henry's heir, but Queen Margaret would not see her son disinherited. Her army killed York near Wakefield on 30 December 1460. It then beat Warwick at St Albans and took back the King. London shut its gates, and the army fell back to York.

York's son Edward, Earl of March, joined Warwick in London. He was proclaimed king on 4 March 1461 and marched north after the Lancastrians in three bodies: Lord Fauconberg leading, Edward with the main army, and the Duke of Norfolk raising men in the east to follow on.

The Yorkists gathered round Pontefract on 27 and 28 March. Henry VI and Margaret waited at York. Their army, under the Duke of Somerset, lay at Tadcaster across the road.

### The places
- **Pontefract**: where the Yorkist army gathered before the battle.
- **Ferrybridge**: the Aire crossing, its bridge broken by the Lancastrians. Warwick's men forced it on 27 March and were surprised there next dawn.
- **Castleford ford**: a ford upstream, where Fauconberg crossed to get behind Clifford.
- **Dintingdale**: a valley about a mile south of the Lancastrian line, where Clifford was caught and killed.
- **Saxton**: the village just behind the Yorkist line.
- **Towton**: the village behind the Lancastrian line, about 2 miles south of Tadcaster.
- **Towton Dale**: a shallow valley across the plateau. The Lancastrians drew up on its north side.
- **Castle Hill Wood**: woodland on the western edge, where Waurin says Lancastrian horsemen hid.
- **Cock Beck**: a steep-sided stream, in flood, west of the plateau. Many fleeing Lancastrians died crossing it, by what is still called Bloody Meadow.
- **Tadcaster**: the Wharfe crossing on the road to York, where the rout made for.
- **York**: where Henry VI and Margaret waited.

### The leaders
- **Edward IV** (York, king): 18 years old. He fought on foot and steadied his left wing when it gave way.
- **William Neville, Lord Fauconberg** (York, vanward): Warwick's uncle. He crossed at Castleford and led the archers. He was made Earl of Kent.
- **Richard Neville, Earl of Warwick** (York): hit in the leg by an arrow at Ferrybridge.
- **John Mowbray, Duke of Norfolk** (York): raised men in the east. His contingent came up the old London road late in the day and turned the battle.
- **Henry Beaufort, Duke of Somerset** (Lancaster, commander): the victor of Wakefield and St Albans. He chose to stand and let the Yorkists come to him. He escaped to Scotland.
- **Henry Percy, Earl of Northumberland** (Lancaster): late to guard the Castleford ford on 28 March. He was killed in the battle.
- **Sir Andrew Trollope** (Lancaster): a professional soldier, much respected. He was killed.
- **John, Lord Clifford** (Lancaster): led the dawn attack at Ferrybridge. He was killed at Dintingdale by an arrow in the throat.

### The battle
Before dawn on 28 March Lord Clifford and John, Lord Neville fell on the Yorkists camped at Ferrybridge. Lord Fitzwalter was mortally wounded rallying his men. Warwick's half-brother, the Bastard of Salisbury, was killed. Edward fought for the narrow crossing while Fauconberg crossed upstream at Castleford. Clifford fell back and was caught and killed at Dintingdale.

On Palm Sunday, 29 March, the armies drew up on the plateau between Saxton and Towton, the Lancastrians on the north side of Towton Dale. Hall says the fighting began about 09:00. Snow began as the lines formed. The wind blew it from the south into the Lancastrians' faces. Norfolk had not yet come.

Fauconberg's archers shot at long range, then stepped back. The Lancastrian archers replied into the wind and snow. Their arrows fell short. When they had shot all they had, the Yorkists came forward, gathered the spent arrows from the ground and shot them back.

The Lancastrians had to attack. Waurin says horsemen hidden in Castle Hill Wood broke the Yorkist left, and that Edward himself steadied it. The lines then fought hand to hand for hours, the Lancastrians feeding in fresh men. The Yorkists were pushed slowly back up the southern ridge.

Norfolk's men came up the old London road unseen and fell on the Lancastrian left. The Lancastrian line broke. Men threw off their armour to run. Both sides had ordered no quarter. Many were cut down from behind or after they gave up.

The worst of the killing was at Cock Beck. The chronicles say the dead lay so thick in the water that men crossed on them. The chase went on to the Wharfe at Tadcaster, where a bridge gave way and more drowned. Henry VI and Margaret fled from York to Scotland. Edward was crowned that June.

### Where the accounts disagree
- **The numbers.** Gregory's Chronicle claims hundreds of thousands. Historic England gives about 80,000 on the field, most modern writers 50,000-65,000. The Battlefields Trust doubts either side had more than about 10,000.
- **The dead.** The heralds counted 28,000, and Hall 36,776. Ross and Wolffe preferred the *Annales rerum anglicarum*'s 9,000 Lancastrians. Tim Sutherland suggests 2,800-3,800, with Ferrybridge and Dintingdale counted in.
- **One day or two.** No contemporary source names 28 March; they say "Palm Sunday eve". Sutherland argues Ferrybridge, Dintingdale and Towton were one running fight starting before dawn on the 29th.
- **How long.** Hall says about nine hours, Polydore Vergil ten. George Neville's letter has the fighting from dawn until the tenth hour of night. English Heritage estimates about three hours.
- **The story itself.** The snow, the arrows and Norfolk come from Hall, writing about 70 years later. Waurin, the only detailed 15th-century account, has none of them, nor Cock Beck; he alone has the Castle Hill Wood ambush.

!!! story "Towton 16"
    In 1996 builders putting up a garage at Towton Hall found a grave of about 38 men from the battle.
    Most had died of blows to the head, many from behind; Shannon Novak counted earlier, healed wounds
    on nine of 28 skulls. One man, numbered Towton 16, was about 45-50 years old. Long before 1461 a
    blade had cut deep into the left side of his lower jaw and broken it. It had healed well, leaving
    a rounded scar in the bone below his first molar (Knüsel, 2005). He still went to Towton.
    Caroline Wilkinson and Richard Neave rebuilt his face from the skull in 2003, scar and all.
