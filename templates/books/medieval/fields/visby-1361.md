### The campaign
Valdemar IV of Denmark, called Atterdag, took Scania back from Magnus Eriksson of Sweden in 1360. Gotland was the next prize. The island was Swedish in name but ran its own affairs, and Visby was a rich Hanseatic town on the Baltic trade routes. Town and countryside did not get on.

Magnus warned Visby by letter on 1 May 1361 that an attack was coming. Valdemar's fleet sailed first to Öland, where his men took the tower at Borgholm. On 22 July about 30 ships, some of them cogs, were seen off Stora Karlsö. The army landed on Gotland's west coast that day, perhaps 2,000 to 2,500 men, many of them hired Germans.

The Gotland farmers called out their district levies. They tried to stop the Danes at Ajmunds bridge on the road to Visby, and were beaten on 24 July at Fjäle mire beside it. The Danes reached Visby on 26 July, ahead of the levies. The townsmen shut their gates.

### The places
- **Vivesholm**: near Klintehamn; one of the two likely landing places.
- **Kronvald**: a fishing place on the Eksta coast; the other likely landing place.
- **Ajmunds bridge**: near Mästerby, on the fastest road to Visby across the Sudertingsån; the levies chose to hold it.
- **Fjäle mire**: about 1 km east of the bridge, where the bog narrows to about 150 m. It was dry enough to cross that summer, and the levies were beaten there on 24 July.
- **Visby**: the walled Hanseatic town. Its gates stayed shut during the battle, and it let Valdemar in on 29 July.
- **Söderport**: the south gate of the ring wall, outside which the battle was fought.
- **Solberga nunnery**: the Cistercian house outside the wall; the dead were buried by its cemetery.
- **Korsbetningen**: "the Cross Meadow", the town's pasture outside the wall, named from the memorial cross over the graves. The graves were dug up in 1905, 1912 and 1928-1930, the last by Bengt Thordeman, who published *Armour from the Battle of Wisby* in 1939.

### The leaders
- **Valdemar IV Atterdag** (Danish, king): about 40 years old. He led the invasion and afterwards added "King of Gotland" to his titles.
- **Christopher, Duke of Lolland** (Danish, the king's son): about 20. He rode into Visby beside his father on 29 July.
- **Eric of Lauenburg** (Danish ally): a north German lord named among Valdemar's commanders.
- **The Gotland commanders** (Gotlanders): their names are lost. The levy was organised in six divisions.
- **Magnus Eriksson** (Swedish, king): Gotland's overlord. He warned Visby of the attack by letter in May.

### The battle
No account gives the hour or the order of the fight in any detail. What follows comes from the chronicles, modern reconstructions and the bones.

On 27 July the levies came up to Visby and found the Danes between them and the wall. The Danish crossbowmen shot first. One modern account believes they knocked out about a fifth of the levy before the lines met. The graves bear this out: Ingelmark counted 126 arrow and bolt wounds, and some men had been hit five, six or seven times. The shafts seem to have come from every side.

Then came the hand-to-hand fight, and the farmers lost it. Many were boys, old men or men with old injuries, in old-fashioned kit. Against them stood professionals in mail and plate. A Lübeck chronicle put the defeat down to the farmers being unarmed and unused to war. The town kept its gates shut.

Ingelmark recorded 456 wounds from swords and axes. Most were to the legs; a mail coat and a shield kept most blows off the body. One man lost both lower legs, probably to a single stroke. One skull has 15 cuts and holes. Some men were cut down from behind, struck on the back of the head as they ran. Some leg wounds were probably given after a man was already down.

About 1,800 Gotlanders died, by the Franciscan chronicle of Visby. Danish losses are unknown; one modern figure is about 300.

The dead lay in the July heat. They were thrown into pits by Solberga's cemetery, many still in their mail, mail hoods and coats of plates; it was too hot, and there were too many, to strip them. On 29 July Valdemar and Christopher rode into Visby. That day the king issued a charter confirming the town's old rights and freedoms. A copy made in 1425 survives.

### Where the accounts disagree
- **The armies.** Modern accounts give the Danes 2,000-2,500 men and the Gotland levy about 5,000 in all. Neither rests on a count made at the time.
- **The dead.** The Franciscan chronicle gives 1,800 Gotlanders. The bones recovered number 1,185 (Ingelmark). The Swedish History Museum lists 256, 798 and 119 skeletons from the first three graves, with a fourth grave left mostly undug. A popular account puts the first grave at 300.
- **The third grave.** Some of its men lay in rows, east to west, with little armour: wounded men who died later, or Danes given a proper burial (the museum offers both).
- **The surrender.** One story, still told as fact in some summaries, has Valdemar break through the wall. Others have the council open the gates after two days. The charter of 29 July fits a bargain better than a storm.
- **The plunder.** The Franciscan chronicle says Valdemar took home "a priceless treasure of gold and silver" from town and country; the Zealand Chronicle adds furs. A Visby letter of 1362 says the Hanse's goods were saved by a payment. The museum finds no evidence that the town was sacked.

!!! story "Three barrels of silver (a legend)"
    Visby's best-known story is a legend. In it, Valdemar has three great beer barrels set up in the
    square. The town must fill them with gold and silver by a set time, or he will burn it. The burghers
    fill them, and the king sails off with his loot. No source written at the time records the barrels.
    The chronicles speak only of treasure taken, and the town was taxed rather than burned. One chronicle
    does say a Danish ship, laden with goods from the churches and the townsmen, was wrecked off the Karlsö
    islands on the way home. Carl Gustaf Hellqvist painted the scene in Munich in 1882, with a dachshund,
    a breed of the 16th century, in the crowd.
