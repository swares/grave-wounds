### The campaign
Henry V reopened the war with France in 1415, while the French princes were divided between the Armagnac and Burgundian factions. He landed at Chef-en-Caux in the Seine estuary on 13 August with about 9,000 men. On 18 August his army closed round Harfleur. Raoul de Gaucourt held the town until 22 September.

The siege cost Henry dearly in sickness. Anne Curry's count of the sick lists finds 1,330 men sent home ill. The Earl of Suffolk died there. Henry left a garrison of 300 men-at-arms and 900 archers in Harfleur.

On 8 October he set out for Calais by the coast road through Fécamp, Arques and Eu. On 13 October he found the Blanchetaque ford held against him. The army turned up the Somme looking for a way over, with the French vanguard under d'Albret and Boucicaut keeping pace on the far bank. Food ran short. On 19 October the English crossed by the causeways at Béthencourt and Voyennes. They marched north, crossed the Ternoise on 24 October and found the French across their road near Azincourt.

### The places
- **Harfleur**: the port at the Seine mouth, besieged from 18 August and taken on 22 September.
- **Blanchetaque ford**: Edward III's crossing of the Somme in 1346; found held on 13 October.
- **Péronne**: the town on the Somme just north of where the English crossed.
- **Béthencourt ford and Voyennes ford**: the crossings the English used on 19 October; the causeway at Voyennes was undefended.
- **Blangy**: where the army crossed the Ternoise on 24 October.
- **Azincourt**: the village at the west side of the field, which gave the battle its name.
- **Tramecourt**: the village and woods at the other side of the field. The ground between narrowed towards the English.
- **Calais**: English since 1347, the march's goal.

### The leaders
- **Henry V** (English, king): 29. He took the main battle in the centre and fought on foot.
- **Edward, Duke of York** (English, the vaward on the right): he had led the van on the march. He was killed in the press.
- **Thomas, Lord Camoys** (English, the rearward on the left).
- **Sir Thomas Erpingham** (English, the archers): about 60, an old soldier of Henry IV's. He gave the signal to advance.
- **Charles d'Albret** (French, Constable of France): an Armagnac and joint commander in name. He led the vanguard and was killed.
- **Jean le Maingre, called Boucicaut** (French, Marshal of France): a veteran of Nicopolis (1396). He was captured and died a prisoner in Yorkshire in 1421.
- **The Dukes of Alençon and Bar** (French, the main battle): both were killed.
- **Charles, Duke of Orléans** (French, with the vanguard): captured and taken to England.
- **Ysembart d'Azincourt** (French): led a small band of men-at-arms and some 600 peasants against the English baggage.

### The battle
The armies sighted each other on the evening of 24 October. Both spent the night in the open, in heavy rain. Henry ordered silence in his camp. In the morning the French stood between the woods across his road. Their men-at-arms stood on foot in a vanguard and a main battle, with mounted wings to ride down the archers and a rearguard of mounted servants. The ground between was freshly ploughed and soaked.

For about three hours after sunrise neither side moved. The French could wait; the hungry English could not. Erpingham threw up his baton and called out a word the French chroniclers wrote as "Nestrocq". The English went forward to long bowshot, about 250-300 m, pulled up their stakes and planted them again, and began to shoot. The musée de l'Armée puts this about 11:00.

The French mounted wings, under strength, charged. A few riders, among them Guillaume de Saveuse, reached the stakes and were killed. The rest turned, and wounded horses bolted back through their own vanguard.

The vanguard then came on through the mud. Under the arrows the French crowded in towards the English men-at-arms, the narrowing ground squeezing them closer. Men fell and could not rise; some were crushed or smothered. When their arrows ran out the archers left the stakes and fell on the French flanks with swords, hatchets and the mallets they had used to drive the stakes. York died in this fighting. Henry stood over his wounded brother Humphrey, Duke of Gloucester, and took an axe blow on his helmet. The main battle came on behind the vanguard and fared no better; Alençon was killed in it.

Late in the fight word came that the English baggage was being plundered. At the same time the Counts of Marle and Fauquembergues gathered about 600 horsemen for a fresh attack. Henry, with many prisoners still armed behind his line, ordered them killed, sparing only the greatest. The counter-attack failed and both counts died. The rest of the French rearguard went away.

!!! story "The order to kill the prisoners"
    The English men-at-arms would not do it. Killing men who had surrendered was beneath them, and each
    prisoner was a ransom lost. So Henry gave the work to a squire with 200 archers.
    Jean le Fèvre, who was with the English army, saw the prisoners "cut in pieces, heads and faces".
    Men whose helmets were off were easy; others were stabbed through the eye-slits of their bascinets.
    When the last French attack broke, Henry stopped the killing. Hundreds of prisoners survived, and most
    chroniclers of the time blamed the French lords who forced his hand rather than Henry.

### Where the accounts disagree
- **The armies.** Curry, from the pay records, gives about 9,000 English (8,500 in another count) against about 12,000 French. Rogers, Barker and Sumption put the English at about 6,000. Sumption gives the French about 14,000; Rogers about 24,000, counting the armed servants; Barker at least four to one.
- **The French losses.** The heralds counted 3,069 knights and squires dead, with at least 2,600 more bodies unidentified, about 6,000 in all. Prisoners are put at 700 to 2,200.
- **The English losses.** The records name at least 112 dead. Monstrelet gives 600; Wavrin 1,600.
- **The length of the fight.** Most accounts give about 3 hours. The musée de l'Armée has the fighting go on until about 17:00.
- **The baggage raid.** Barker, following the *Gesta Henrici Quinti*, puts it at the start; others put it late, as a trigger for the killing.
- **The site.** The traditional field lies between the Tramecourt woods and Azincourt. Metal-detector surveys there have found no trace of the battle. Michael Livingston has argued for ground west of Azincourt.
