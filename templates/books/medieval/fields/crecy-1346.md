### The campaign
Edward III of England claimed the French crown. In 1346 he landed at Saint-Vaast-la-Hougue in Normandy on 12 July, taking the French by surprise. His men stormed Caen on 26 July and looted it for five days.

The army marched east, burning as it went, and reached Poissy, about 20 miles from Paris. Philip VI of France shadowed it along the far bank of the Seine. On 16 August Edward turned north for the coast and the ships he expected at Le Crotoy. Philip reached the Somme first and held its bridges and fords.

On the morning of 24 August the English forced the tidal ford of Blanchetaque against Godemar du Fay. They plundered Noyelles and Le Crotoy for food, but no fleet was waiting. On 25 August they went round the Forest of Crécy and reached the slope above the Vallée des Clercs by evening, in Ponthieu, a county held by the English crown. Philip marched out from Abbeville on 26 August to find them.

### The places
- **Abbeville**: Philip VI's base and bridge on the Somme, which he left on 26 August.
- **Acheux-en-Vimeu**: the English camp on the night before the ford.
- **Blanchetaque ford**: a tidal ford across the Somme, passable a few hours twice a day; forced on 24 August.
- **Port-le-Grand**: the north bank of the ford, where Godemar du Fay drew up about 3,500 men, 500 of them men-at-arms.
- **Noyelles-sur-Mer and Le Crotoy**: towns north of the ford, looted for their stores and then burned.
- **Crécy**: the town on the River Maye; the English right rested on it.
- **Wadicourt**: the village at the northern end of the English line, anchoring its left.
- **The Vallée des Clercs**: the valley below the English slope, where the French attacked; the accepted field since at least 1757.
- **Croix de Bohême**: a stone cross where, by tradition, the blind King John of Bohemia fell.
- **Labroye (La Broye castle)**: where Philip came after dark on the night of the battle, asking to be let in.

### The leaders
- **Edward III** (English, king): 33 years old. He chose the ground and kept the third battle, his reserve, in his own hand.
- **Edward, Prince of Wales** (English, first battle): later the Black Prince, then 16. His battle took the brunt of the charges.
- **William de Bohun, Earl of Northampton** (English, constable): probably led the men-at-arms at Blanchetaque, and served with the Prince's battle, or with Arundel's, depending on the account.
- **Richard FitzAlan, Earl of Arundel** (English, commander of the second battle).
- **Philip VI** (French, king): had two horses killed under him and was hit in the jaw by an arrow.
- **John of Luxembourg, King of Bohemia** (French ally): blind, aged 50. He was killed with his escort.
- **Charles, Count of Alençon** (French, Philip's brother): led the first men-at-arms through the Genoese, and was killed.
- **Antonio Doria and Carlo Grimaldi** (French, captains of the Genoese crossbowmen): led the hired crossbowmen.
- **Godemar du Fay** (French): held Blanchetaque and was routed.

### The battle
French scouts found the English about noon on 26 August. Philip's council advised waiting a day, but the attack began about 17:00. Whether Philip chose this, or his front ranks pressed on, is not clear. The French unfurled the oriflamme, the banner that meant no prisoners.

The English stood on foot in three battles, their horses in a laager of carts behind, pits dug in front. Just before the fighting a thunder shower passed. The sun came out behind the English, in the French faces.

The Genoese crossbowmen went in first, tired from the march. Their pavises, the big shields they shot from behind, were still with the baggage. Froissart says the English archers shot "so thick that it seemed snow". The Genoese broke. The French men-at-arms behind them rode through them, cutting them down as they came.

Then came mounted charges up the slope, contingent after contingent as each arrived. They were disordered by the fleeing crossbowmen, the pits and the mud, and shot at from the flanks. The heaviest fighting fell on the Prince's battle. One report has the Prince beaten to his knees, his standard-bearer standing over the banner.

The sun set about 18:40 and the charges went on into the dark. Philip's own battle went in late, and the oriflamme was left on the field. About midnight Philip left the field with a handful of men. At La Broye he called at the gate: "Open your gate quickly, for this is the fortune of France" (Froissart, Berners' translation). By morning he was at Amiens.

The English slept on the field. Next morning, in mist, mounted English men-at-arms caught French levies still coming up and cut them up. On 4 September Edward laid siege to Calais. It fell on 3 August 1347.

### Where the accounts disagree
- **The English army.** Ayton puts it at about 14,000 and Rogers at 15,000. Sumption, working from what the fleet could carry, gives 7,000-10,000.
- **The French army.** Chroniclers give 72,000 or 120,000. Modern estimates are about 8,000 mounted men-at-arms, 2,000-6,000 crossbowmen and an unknown number of foot. Schnerb doubts the higher crossbow figure.
- **The losses.** The heralds counted 1,542 French knights and squires dead, not counting the next day's. Froissart's heralds give 1,200 knights "and more than thirty thousand other". An English roll-call found about 40 dead; some historians suggest 300.
- **The number of charges.** Knighton counts 16 attacks before dawn; later writers often say 15. Rogers and Ayton doubt any fixed number.
- **The oriflamme.** Sumption has it left on the field; DeVries has it captured when its bearer fell; yet Miles de Noyers, who carried it, lived until 1350.
- **The Prince's danger.** Froissart has Edward refuse him help, saying to "suffer him this day to win his spurs". Other accounts have the King send men from the reserve.
- **The site.** The Vallée des Clercs has been accepted since at least 1757, but no archaeology of a large battle has been found there. Michael Livingston (2015) argues the fight was by the Forest of Crécy, about 5.5 km south.

!!! story "One stroke with my sword"
    John of Luxembourg, King of Bohemia, was blind. He rode with Philip as an ally.
    When he heard how the battle was going, he asked where his son was. Then he called on his knights to lead
    him forward, so "that I may strike one stroke with my sword" (Froissart, Berners' translation).
    So that they would not lose him in the press, his companions tied the reins of their bridles to each
    other and put the King in front. They rode into the English line in the failing light. All of them were
    killed.
