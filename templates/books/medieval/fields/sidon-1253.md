### The campaign
Louis IX of France invaded Egypt in 1249. In 1250 his army was beaten at Mansurah and he was taken
prisoner on the retreat. Once ransomed, he did not go home. He stayed in the Holy Land until 1254
and spent much of his money on the walls of the crusader towns: Acre, Jaffa, Caesarea and Sidon.

Sidon was a port on the coast north of Tyre. The crusaders had built a castle on an islet off its
harbour in 1227-1228, and another stood on a mound at the landward edge of the town. Louis's masons
were rebuilding the town wall in 1253.

The Muslim powers were divided. Egypt was now ruled by the Mamluk Aybak, and Damascus by the
Ayyubid an-Nasir Yusuf. Louis had bargained with Egypt against Damascus. In April 1253 the two made peace,
with the caliph of Baghdad mediating. That left the army of Damascus free to strike at the Franks.

Later in 1253 that army came down on Sidon while the wall was still unfinished. Louis was not
there.

### The places
- **Sidon**: the walled port, under repair in 1253, sacked that year and again in 1260.
- **Sea Castle**: the crusader castle on its islet, about 80 m off the waterfront, built in
  1227-1228 and completed under Louis IX.
- **Castle of St Louis (Qal'at al-Mu'izz)**: the land castle on its mound at the town's edge,
  built on the remains of an older fortress.
- **The town ditch**: the ditch along the town's landward side. The two mass graves were dug into
  its floor, north-north-east of the land castle.
- **The gateway**: a fortified gate in the town wall, with a crossing over the ditch, found close
  to the graves.
- **Acre**: the chief city of the kingdom, whose walls Louis repaired first.
- **Jaffa**: the southern port whose walls Louis also repaired.
- **Damascus**: an-Nasir Yusuf's capital, inland beyond the mountains, from which the raid came.

### The leaders
- **Louis IX** (France, king): the crusade's leader. He paid for Sidon's walls and came afterwards to
  bury the dead.
- **Jean de Joinville** (France, knight): Louis's companion, who wrote the eyewitness
  account of the king at Sidon.
- **An-Nasir Yusuf** (Ayyubid, ruler of Damascus): his peace with Egypt in 1253 freed his army. In
  1260 the Mongols took him prisoner and put him to death.
- **Aybak** (Mamluk, sultan of Egypt): made peace with Damascus in April 1253 and was recognised as
  sultan.
- **Kitbuqa** (Mongol, general): led the force that sacked Sidon in August 1260.
- **Julian Grenier** (Franks, lord of Sidon): in 1260 he raided Damascene country and killed a
  Mongol officer, and the Mongols took their revenge on Sidon. He then sold the lordship to the
  Templars.

### The battle
The written record is thin. *L'Estoire d'Eracles* says the attackers sacked the town, killed
hundreds and took others prisoner. No account gives the hour, the strengths or the names of the
captains.

The unfinished wall could not be held. The mass graves show what followed. The study by Mikulski and
his colleagues counted at least 25 men and boys, from about 15 years old to elderly. There were no
women or small children. Several had healed head wounds from earlier fights; some may have been
soldiers.

Most of the dead had more wounds on the back than the front, so many were cut down as they ran. The
blows fell mostly on the head, neck and shoulders. That fits horsemen striking down at men on foot.
Some men had cuts on the hands and wrists, as if they had raised them against a blade. One man had
at least 12 wounds on 16 bones.

Then there are the necks. Almost every man had a cut across the back of the neck. The authors think
some captives were beheaded after the fight.

The bodies lay in the open for weeks before anyone buried them. They had been stripped; almost no
weapons or valuables were found. Some bones were charred. When the dead were at last gathered, they
went into a pit cut in the ditch floor, with more laid on the surface beside it.

### Where the accounts disagree
- **1253 or 1260.** Sidon was sacked twice in seven years. A coin of Frederick II from about
  1245-1250 and radiocarbon dates fit either. The authors favour 1253: the weeks of exposure match
  Joinville's account. Their abstract says only that the men "likely" died in one of the two
  attacks. The game dates the graves to 1253.
- **Who attacked.** Mikulski and his colleagues, following *Eracles*, call the attackers "Mamluk
  forces from Damascus". In 1253 Damascus was ruled by the Ayyubid an-Nasir Yusuf, so many writers
  would call them Ayyubid. The game's wound table says Mamluk; its battlefield says an army from
  Damascus. Wikipedia's page on Sidon mentions an Ayyubid sack in 1249 and nothing in 1253.
- **Battle or execution.** The neck wounds could be killing in the fight or beheading of
  prisoners after it. The authors lean towards executions for some.
- **Louis's burial.** Joinville says the king buried the dead of 1253. Whether these are those
  same men cannot be proved.
- **The numbers.** *Eracles* gives hundreds dead. No source gives either army's strength.

!!! story "The king and the dead of Sidon"
    When Louis reached Sidon after the raid, the dead were still lying unburied. Joinville, who
    was with him, says the king "had personally carried the bodies, all rotting and stinking, to
    place them in trenches in the ground" (Caroline Smith's translation). Joinville adds that the
    king never covered his nose. Some 70 years later, an artist painting a prayer book for Queen
    Jeanne d'Évreux showed the scene among the deeds of Saint Louis. The graves dug at Sidon in
    2015 held bodies exposed for weeks, as Joinville describes.
