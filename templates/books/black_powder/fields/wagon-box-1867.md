### The campaign
In July 1867 the Oglala under Red Cloud, other Lakota bands, Northern Cheyenne and a few Arapaho held their Sun Dance on the Tongue and Rosebud rivers. They then planned to strike the Bozeman Trail forts again. They could not agree which fort to attack first, and split. One party, mostly Cheyenne, went against Fort C. F. Smith. On 1 August it attacked a hay camp there, in the Hayfield Fight, and was beaten off. The other, mostly Lakota, went against Fort Phil Kearny.

The fort burned wood for building, cooking and the winter. Civilian contractors, Gilmore and Porter's men by WyoHistory's account, cut it in the pinery west of the fort, under an infantry guard. On 31 July Captain James Powell took Company C, 27th Infantry, out for a 30-day turn as the guard, 51 men by Wikipedia's count. The company had new Springfield breech-loading rifles, converted from muzzle-loaders, which could be fired much faster than the old muskets.

The contractors' men had made a corral of 14 wagon boxes taken off their running gear and set on the ground, about 60-70 feet by 25-30. Each box was about 10 feet long, 4½ feet wide and 2½ feet high, of planks about an inch thick. On the morning of 2 August, 14 soldiers were out escorting the wood wagons and 13 guarding the woodcutters' camp.

### The places
- **Fort Phil Kearny**: on the Little Piney, south-east of the corral; accounts give three to six miles. The relief came from here.
- **Pilot Hill**: the fort's lookout, from which the fight was seen.
- **The pinery**: the woods on the mountain slope where the wood was cut.
- **Big Piney and Little Piney creeks**: the corral stood on the open ground between them. The woodcutters' camps were along both.
- **Sullivant's Ridge**: the hill to the east where several hundred warriors watched.
- **The two sites**: the place Sam Gibson pointed out in 1908, now marked by the 1921 and 1936 monuments, and a rival site marked with a pipe in 1916.
- **Fort C. F. Smith**: the fort on the Bighorn where the other party fought the Hayfield Fight on 1 August.

### The leaders
- **James W. Powell** (US Army, captain, 27th Infantry): commanded at the corral and wrote the official report.
- **John C. Jenness** (US Army, lieutenant): Powell's second. Shot in the head and killed.
- **Benjamin F. Smith** (US Army, major): led the relief from the fort.
- **Samuel S. Gibson** (US Army, 27th Infantry): aged 18, the youngest man in the corral. He wrote the fullest soldier's account.
- **Red Cloud** (Oglala Lakota): the leader of the war. Jenness was said to have seen him on a hill through field glasses; WyoHistory calls his presence unverified.
- **Red Cloud's nephew** (Oglala Lakota): led the main mounted charge, by WyoHistory's account. His name is not given.
- **High Back Bone (Hump)** (Miniconjou Lakota) and **Crazy Horse** (Oglala Lakota): Ostlind names both as leaders of the attack.
- **Fire Thunder** (Oglala Lakota): fought there, and later told John Neihardt of it.
- **White Bull** (Miniconjou Lakota): mostly watched, by his own account to Stanley Vestal.

### The battle
Gibson says the fight began about 07:00. He was on picket and saw seven mounted warriors coming over the divide from the north. More rose behind them. A party of about 200 on foot went for the mule herd. Others struck the woodcutters' camps and burned them. Three soldiers and four civilians were killed outside the corral. Powell went out at the attackers' rear, which let others reach the woods or the fort. Some warriors stopped to take the horses and mules, which gave the corral time. In the end 26 soldiers and 6 civilians were inside.

Within about 15 minutes, WyoHistory says, some 800 mounted warriors were round the corral. The first charge, from the south-west, met steady fire and turned away. The men fired through holes and over grain sacks, yokes and kegs piled between the boxes. They had about 7,000 rounds.

For about three hours the warriors came on foot. Some got within a few feet of the boxes. Gibson writes that the bullets tore the tops of the boxes "to slivers". Burning arrows set the hay and dung alight. Jenness was killed, with Privates Henry Haggerty and Thomas Boyle, and two men were wounded. WyoHistory has a mounted charge in a wedge, led by Red Cloud's nephew, between 12:00 and 13:00. Powell later said his ammunition was running low and his men worn out.

Smith left the fort with about 100 men and a mountain howitzer; Wikipedia says about 11:30. A shell fell short of the ridge, but the warriors drew off. Smith found most of the defenders alive. He said their faces looked as if blacked with "burnt cork". The fight had lasted about six hours. Fire Thunder later told Neihardt that the attack failed because the soldiers had new guns. Even so, the Lakota counted the day a success: they had burned the camps, taken the mules and killed soldiers.

### Where the accounts disagree
- **The warriors' losses.** Hyde, Vestal and several Sioux leaders say 6 killed and 6 wounded. Powell reported 60 killed and 120 wounded; Keenan calls this "wildly exaggerated". Soldiers who were there said 300-800. A Sioux told Colonel Richard Dodge in 1867 of 1,137 dead and wounded, and Grenville Dodge said Red Cloud gave him over 1,100 in 1885. Descendants at the 1969 re-enactment said 1,200-1,500.
- **The warriors.** Gibson's comrade cried out that there were thousands. WyoHistory says over 1,000, with 800 mounted round the corral. Wikipedia puts both parties together at 300-1,000.
- **The soldiers' losses.** Wikipedia gives 7 killed and 2 wounded; WyoHistory, from Hyde, 6 soldiers and 4 civilians killed. Gibson names the second private Doyle, not Boyle.
- **The company.** WyoHistory calls it the 26th Infantry; most sources, and the game, the 27th.
- **The rifles.** WyoHistory says a contractor brought about 700 rifles shortly before. The Army Heritage Museum says the 27th at the fort had received 195 by May 1867. Wikipedia adds some Henry rifles, and Spencers among the civilians.
- **The times.** Gibson has about 07:00; Wikipedia, 07:30 to 13:30. The game starts the corral fight at about 09:15.
- **The order of the attacks.** WyoHistory puts the big mounted charge last, around noon. Wikipedia has the mounted charge first and a final one from the south-east.
- **The site.** Gibson's site, supported by cartridges found in 1916, is the one historians now accept.

!!! story "The boy who found the corral"
    Sam Gibson was 18 on the day of the fight, the youngest soldier in the corral. In 1908
    he came back and showed Charles Bezold where it had stood, by a fence along the Little
    Piney. Days later Bezold found cartridge cases and a crushed canteen there. Another
    survivor, Max Littmann, was taken in 1916 to a different spot, marked with a pipe.
    Grace Raymond Hebard of the University of Wyoming searched both. At Gibson's spot she
    found cartridges that fitted the Springfields and a piece of an oak wagon box; at the
    pipe, nothing. In 1919 Gibson marked the place again with a spike and a broomstick. The
    state put a granite stone there in 1921.
