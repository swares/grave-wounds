### The campaign
In 1863 John Bozeman opened a trail north from the North Platte, through the Powder River country, to the Montana goldfields. The Lakota, Cheyenne and Arapaho had taken this country from the Crow by about 1860 and hunted it.

In June 1866 commissioners called the Lakota to Fort Laramie to agree to safe passage on the trail. On 13 June Colonel Henry B. Carrington arrived there with the 18th Infantry, on his way to build forts along it. The next day the Oglala leader Red Cloud refused to deal with him and left, with They Fear Even His Horses and others. Carrington garrisoned Fort Reno on 28 June, began Fort Phil Kearny on the Little Piney in mid-July, and founded Fort C. F. Smith on 13 August.

By mid-December Shannon Smith counts nearly 70 soldiers and civilians dead in more than 50 fights. Captain William J. Fetterman arrived on 3 November. On 6 December, relieving a wood train, Lieutenant Horatio Bingham chased decoys and was killed with a sergeant. On 19 December Captain James Powell relieved another train and, as ordered, did not follow over Lodge Trail Ridge.

The Oglala, Miniconjou, Northern Cheyenne and Arapaho camped on the Tongue River planned a large ambush before winter scattered them. They chose ground on the Bozeman Trail north of Lodge Trail Ridge, out of sight of the fort.

### The places
- **Fort Phil Kearny**: Carrington's headquarters, a stockade of 17 acres on the Little Piney, near present-day Story, Wyoming.
- **Pilot Hill**: the lookout near the fort whose pickets signalled attacks on the trains.
- **Sullivant Hills**: on the wood road, where the train was attacked on 21 December.
- **The pinery**: the woods about five miles north-west of the fort, where the trains cut timber.
- **Lodge Trail Ridge**: about two miles north of the fort. Carrington forbade pursuit over it.
- **Peno Creek**: the creek and flats at the north foot of the ridge, where the warriors waited.
- **The rocks**: boulders at the end of the fight nearest the fort, where most of the dead lay. A monument went up near them in 1908.
- **Fort Laramie**: about 230 miles south-east, where John "Portugee" Phillips carried the news.

### The leaders
- **Henry B. Carrington** (US Army, commanding): colonel of the 18th Infantry. He was later blamed for the loss.
- **William J. Fetterman** (US Army, captain): a brevet lieutenant colonel from the Civil War, who claimed the command by seniority. Killed.
- **Frederick H. Brown** (US Army, captain): the quartermaster, about to be transferred. Killed.
- **George W. Grummond** (US Army, lieutenant): led the cavalry, ordered to obey Fetterman and not leave him. Killed.
- **Red Cloud** (Oglala Lakota): the leading voice for war. Wikipedia has him present, his role unknown; the fort's history doubts he fought.
- **High Back Bone (Hump)** (Miniconjou Lakota): named as the chief fighting leader.
- **Crazy Horse** (Oglala Lakota): by later tradition a leader of the decoys. Monnett finds no early Indian account that puts him there.
- **American Horse** (Oglala Lakota): named by Ricker's informants as a decoy. He said he killed Fetterman.
- **Little Wolf** (Cheyenne), **Black Shield** and **Black Leg** (Lakota), **Medicine Man** and **Black Bear** (Arapaho): other leaders named by the fort's history.

### The battle
On 21 December, Carrington testified, "it was nearly 11 o'clock" when Pilot Hill signalled that the wood train had corralled under attack. He sent 49 infantry and 27 men of the 2nd Cavalry. Fetterman took the command from Powell. Brown and two civilians, James Wheatley and Isaac Fisher, with Henry repeating rifles, went too: 81 in all. The written order was "Under no circumstances pursue over the ridge viz; Lodge Trail Ridge."

Fetterman did not go west to the train but north-east, to the south end of Lodge Trail Ridge. Carrington's report first took this as a move against the attackers' rear. Ten decoys, two each from the Oglala, Miniconjou, Brulé, Cheyenne and Arapaho, drew the column along the crest towards Peno Creek. The cavalry went ahead and the infantry fell behind.

Carrington heard firing about 12:00. At a signal the warriors came up out of the ravines and across the flats. The cavalry fell back up the ridge. Wheatley, Fisher and a few soldiers held rocks at the far end and were killed there. The rest of the cavalry made a stand on the ridge and were overrun. The infantry, with Fetterman and Brown, made their stand at the rocks nearest the fort. The Miniconjou White Bull, then 17, told Stanley Vestal in 1932 that he pulled a wounded man out of the fight and killed a soldier with an arrow. Only six soldiers died of gunshots.

Captain Tenodor Ten Eyck went out with about 75 men and reached the ridge top about 12:45. The warriors drew off. His men brought back 49 bodies, "nearly all ... heaped near four rocks at the point nearest the Fort", Carrington says. The rest, Grummond among them, came in next day, stripped and mostly mutilated. Phillips and Daniel Dixon rode for help that night. Carrington was relieved in January 1867.

### Where the accounts disagree
- **The warriors.** Carrington's report says "probably three hundred"; his telegram, "nearly three thousand". Surgeon C. M. Hines guessed 4,000-5,000. Wikipedia gives about 1,000; a review of Monnett's 2017 book, more than 1,500.
- **Their losses.** Ambrose gives 13, some hit by their own arrows; George Bent, 14. White Elk, a Cheyenne, said 2 Cheyenne and 50-60 Lakota. Red Cloud later named 11 Oglala dead.
- **The soldiers.** Carrington gives 81; Smith counts 80.
- **The order of the fight.** Ambrose has the infantry overrun first, the cavalry 20 minutes later. Smith and the fort's history have the cavalry caught first, the infantry last at the rocks. Vaughn found the infantry could not have reached the cavalry's ground in the time.
- **The sides of the trap.** Wikipedia puts the Cheyenne and Arapaho west of the trail and the Lakota east. The game's map has them the other way round.
- **The boast.** Legend has Fetterman say that with 80 men he could ride through the whole Sioux nation; WyoHistory's battlefield page says 100. Smith traces his reckless image to books by Carrington's two wives (1868, 1910); Monnett also treats it as myth.
- **The orders.** The Carringtons say the order was given twice. Smith suggests the two men may have planned an attack from the rear.
- **Fetterman's death.** Carrington found him and Brown each shot in the left temple, as if they shot each other. American Horse claimed to have killed him.
- **Crazy Horse.** Monnett traces the decoy story to Mari Sandoz's 1942 book, though he doubts Crazy Horse was absent.

!!! story "The bugler"
    Adolph Metzger was the cavalry's bugler, a German-born soldier who had served since 1855.
    Accounts say that at the end he fought with his bugle, striking out with it until it was
    battered out of shape. He is thought to have been the last soldier to die, though whether
    he fell with Wheatley and Fisher or near Fetterman is not clear. American Horse testified
    that the warriors did not mutilate his body. They covered his head with a buffalo robe.
    When Carrington's men came to collect the dead, his was the only body not cut. Nobody
    recorded why he was spared.
