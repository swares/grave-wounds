### The campaign
In 1863 John Bozeman opened a trail from the emigrant road on the North Platte, north through the Powder River country, to the Montana goldfields. The Lakota, Cheyenne and Arapaho had taken this country from the Crow by about 1860, and they hunted it. The army's 1865 Powder River Expedition did nothing to open the road.

In June 1866 government commissioners called the Lakota to Fort Laramie to agree to safe passage. On 13 June Colonel Henry B. Carrington arrived there with the 18th Infantry, on his way to build forts on the trail. The next day the Oglala leader Red Cloud refused to deal with him and left the talks, with They Fear Even His Horses and others. Carrington marched on 17 June. He garrisoned Fort Reno on 28 June, began Fort Phil Kearny on the Little Piney in mid-July, and founded Fort C. F. Smith on the Bighorn on 13 August. The country was then part of Dakota Territory.

The warriors attacked hay parties, herds, travellers and the wood trains that fed the fort's sawmills. By mid-December Shannon Smith counts nearly 70 soldiers and civilians dead in more than 50 fights. Captain William J. Fetterman arrived on 3 November. On 6 December a wood train was attacked and Carrington and Fetterman went out in two columns. Lieutenant Horatio Bingham was drawn after decoys and killed with a sergeant. On 19 December Captain James Powell relieved another train and, as ordered, did not follow over Lodge Trail Ridge. The next day Carrington turned down a plan by Fetterman and Captain Frederick Brown to raid the villages on the Tongue River.

Those villages, of Oglala, Miniconjou, Northern Cheyenne and Arapaho, planned a large ambush before the winter scattered them. They chose ground on the Bozeman Trail north of Lodge Trail Ridge, out of sight of the fort.

### The places
- **Fort Phil Kearny**: Carrington's headquarters, a stockade of about 17 acres on the Little Piney, near present-day Story, Wyoming.
- **Pilot Hill**: the lookout hill near the fort, whose pickets signalled when a train was attacked.
- **Sullivant Hills**: the hills on the wood road where the train was attacked on 21 December.
- **The pinery**: the woods about five miles north-west of the fort, where the trains cut timber.
- **Lodge Trail Ridge**: the ridge about two miles north of the fort. Carrington's order forbade pursuit over it.
- **Peno Creek**: the creek and flats at the north foot of the ridge, where the warriors waited.
- **The rocks and the monument**: the boulders near the south end of the fight, nearest the fort, where most of the dead lay. A monument was put up near them in 1908.
- **The Tongue River villages**: the camps about 50 miles north from which the warriors came.
- **Fort Laramie**: about 230 miles south-east, where John "Portugee" Phillips carried the news.

### The leaders
- **Henry B. Carrington** (US Army, commanding): colonel of the 18th Infantry and of the Mountain District. He gave the orders for the relief and was later blamed for the loss.
- **William J. Fetterman** (US Army, captain, 18th Infantry): a brevet lieutenant colonel from the Civil War, who claimed the command by seniority. Killed.
- **Frederick H. Brown** (US Army, captain): the post quartermaster, under orders to leave the post. He joined the column; killed.
- **George W. Grummond** (US Army, lieutenant): led the cavalry. Carrington ordered him to obey Fetterman and not leave him. Killed.
- **Tenodor Ten Eyck** (US Army, captain): led the second relief, which found the dead.
- **Red Cloud** (Oglala Lakota): the leading voice for war. Wikipedia counts him present with his role unknown; the fort's history doubts he fought.
- **High Back Bone (Hump)** (Miniconjou Lakota): named as the chief fighting leader.
- **Crazy Horse** (Oglala Lakota): by later tradition a leader of the decoys. Monnett finds no early Indian account that puts him there.
- **American Horse** (Oglala Lakota): named by Ricker's informants as a decoy. He said he killed Fetterman.
- **Little Wolf** (Northern Cheyenne), **Black Shield** and **Black Leg** (Lakota) and **Medicine Man** and **Black Bear** (Arapaho): named by the fort's history among the other leaders.

### The battle
On 21 December a train went out about 10:00 towards the pinery. Carrington testified that "it was nearly 11 o'clock" when Pilot Hill signalled it had corralled under attack. He sent 49 infantry and 27 men of the 2nd Cavalry. Fetterman took the command from Powell. Brown and two civilians, James Wheatley and Isaac Fisher, carrying Henry repeating rifles, went too. Carrington put the total at 81. His written order was "Under no circumstances pursue over the ridge viz; Lodge Trail Ridge."

Fetterman did not go west to the train. He went north-east towards the south end of Lodge Trail Ridge. Carrington's report took this as a move to cut the attackers off from behind. Small groups of warriors drew him on to the ridge, and ten decoys, two each from the Oglala, Miniconjou, Brulé, Cheyenne and Arapaho, led him along its crest towards Peno Creek. The cavalry went ahead and the infantry fell behind.

Carrington says firing was heard about 12:00. At a signal the warriors came up out of the ravines on both sides and across the flats; the Cheyenne and Arapaho, by most accounts, on the west, the Lakota on the east. The cavalry fell back up the ridge. Wheatley, Fisher and a few soldiers held some rocks at the north end and were killed there. The rest of the cavalry made a stand on the ridge and were overrun. The infantry, with Fetterman and Brown, made their last stand at the rocks nearest the fort. Only six of the dead had been killed by gunshot. Wikipedia puts the fight at about 20 minutes for the infantry and 20 more for the cavalry; the order is disputed.

Carrington sent Ten Eyck with about 75 men, later reinforced. He reached the top of the ridge about 12:45 and saw the warriors in the valley, but they drew off. His men brought back 49 bodies, "nearly all ... heaped near four rocks at the point nearest the Fort", Carrington says. The rest, Grummond among them, were brought in the next day. All had been stripped, and most mutilated.

That night Phillips and Daniel Dixon rode for help. Phillips reached Fort Laramie in a blizzard on Christmas night. Carrington was relieved of command and left in January 1867.

### Where the accounts disagree
- **The warriors.** Carrington's report says "probably three hundred"; his telegram the same day, "nearly three thousand". Surgeon C. M. Hines guessed 4,000-5,000. Wikipedia gives about 1,000; Monnett's review cites more than 1,500.
- **Their losses.** Ambrose gives 10 Lakota, 2 Cheyenne and 1 Arapaho, some hit by their own arrows. George Bent gives 14. White Elk, a Cheyenne, said 2 Cheyenne and 50-60 Lakota. Red Cloud later named 11 Oglala. Carrington counted 65 pools of blood in an acre.
- **The soldiers.** Carrington gives 81, which the data follows. Smith counts exactly 80.
- **The order of the fight.** Ambrose has the infantry overrun first and the cavalry 20 minutes later. Smith, and the fort's history, have the cavalry ahead and caught first, and the infantry last at the rocks. Vaughn showed the infantry could not have reached the cavalry's ground in the time.
- **The boast.** The legend has Fetterman say that with 80 men he could ride through the whole Sioux nation. WyoHistory's battlefield page gives 100 men. Smith traces his reckless image to the books of Carrington's two wives (1868, 1910) and calls it flawed; Monnett also treats it as myth.
- **The orders.** Carrington and his wives say the order was given twice. Smith suggests Carrington and Fetterman may have planned an attack from the rear.
- **Fetterman's death.** Carrington found him and Brown each with a revolver shot in the left temple, suggesting they shot each other. American Horse claimed to have killed him.
- **The sides of the trap.** Wikipedia puts the Cheyenne and Arapaho west of the trail and the Lakota east. The game's map, for play, has Lakota in the west ravines and Cheyenne and Arapaho in the east.
- **Crazy Horse.** Most popular accounts make him the leading decoy. Monnett traces this to Mari Sandoz's 1942 book and finds no early Indian source for it, though he doubts Crazy Horse was absent.

!!! story "The bugler"
    Adolph Metzger was the cavalry's bugler, a German-born soldier who had served since 1855.
    Accounts say that at the end he fought with his bugle, striking out with it until it was
    battered out of shape. He is thought to have been the last soldier to die, though whether
    he fell with Wheatley and Fisher or near Fetterman is not clear. American Horse testified
    that the warriors did not mutilate his body. They covered his head with a buffalo robe.
    When Carrington's men came to collect the dead, his was the only body not cut. Nobody
    recorded why he was spared.
