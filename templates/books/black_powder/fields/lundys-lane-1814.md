### The campaign
On 3 July 1814 Major General Jacob Brown crossed the Niagara near Lake Erie with about 3,500 Americans and took Fort Erie. Two days later Brigadier General Winfield Scott's brigade beat Major General Phineas Riall's British at Chippawa. Riall fell back to Fort George. Brown followed as far as Queenston, but he had no heavy guns for the forts, and Commodore Chauncey's squadron on Lake Ontario never came.

On 24 July Brown fell back to Chippawa to secure his supplies. Riall followed and took post at Lundy's Lane, about 4 miles north, on a low hill above the falls. Lieutenant General Gordon Drummond sailed from York, reached Niagara at daybreak on 25 July and took command. He sent Lieutenant Colonel John Tucker south from Fort Niagara along the American bank to press Brown.

Brown answered by sending Scott north towards Queenston, to counter the threat to the American bank. Riall ordered a retreat and sent Colonel Hercules Scott's brigade towards Queenston. Drummond countermanded both orders and hurried his troops to the hill.

### The places
- **Lundy's Lane**: the road west from the Portage road over the hill; the British line stood along it.
- **Drummond Hill**: the rise above the lane, with a meeting house and burying ground where the British guns stood; now Drummond Hill Cemetery.
- **The Portage road**: the main road north and south along the river, by which Scott came up from Chippawa.
- **Willson's tavern**: on the Portage road, where the British pickets were chased away as Scott came up.
- **Chippawa**: the American camp from 24 July, behind the creek where they had won on 5 July.
- **Bridgewater**: the American headquarters, about 2 miles off, which gave the battle its American name. The British called it Niagara Falls.
- **Queenston**: below the escarpment, where Brown had camped and Drummond fell back after the battle.
- **Fort George and Fort Niagara**: the British forts at the mouth of the river.
- **Fort Erie**: the American foothold on the Canadian bank, where Brown's army went afterwards.

### The leaders
- **Jacob Brown** (American, commander): came up with the main body after dark; wounded, he ordered the retreat at midnight.
- **Winfield Scott** (American, 1st Brigade): attacked the hill without waiting, and was badly wounded late in the night.
- **Thomas Jesup** (American, 25th Infantry): led his regiment round the British left in the dusk.
- **Eleazer Ripley** (American, 2nd Brigade): held the crest and commanded after Brown and Scott were hit.
- **James Miller** (American, 21st Infantry): took the British guns with the bayonet.
- **Peter B. Porter** (American, volunteers and militia): led the third brigade, about 550 men.
- **Gordon Drummond** (British, commander): born in Quebec, lieutenant governor of Upper Canada. He was shot in the neck and stayed in the field.
- **Phineas Riall** (British, advance division): wounded in the arm and taken prisoner in the dusk.
- **Hercules Scott** (British, 1st Brigade): his column, sent towards Queenston and back, reached the field about 21:00.
- **Joseph Morrison** (British, 89th Foot): commanded the troops Drummond brought from Fort George.

### The battle
Scott's brigade, about 1,000 men with Towson's battery, came up the Portage road in the early evening. Drummond says the action began at six. He had formed on the hill: the 89th, the Royal Scots and the 41st's light companies behind the crest, two brass 24-pounders on top, the Glengarry Light Infantry on the right and the militia on the left. Scott attacked rather than wait for Brown, and his brigade was cut up in the open in front of the guns.

On the British left, Major Jesup's 25th Infantry found a track round the flank. It drove back the light company of the 8th and the Incorporated Militia, and in the dusk took a group of wounded men and messengers behind the British line. Riall was among them, with the militia captain William Hamilton Merritt.

About 21:00 Brown came up with Ripley's brigade and Porter's volunteers, and they relieved Scott's men. Drummond had drawn his centre back, and the guns stood exposed. The 21st Infantry under Lieutenant Colonel Miller came up through cover in the dark, fired one volley at a few yards and charged. They took the guns and drove the British centre off the hill. At about the same time Hercules Scott's tired column came onto the field and ran into Ripley's brigade.

Drummond counter-attacked three times to win back the guns. The first was beaten off at short range. In the second, Scott led the remnant of his brigade in an attack of his own. It came under fire from both sides and broke, and he was badly wounded. The third came shortly before midnight, and at one point the lines met with the bayonet. It too was beaten off.

By midnight only about 700 Americans stood in line, and Brown and Scott were both disabled. Short of water and ammunition, Brown ordered Ripley back to Chippawa. They left the British guns, though they took one 6-pounder. At daybreak Ripley marched back with about 1,200 men, found Drummond on the hill with about 2,200, and withdrew. The Americans fell back to Fort Erie, where Drummond besieged them in August.

### Where the accounts disagree
- **When it began.** Drummond says six. Bonnycastle and the Lundy's Lane Historical Society's 1912 memorial say 17:30. Mansfield's life of Scott says about 40 minutes before sunset. Lieutenant Charles Anderson of the Provincial Artillery Drivers wrote of fighting from 16:00. The Niagara Falls museums have Scott leaving the woods shortly after 19:00.
- **The armies.** The American Battlefield Trust gives 2,500 Americans and 3,500 British. The 1912 memorial gives about 4,000 Americans and 3,000 British. Bonnycastle claimed the Americans had 5,000.
- **The losses.** The British return gave 84 killed, 559 wounded, 42 prisoners and 193 missing; the American return of 30 July, 171 killed, 572 wounded and 117 missing. Graves revises the prisoners and missing to 169 and 55 British, and the American figures to 174 killed, 79 prisoners and 28 missing. Bonnycastle has 870 British and 930 American killed and wounded.
- **Riall's capture.** Drummond writes that Riall was taken by American cavalry as he was led to the rear. Modern accounts give the credit to Captain Ketchum's light company of the 25th Infantry.
- **Who sent Miller.** Most accounts have Brown give the order, and Miller answer "I'll try, sir". Brown's own report, as summarised by the editors of the Madison papers, has Ripley order Miller forward.
- **The guns.** Drummond says he took two American pieces but lost one in a mix-up in the dark. Graves blames the loss of the British guns on Drummond's failure to screen them with light troops.
- **The next morning.** Drummond says the Americans abandoned their camp, threw their baggage into the rapids and burned Street's mills. Anderson says the British held the hill until about 10:00 and then withdrew to Queenston.
- **Who won.** Both sides claimed it. Parks Canada says the Americans failed to drive the British off, which ended their offensive in Upper Canada. Barbuto calls it a British strategic victory. Brown blamed Ripley for not going back for the guns; Ripley asked for a court of inquiry.
