### The campaign
In 1780 the British took Charleston and beat the American southern army at Camden. Nathanael Greene took command of what was left in the autumn. With his army weak and the country round Charlotte stripped of food, he split it. On 21 December 1780 Daniel Morgan left Charlotte with about 600 men, Bearss says: Maryland and Delaware Continentals, Virginia militia and William Washington's dragoons. He went west, camped on the Pacolet at Grindal's Ford, and was joined by militia under Andrew Pickens.

Lord Cornwallis was getting ready to invade North Carolina. He feared Morgan would strike at the British post at Ninety Six. In early January 1781 he sent Lieutenant Colonel Banastre Tarleton after him, reinforced with the 7th Fusiliers, a troop of the 17th Light Dragoons and a second 3-pounder. Tarleton drove his men hard over swollen rivers. On 16 January he crossed the Pacolet, and Morgan fell back.

Morgan stopped that day at Hannah's Cowpens, a frontier grazing ground. The Broad River, in flood, lay about 6 miles behind him. He chose to fight rather than be caught crossing it. More militia joined him that afternoon. Tarleton woke his men at 02:00 on 17 January and marched through the dark.

### The places
- **Hannah's Cowpens**: a frontier grazing ground for cattle, where Morgan camped on 16 January.
- **The two rises**: the gentle slope up which the British attacked, with the Continentals on the first crest and Washington's horse hidden behind the second.
- **The Green River Road**: the road along which Tarleton fled towards Cornwallis's camp.
- **The Broad River**: in flood about 6 miles behind Morgan, so that his men had nowhere easy to run.
- **The Pacolet River and Grindal's Ford**: Morgan's camp in late December, and the river Tarleton crossed on 16 January.
- **Ninety Six**: the British post in the backcountry that Cornwallis feared Morgan would attack.
- **Charlotte**: where Greene split his army in December 1780.

### The leaders
- **Daniel Morgan** (Americans, commander): aged 45, a veteran of Quebec and Saratoga, and ill with rheumatism. He planned the battle in three lines.
- **John Eager Howard** (Americans, the third line): commanded the Continentals and the Virginia men on the crest.
- **Andrew Pickens** (Americans, the militia): commanded the second line of Georgia and Carolina militia.
- **William Washington** (Americans, cavalry): commanded the Continental dragoons and the mounted militia held behind the rise.
- **McDowell** and **Cunningham** (Americans, skirmishers): led the picked riflemen in front, about 60 each; Cunningham's were Georgians.
- **Banastre Tarleton** (British, commander): aged 26, commander of the British Legion. He escaped.
- **Lord Cornwallis** (British, commander in the South): not on the field. He heard the news that evening.
- **Robert Jackson** (British, surgeon): gave Tarleton his horse when Tarleton's was shot, Bearss says.

### The battle
Before dawn Morgan went among the militia. Thomas Young recalled him telling them to hold up their heads, and "three fires, and you are free". He drew up his men on the slope in three lines. In front were about 120 picked riflemen under McDowell and Cunningham, told to aim at officers and sergeants. About 150 yards behind them stood Pickens' militia, asked to fire two volleys and then fall back behind the Continentals. On the crest about 150 yards further back stood Howard's Continentals with Virginia militia and state troops. Washington's horse waited out of sight behind the second rise.

The British reached the field at sunrise, about 07:00, tired and short of food and sleep. Tarleton formed his light infantry, Legion foot and the 7th Fusiliers in line, with his two 3-pounders and 50 dragoons on each flank. The 71st Highlanders and about 200 Legion horse stood in reserve. He attacked at once. Young remembered the British line as "the most beautiful line I ever saw".

The riflemen fired and fell back on the militia. The militia fired and then went back round the Continentals' left. British dragoons rode at them, and Washington's horse came out and drove them off. Then the British foot came up the slope against Howard. For about 15 minutes, Bearss says, neither side gained ground.

Tarleton sent the 71st round the American right. Howard ordered the company on his right flank to face about and wheel to meet them. The men took it as an order to retreat and marched back, and the line went with them. Morgan rode up and asked why they were retreating. Howard said he was trying to save his right flank. The British, thinking the Americans beaten, came on in disorder. The Continentals halted, turned and fired at close range, then charged with the bayonet.

Washington's horse struck the British from the flank and rear, and the militia came back round the other flank against the Highlanders. The British foot and the 71st surrendered with their two guns. Tarleton tried to bring his reserve horse into the fight, but most of them rode away. With the few who stayed he charged Washington's horsemen, fought hand to hand, and escaped down the Green River Road. The fighting had lasted under an hour, and by 08:00 it was over. Morgan buried the dead and marched north with his prisoners. On 18 January Cornwallis wrote to Clinton that it was "impossible to foresee all the consequences that this unexpected and extraordinary event may produce".

### Where the accounts disagree
- **The American army.** Morgan reported about 800 men, two-thirds militia. The NPS gives at least 1,500, Mount Vernon about 1,000, and the American Battlefield Trust about 1,065. Babits, working from pension statements, puts it at about 2,000 (Wikipedia gives his figure as 1,887-1,912).
- **The riflemen.** Bearss counts about 120 skirmishers; Mount Vernon about 150; Babits more than 300.
- **The British army.** Morgan gave at least 1,150 with the baggage guard, and said British officers owned to 1,037 in action. Bearss gives nearly 1,100; Babits 1,250.
- **The British losses.** Morgan reported about 110 killed, about 200 wounded and 531 prisoners. Bearss gives 110 killed and 830 prisoners, 200 of them wounded. The Trust gives 110 killed, 229 wounded and 529 taken.
- **The American losses.** Morgan's returns give 12 killed and 61 wounded. Babits argues these covered only the Continentals; he gives about 25 killed and 124 wounded, and the NPS 24 and 120.
- **The militia.** The common account, from Morgan's plan, has them fire two volleys and fall back in order. Babits says they fired at least five volleys at close range and were driven off by the bayonet.
- **The mistaken order.** Bearss names Wallace's Virginia company as the one told to face about. Babits says Howard was outflanked and tried to turn a company against the Highlanders.
- **The night march.** The NPS and Lieutenant Mackenzie have Tarleton start at 02:00; Bearss has him call in his pickets at 03:00.
- **Washington and Tarleton.** The NPS, Mount Vernon and the Trust all describe a fight between the two. Mount Vernon has Tarleton shoot Washington's horse; the Trust has Tarleton's own horse shot. Wikipedia notes that Tarleton never mentioned a duel, was not wounded, and that the story may not be true.

!!! story "His seventeenth birthday"
    Thomas Young turned 17 on the day of the battle. He rode with Major Benjamin Jolly's South Carolina
    mounted militia. When they came out of the woods, he wrote, "We made a most furious charge", through
