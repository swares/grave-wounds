### The campaign
Gustavus Adolphus of Sweden landed in Pomerania in June 1630. He beat the Imperial and Catholic League armies at Breitenfeld in September 1631 and on the Lech in April 1632. The Emperor recalled Albrecht von Wallenstein to command. On 3 September 1632 Gustavus attacked Wallenstein's camp at the Alte Veste near Fürth and was beaten off.

Wallenstein then marched north into Saxony to join Heinrich Holk, hoping to drive the Elector of Saxony out of the war and draw the King after him. Leipzig fell at the start of November. Gustavus followed by forced marches and reached Naumburg.

On 14 November Wallenstein decided to go into winter quarters round Leipzig. He sent Pappenheim's corps west towards Halle. The next day the Swedes learned of it. Gustavus left Naumburg, took Weißenfels and, on 15 November, drove Isolani's Croats from the crossing of the Rippach. The delay cost him the daylight. His army spent the night in battle order in the open, about 2 km from Lützen. Wallenstein recalled Pappenheim at midnight and had his men dig along the Leipzig road through the night.

### The places
- **Lützen**: a small town on the road from Weißenfels to Leipzig. It anchored the Imperial right and was set on fire during the battle.
- **The Via Regia**: the high road to Leipzig. Its ditches were deepened overnight and lined with Imperial musketeers.
- **The windmill hill**: a low rise by Lützen with three windmills and the main Imperial battery.
- **The Floßgraben**: a canal across the Swedish approach, which they had to cross before forming up.
- **The Rippach**: the crossing between Weißenfels and Lützen where the Croats held the Swedes on 15 November.
- **Naumburg and Weißenfels**: the Swedish base and the town taken on the march.
- **Halle**: where Pappenheim was sent on 14 November, and from where he was recalled.
- **Leipzig**: Wallenstein's base, and the road of his retreat.
- **The Schwedenstein**: a boulder set up the day after the battle near where Gustavus fell. A chapel was built beside it in 1907.

### The leaders
- **Gustavus Adolphus** (Swedes, King of Sweden): led the cavalry of the right wing. He was killed.
- **Bernhard of Saxe-Weimar** (Swedes, left wing): took over command when the King fell and led the late attack on the windmills.
- **Knyphausen** (Swedes, second line): held the line with the reserve when the first line broke.
- **Nils Brahe** (Swedes): commanded the infantry of the centre, according to Niderost.
- **Franz Albrecht of Saxe-Lauenburg** (Swedes, with the King): rode with Gustavus when he was killed, and survived.
- **Albrecht von Wallenstein** (Imperial, commander): fought on the defensive behind the road and withdrew in the night.
- **Gottfried Heinrich zu Pappenheim** (Imperial, cavalry): aged 38. He rode back from Halle with his horse, was mortally wounded and died on the road to Leipzig.
- **Heinrich Holk** (Imperial): led the counter-attack on the Swedish centre.
- **Ottavio Piccolomini** (Imperial, cuirassiers): his horsemen fought on the Imperial left, where the King was killed.
- **Isolani** (Imperial, Croat light horse): held the Rippach crossing on 15 November.

### The battle
Fog lay over the ground on the morning of 16 November. The Swedes had formed by about 07:30, Niderost says, and held a service. The crossing of the Floßgraben and the fog slowed the deployment. The guns opened about 10:00, to little effect. About 11:00 the Swedish lines began their advance on the road.

On the Swedish right the King's horse drove the Imperial musketeers out of the road ditches and pushed back the cavalry beyond. Lützen was burning, and the smoke drifted across the centre. In the centre the Swedish brigades crossed the ditches. On the left Bernhard made little headway against the windmills.

Between 12:00 and 12:30 Pappenheim arrived with about 2,300 horse and struck the Swedish right. He was hit by a cannonball and musket balls, and was carried off. Holk counter-attacked in the centre. The Swedish brigades were driven back across the road with heavy loss. Both armies lost order, and the fighting broke into scattered firefights in the smoke and fog.

Some time after 13:00 Gustavus rode to help his foot. He was hit in the left arm. In the fog his small party ran into Imperial cuirassiers. He was shot in the back, stabbed and shot in the head. His body was found that evening among the dead on the Imperial side of the ditch. Knyphausen brought up the second line and held. Bernhard took command. By about 15:00 his first attack had failed and there was a lull.

The second phase is less well recorded. From about 15:30 the Swedes went forward again, retook the centre battery and, as the light failed, Bernhard's men took the windmill hill and its guns and turned them on the Imperialists. Fighting died down at dusk. About 18:00 Pappenheim's foot came up, about 2,900 strong, too late to attack. Wallenstein used them to cover his retreat, which began in the night. He left guns and baggage behind and more than 1,200 wounded, and fell back through Leipzig into Bohemia. The Swedes left the field about 11:00 the next morning.

### Where the accounts disagree
- **The armies.** Roberts gives the Swedes 18,996 men and 60 guns, and Wallenstein 16,770 men and 38 guns before Pappenheim's 2,300 horse came up. The Wikipedia summary gives the Swedes 18,738 and 60 guns and the Imperialists 19,175 and 43 guns; its text has Wallenstein at 13,000-15,000 before Pappenheim. Geijer says the King reckoned Wallenstein at 30,000.
- **The windmill battery.** Niderost has 14 guns on the windmill hill.
- **The losses.** The Wikipedia summary gives about 6,000 Swedish and 5,160 Imperial killed, wounded or taken; Roberts gives about 6,000 Swedish and an uncertain Imperial loss. Rickard gives 10,000 and 12,000. Nicklisch and her colleagues put the dead at 6,000-9,000.
- **The end of the day.** Wikipedia has Wallenstein order the withdrawal about 17:00; Roberts has the retreat begin about 20:00.
- **Who won.** The Swedes held the field and took the guns. Nicklisch and her colleagues note that the Imperialists took more Swedish colours, and say there was no clear victor. Wilson calls it a marginal Imperial tactical victory. Guthrie, quoted by Roberts, called it "a defeat for both sides".
- **The King's death.** The accounts of it differ in detail. Schürger judges the page Leubelfing's account unreliable. Brzezinski argues the Swedes fought on because the news was kept from them.
- **The fog.** Some writers think the smoke of burning Lützen made much of it.
- **The date.** 16 November in the Gregorian calendar; 6 November in the Julian, used in Sweden at the time.

!!! story "Forty-seven men"
    In 2011 archaeologists digging a trial trench on the north-east edge of Lützen, by the old Via Regia,
    found a pit 3.5 m by 4.6 m. It held 47 men, aged from about 15 to 50, most in their twenties. It was lifted
    out in two blocks of about 25 tonnes each and studied in Halle. More than half of the men had been hit by
    bullets, 21 of them in the head. Many carried old, healed injuries from earlier fights. Only five showed
    signs of a Scandinavian childhood. Nicklisch and her colleagues think most were foot of the Swedish Blue
    Brigade, caught by Imperial horse from the flank, though they say some may have been Imperial soldiers.
