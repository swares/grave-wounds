## The period's fighting {#fighting}

The battles in this book run from 1632 to 1867, and its armies from 1618 to 1877. In those
years gunpowder decided most fights. But most of what armies did was still marching, foraging,
digging and falling sick, not battle.

### Armies

In the Thirty Years' War most soldiers were hired. A prince contracted with **colonels**, who
raised regiments for him and were owed for them. Albrecht von Wallenstein raised the Emperor's
army this way in the 1620s and grew rich on it. Peter Wilson argues that credit, more than
plunder, held the system together, and that the sums owed to colonels and men made armies
hard to pay off and the war hard to end.

Sweden was different. Under Gustavus Adolphus's law of 1619 the parish priest listed every boy
who reached 15. When the Riksdag voted a levy, the men were taken in groups of ten and one of
each ten was chosen. These **conscripts** filled the infantry; the horse and the guns were
mostly volunteers. They did not last. Lars Ericson counts the Swedish share of the king's army
at about 85% in 1621, a little over half when it landed in Germany in 1630, and only 10 to 15%
at Lützen. The rest were Germans, Scots and other hired men. Disease, cold and hunger killed
far more of the conscripts than battle did.

By the American Revolution every great power kept a **standing army**. Britain's was raised
by voluntary enlistment. Recruiting parties with a drum worked the fairs and inns, and the
bounty was the lure; in 1812 a man who signed on for life was promised nearly £24. Many were
driven to it by poverty. The **Continental Army** first enlisted men for a year, as colonial
troops had served. From late 1776 Congress set each state a quota of regiments and asked for
men for three years or the war. Beside them served the states' **militia**, called out for a
campaign, as at Cowpens.

Revolutionary France found another way. The **levée en masse** of 23 August 1793 called on the
whole nation, and Caiani puts the army it raised at about 600,000. From 1798 France called a
class of young men every year: **conscription**, which fed Napoleon's armies. The Russian
army of the Crimea was drawn from serfs and other poll-tax payers, chosen by lot in their
villages. In 1825 a line soldier served 25 years; the term was cut to 20 in 1834 and to 12 in
1855.

The American Civil War began with volunteers raised by the states. They came forward so fast
in 1861 that some were turned away. The Confederacy passed a **draft** in April 1862 and the
Union in 1863; both let a drafted man send a substitute, and the Union also let him pay $300.
Bounties brought many more men in than the draft did.

The **Plains nations** had no army. A Lakota, Cheyenne or Arapaho man went to war with a party
led by a man others chose to follow. The **men's societies** gave the camp its order. Among the
Cheyenne the societies (the Kit Foxes, the Elk Horn Scrapers, the Dog Men and others) took
turns keeping discipline, policing the buffalo hunt and leading in war. The Dog Men became a
band of their own and were the "Dog Soldiers" of the 1860s. Among the Lakota the council named
**akicita**, marshals, who kept the camp and the hunt in order and could punish anyone who
broke ranks. Honour came from **counting coup**: striking or touching an enemy and coming away
unhurt counted for more than killing him.

### Arms

In 1632 a musketeer fired a **matchlock**, its powder lit by a smouldering cord. It was slow,
heavy and useless in rain. **Pikemen** guarded the musketeers against horse. A Swedish company
had about 4 muskets to 3 pikes, and as the century went on the musket gained. By about 1700
the bayonet on the musket's muzzle had made the pike unnecessary, and armies gave it up.

The **flintlock musket** of the 18th century, Britain's "Brown Bess" or the French Charleville,
fired a lead ball of about three-quarters of an inch. A trained man loaded and fired about 3
times a minute. The ball fitted loosely, so the musket was quick to load and inaccurate: a man
could be hit at about 100 yards, and beyond that it was luck. Hence the **volley**. The
**rifle**, grooved inside, was far more accurate but slower to load, and the American long
rifle took no bayonet. Riflemen fought as skirmishers ahead of the line.

The **percussion cap** of the early 19th century replaced the flint and pan, and misfired much
less in the wet. Then about 1849 Claude-Étienne Minié and others made the **Minié ball**: a
conical bullet of soft lead, small enough to drop down a rifled barrel, whose base spread when
fired to grip the grooves. Every infantryman could now carry a rifle that loaded as fast as a
musket. The British and French had **rifle-muskets** in the Crimea; most Russian infantry still
had smoothbores. In the Civil War the .58 Springfield and the Enfield were the common arms,
accurate to about 500 yards.

**Breech-loaders**, loaded at the rear, came next. The Sharps fired perhaps 8 to 10 times a
minute, and the Spencer, with a 7-round magazine, more. By 1867 the Army's infantry on the
Bozeman Trail had breech-loading Springfields. The men at Fetterman's fight the year before
had had muzzle-loaders, apart from the cavalry's carbines and two civilians' repeating rifles.

On the Plains most warriors fought with the **bow**, the lance, the club and the shield. Some
had guns, traded or taken, and more after each victory over the soldiers.

The **guns** fired three kinds of round. **Round shot**, a solid iron ball, was for smashing
walls, but on open ground it bounced on and could cut through a file of men. **Shell** and
**spherical case** were hollow and burst over the enemy on a fuse. **Canister**, a tin full of
iron balls, turned a gun into a great shotgun out to about 400 yards. Against men coming on
across open ground, canister did the worst damage of all.

### Battles

Muskets that could not aim had to be fired together. So infantry fought in **line**, three
ranks deep in most armies and two in the British, to bring every musket to bear. The line was
slow and hard to keep. To march, to cross broken ground or to attack, battalions formed
**column**, which moved faster and held nervous men together, then deployed into line to fire.
Light infantry and riflemen skirmished in open order ahead of both.

Every shot made **smoke**. After a few volleys a line could see little beyond its own front,
and on a still day the smoke hung. At Raymond in 1863, Warren Grabau writes, smoke and dust cut
the view to a few yards, and regiments came out of the woods where no one expected them. No
one saw the whole of a battle, which is one reason the accounts of every field in this book
disagree.

Horsemen still charged. Against them infantry formed a **square**, the front ranks kneeling
with bayonets out and the rear ranks firing. Horse rarely broke a steady square, but a square
was a dense target for guns.

The rifle-musket changed less than men expected. It gave the defender more range and the
attacker more ground to cross under fire. From the Crimea on, armies dug. The siege lines
before Sevastopol held for 11 months; the Union and Confederate armies faced each other in
**trenches** at Petersburg for nine and a half months in 1864-65. Men who had dug in were very
hard to drive out.

### Wounds and surgeons

A soft lead ball did not pass cleanly through a man. It flattened and turned, shattered the
bone it struck, and carried in pieces of cloth. The Civil War surgeons described the bullets
they drew out as bent into "all imaginable forms". A ball through a limb bone often meant the
limb had to come off. **Amputation** was the commonest operation of the Civil War: three of
every four, one account says. Done quickly, it stopped the damage before gangrene set in. A
wound to the belly or the chest was usually fatal.

Until 1846 every operation was done on a man awake. On 16 October 1846, at the Massachusetts
General Hospital, William Morton put Gilbert Abbott to sleep with **ether** while a surgeon
removed a tumour from his neck. On 4 November 1847 James Young Simpson in Edinburgh found the
effect of **chloroform**, and it soon replaced ether. Crimean and Civil War surgeons used both.

What no one understood was infection, and disease killed more than battle. Of about 21,000
British soldiers who died in the Crimean War, about 16,300 died of disease. Florence
Nightingale reached the base hospital at Scutari, across from Constantinople, on 3 November
1854. The building sat on a broken sewer and had no fresh air; by February 1855 the death rate
there had reached 42%. A man had a better chance in the hospitals at the front. The death rate
fell only after a sanitary commission repaired the drains and the ventilation. In the Civil War
about two-thirds of the roughly 620,000 dead died of disease. The **US Sanitary Commission**,
founded in 1861, raised money, supplies and nurses for the Union's sick and wounded, and
Jonathan Letterman gave the Army of the Potomac an organised ambulance service.

!!! story "Captain Molly"
    On 16 November 1776 British and Hessian troops stormed Fort Washington, on Manhattan.
    John Corbin, a Pennsylvania artilleryman, was killed at his gun. His wife Margaret had
    followed the regiment to cook, wash and nurse, and was beside him. She took over the gun
    and kept it firing until grapeshot tore her shoulder and chest. She never had full use of
    her arm again. On 6 July 1779 Congress gave her half a soldier's pay for life and a suit
    of clothes: the first pension the United States gave a woman for military service. She
    was known as "Captain Molly". She is not "Molly Pitcher", who is a composite legend drawn
    from several women; Mary Hays at Monmouth in 1778 is the other woman most often named.

!!! story "Where the Girl Saved Her Brother"
    On 17 June 1876 Lakota and Cheyenne warriors met General Crook's column on Rosebud Creek,
    in Montana. During the fighting the horse of Comes in Sight, a Cheyenne, was shot and he
    was left on foot among the soldiers. His sister, Buffalo Calf Road Woman, rode in under
    fire, took him up behind her and carried him out. A Cheyenne artist drew the ride in a
    ledger book now in the Smithsonian. The Cheyenne call the fight "Where the Girl Saved Her
    Brother". Eight days later she was at the Little Bighorn. She died of diphtheria in 1879,
    a prisoner at Fort Keogh.

!!! story "The doctress"
    Mary Seacole, a Jamaican who had nursed cholera and yellow fever in the Caribbean and
    Panama, was in London when the war with Russia began. The War Office would not send her,
    and Florence Nightingale's party would not take her. She paid her own way. In 1855, with
    Thomas Day, she opened the "British Hotel" near Balaklava, a store, canteen and place to
    rest, and sold food and medicines to the army. She went up to the front to tend the
    wounded. When the army left in 1856 she came home with unsold stock and debts and was made
    bankrupt. Her book, *Wonderful Adventures of Mrs Seacole in Many Lands*, came out in 1857.

### Sieges and raids

Many of the years in this book were sieges. In the Thirty Years' War a town that would not
yield could be stormed and sacked. When Tilly's army took Magdeburg on 20 May 1631, the town
burned in a day; perhaps two-thirds of its 30,000 people were killed or died afterwards of
hunger and exposure. Armies lived off the land, and soldiers took what they wanted from the
farms and villages they passed through. Sevastopol and Petersburg were sieges of trench,
battery and sap that lasted most of a year.

On the Plains, war was mostly raiding: for horses and honours against enemy nations such as
the Crow and the Shoshone, and from 1866 against the wagon trains, wood trains and herds of
the forts on the Bozeman Trail.

!!! gm "For the GM"
    Expect a black-powder fight to be decided at close range. The muskets of 1632 to 1815 hit
    little beyond 100 yards, and the volley and the charge settle it; the rifle-musket reaches
    farther but fights by the same rules. Guns matter most when the enemy comes close or comes
    deep: canister for the first, round shot through a column for the second. Men behind works
    are hard to shift, and the attacker must close and charge. Breech-loaders are different:
    from the Wagon Box on, a serious wound puts a man down and the fire is fast. After the
    fight, there are no antibiotics. Wound fever and the camp's sickness will kill more than
    the battle did, and a clean camp and a good surgeon are the only help.
