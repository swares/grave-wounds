Grave Wounds Field Map

Battles of hundreds: the rank and file fight as units, a minute an exchange, each hex 10 m. Set up the units, then each exchange side A moves, side B moves, and every fight and volley is resolved together through the wound tables. Morale decides most fights; the rout does most of the killing.

The field

Each unit is a block of hexes along its front (the dark edge), facing the arrow. Close order holds 40 men a hex, open order 20. Units touching across a front fight; archers shoot at the nearest enemy in range in front of them.

          Fire support

          Off-map artillery and mortars on call. On its side's move, pick a battery, Call fire, then click a hex one of the side's units can see. Field guns and howitzers land the next exchange, mortars this one; each shell scatters. A battery that has fired is located (◉): the other side's guns can fire on it (Counter-battery) with no one needing to see it, knocking out tubes and killing and wounding crews (a tube without enough men falls silent). Displace moves it: silent for an exchange or more, then no longer located. Place it (in set-up) puts a battery's guns on the map: it reaches only its range from there, and an enemy within a hex overruns it. A creeping barrage can take a second leg (Then). Smoke drifts with the wind. Aircraft are called the same way: fighter-bombers come in two exchanges later with bombs and napalm over a wide area, two sorties an aircraft; helicopter gunships at once with rockets and guns. They are never located, do not fly in fog, storms or heavy snow, and fighter-bombers not at night. At night fire can be called only where the light lets someone see it fall; Illumination (mortars and guns) needs no one to see the hex and lights it and the hexes round it for two exchanges, as does a flare (Clicking the map).

            Heroes

            Named fighters from the wound roller can join a unit: in its front rank, shooting with it, or behind it. They fight with their own skill, and their wounds go back to the roller.
