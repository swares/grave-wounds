# The march and the camp {#travel}

Between battles the team marches, camps and sickens. The [travel map](travel.html) moves
forces across a map of hexes, usually 0.6 mile (1 km) across, on one clock for everyone.

## Marching {#marching}

A force marches at its kind's road speed:

{{table:speeds}}

Other ground takes longer; a step from one hex to the next costs the average of the two:

{{table:terrain}}

The march starts {{t_start}} hour after sunrise and stops {{t_stop}} hour before sunset, up to
{{t_hours}} hours a day: a short winter day is a short march. Mud and snow slow it (next
chapter), and a flooded ford cannot be crossed. These speeds are for a small, fit band; a
large army's column made less ground a day.

**The clock.** Every force on a map keeps the same time, and it moves on only when the GM
moves it: an hour, to nightfall, or until something happens. **Orders** say whether a force
marches on its route or halts. When forces of different sides come within {{t_within}} hex of
each other, the clock stops and both halt: the fight can be set up, on the roller's battle
map or the field map, or they can be given new orders.

## On the road {#events}

An army loses men without a battle. Each night every fit man takes his era's chance of
**deserting**, of **falling out** and being lost to the force, and of being **injured** (a fall,
a kick, an accident: he joins the sick and wounded), and every man of a mounted force or a
wagon train of losing his **horse**. The chances depend on what the force did that day: in camp
they are small (Valley Forge lost 3 men in 10,000 a day to desertion); on the march larger; and
**withdrawing** far larger (the retreat to Corunna lost 1 in 100 a day, the British retreat from
Mons nearly 2). A big force simply loses more men. Then there is a chance of an **incident**: a
d100 on the era's table (foragers set on by peasants, a bridge down, a convoy ambushed, a mine
in the road, a checkpoint), some for small parties or wagons only, some costing men or a step
of fatigue, each with a hook for the GM. The rates come from the records where they exist and
are estimates where they do not; the incidents are estimates. At the GM's choice, named
characters take the injury chance too.

A force **short of food** deserts about fifteen times as fast (the starving camp at Nuremberg in
1632) and straggles five times; one whose **pay is in arrears** deserts three times as fast.
The larger applies, not both. A mounted force keeps its count of **horses**: lost horses, and
the horses its deserters ride off on, come off it, and under half a horse a man it marches at
foot pace. A wagon train short of teams slows to three quarters, and under half its teams to
half. The GM adds remounts when the force finds them.

## Loads {#loads}

What a man carries depends on his **order**: fighting order (weapon, ammunition, water), marching
order (with his pack) or full kit (with extra rations and the rest). Each era has its own weights,
from the records where they exist: about 12 kg in fighting order for a Napoleonic redcoat and 27 kg
marching, 27 kg on the Somme in 1916, 43 kg on an approach march in Afghanistan. Armour is added
where the era's orders leave it out (a medieval man-at-arms' harness, 12 to 22 kg). His **load**
as a share of his body weight (his era's average man's; less for a woman), against his Strength
(×0.8 under 30 up to ×1.3 at 75), falls in a band:

{{table:loads}}

**On the march** a force carries its era's marching order unless the GM changes it. The roads'
paces are already those of men in marching order, so a heavier order slows the force (the
band's march against the usual band's) and tires it more, and a lighter order is no faster. Each
named man who marches takes his band's chance of blistered feet (a minor wound on his track): the
full chance for 20 km or more, less for a shorter day, a quarter once his feet have hardened after
a week on the road, and none while they are still sore.

**What he buys weighs.** Each thing bought from the price list adds its weight to his load: a
musket about 5 kg, a blanket 2, a canteen 1.7, a mail hauberk 9.3 (from the records where they
exist, the rest estimates). Food, a bed, a fare, a service and an animal weigh nothing on him.
Taking the purchase back off his ledger takes its weight off too. **Coin weighs**: about a tenth
of a kilo for a hundred medieval pennies, and a fat purse of silver is a load; paper money from the
Civil War on next to nothing.

**Pack animals and carts.** A packhorse, mule or wagon bought from the price list carries kit off
him, though never what he fights in (his fighting order and his armour):

{{table:pack_animals}}

Wheels are slow off the road: a force with a cart, wagon or vehicle among its named men marches at
{{wt_off_road}}% off the road and at {{wt_mud}}% of that in mud. Where the army does not feed it and
the ground cannot graze it (open country, farmland, heath and hills graze), each animal eats its
fodder from its owner's purse every night; a vehicle burns its fuel wherever it is. A man with no
money for it sees his animals go short.

**In a fight** a man's band costs his Dodge (his defence is the better of Parry and Dodge less
that), and Overloaded or worse he cannot run. A green man (one term) fights a band heavier under
fire. Each era fights in its usual order (the British of the Napoleonic wars with their packs on;
most others in fighting order). A side may **drop its packs** before the fight; if it loses the
field, the packs are lost with it, and that night its force has no blankets or rations.

## Water, heights and pursuit {#hazards}

**Swimming.** Each man is a non-swimmer, a basic or a strong swimmer, rolled when he is made from
his era's rough shares (most medieval men could not swim; perhaps a third of 1940s soldiers could
not; a seaman can). In deep water he rolls each minute: d100 at or under his Endurance and:

{{table:swim_mods}}

A success holds him, a critical lifts him a step, a failure takes him down one (a fumble two):
afloat, struggling, going under, drowning, drowned. He may shed his pack (a minute, a load band
lighter); plate cannot be shed in the water. Reaching the bank after drowning, he is near-drowned:
two steps of fatigue. **Cold water** takes his breath the first minute (a Nerve check, −20 below
15 °C, −40 at 5 °C or less: failing, a step down), and after a while his limbs fail: from 10
minutes in icy water, 45 at 10-15 °C, he swims 10 worse for every ten minutes more (the 1-10-1
rule; in 10 °C water half of a group of competent swimmers had failed within an hour).

**Fords.** Infantry could wade to about 3 ft, cavalry 4 ft, guns 2 ft 4 in. Each ford on the
travel map has its own depth and current (fixed by its place, deeper and faster in flood). In
water deeper than he wades, every named man is at risk; shallower, only a man with a Heavy load
or worse. He makes an Agility check (Ride for a horseman) at the current's difficulty:

{{table:ford_currents}}

It is the current, not the depth, that sweeps men away: the 10th Hussars' ford on the Kabul River
in 1879 was about 3 ft deep with a current of about 4 m/s, and 46 men drowned with 13 horses.
Swept off, he takes the water's cold shock (the week's warmth sets how cold it is; a flood is a
row colder), then swims for the bank for the current's minutes; a horseman's horse may drown
too. A force may choose to risk a flooded ford (never with wagons or guns), and before the motor
age baggage wagons cannot cross a ford deeper than 2 ft 4 in at all: they go round by a bridge or a
shallower ford. A map may give a ford its own depth and current, where the record has them.

**Climbing.** Agility + Fieldcraft ÷ 2, for each 10 m of a rope or ladder (3 m of a free climb):
a ladder or slope +20, a knotted rope 0, a wet rope or a rough wall −20, a sheer face −40, less for
his load and armour. A failure gains no height; a fumble is a fall. A rope or ladder may give way
once on the way (a grapnel pulls out, the defenders push the ladder off). At Pointe du Hoc the
Rangers climbed 26-30 m cliffs, the first men up within ten minutes.

**Falls.** By the height, d100:

{{table:falls}}

A deliberate feet-first jump that passes an Agility check, or soft ground, is a row lighter;
head first, rock, or a heavy load or armour, a row heavier. A moderate injury is a light crush
wound, a serious one serious, a grave one critical, and death a critical wound to the head; where
it lands follows how he fell (feet first: mostly legs and lower back; thrown from a horse: arms
and head). The dead in one modern study had fallen a median 15 m, the survivors 6 m.

**Chases** are rounds of about ten seconds, the gap close, near, far, distant or lost. Pursuer and
quarry roll Agility against each other (Ride on horseback); the winner moves the gap a step, two
on a critical, and a tie goes to the faster (a man is slower the heavier his load). A horseman on
open ground gains a step every second round whatever the dice; in cover the man on foot has +20
against him. A man on foot tires a step every three rounds, and a horse galloped more than
fifteen is blown. Most of the dead of a beaten army fell in the pursuit, not the fight.

## Patrons {#patrons}

A **patron** is a lord, a colonel, a governor, a band chief or a commanding officer: a man who can
do things for his clients and expects service. A man's **Standing** with each patron is a d100
figure, {{pt_start}} when he is taken on:

{{table:patron_bands}}

**Asking a favour.** d100 at his Standing + his Persuade ÷ {{pt_persuade}} (asking well), at the
favour's difficulty. Granted, it spends Standing: he expects it repaid (a critical spends
nothing). Refused, nothing changes; a fumble offends him ({{pt_offended}}).

{{table:favours}}

His word for a vacancy adds {{pt_promotion}} to the man's Command when it is next rolled. Two
clients who want the one favour or the one vacancy roll their Standing against each other.

**What moves Standing:**

{{table:standing_events}}

**The patron's fate.** At End of season each client gains {{pt_season}} for the season served,
then the patron rolls d100 on his era's line: he may die (his heir takes his clients on at half
Standing), fall from favour (each client rolls d100: at or under his Standing he shares the fall,
losing his Standing and a rank, or worse at the GM's word; otherwise he gets clear with half),
change sides (each client follows, keeping his Standing, or stays and loses it) or go (sold out,
disbanded, posted or rotated home: each client may take half his Standing to a new patron).

{{table:patron_fate}}

Of Hastings' sixty-seven indentures only two paid a fee: the rest rested on his promise of good
lordship. After Wallenstein's murder in 1634 seven generals who had signed for him were demoted
and imprisoned, and the men who betrayed him took the estates. Among the Lakota, generosity
counted for more than possession: horses given away raise a man's Standing as they raise his
honours.

## Prisoners {#prisoners}

A man **taken prisoner** is not simply lost. The GM picks his captors' situation from his era's
list, and any circumstances: a garrison stormed after refusing terms (+20), captors who had just
lost heavily (+15), one they gave no quarter (+30), a formal surrender (−25), a man visibly worth a
ransom (−20, where ransom was the custom), taken wounded (+5). Roll d100 less those figures: at or
under the first figure below he is killed out of hand, at or under the second stripped and robbed
(his purse is taken), and otherwise held with his things.

Each season (3 months) he is held: first a d100 for **release** (an exchange, parole, the war's
end); if not, an **escape** if he tries (Stealth, with the camp's figure, to get out, then
Fieldcraft to get home; retaken, he may be killed for it, and every recapture makes his captivity
harsher); then the **death** check at his captors' rate (doubled in a hard winter, halved for an
officer kept apart, a quarter for an officer on parole). Each season adds Strain, two in a harsh
camp (3% a season or worse), where he also loses a point of Endurance. Released, ransomed or
escaped, he rejoins the team. A player character with a fate point left is spared a roll that would
kill him, when taken or in captivity: he is held instead ([Lethality](#lethality)).

{{table:prisoners}}

**Ransom** (medieval, Thirty Years' War officers, Chechnya): about a year of his pay and his keep
for a medieval man, a month's pay for each step of rank for an officer of the Thirty Years' War,
and what a family could raise in Chechnya; paid from his purse or the leader's. **Parole**: an
officer of 1700 to 1865 may give his word, and is held freer and safer; escaping then breaks it.
Under the Dix-Hill cartel of 1862 men were paroled within ten days and exchanged by rank (a
corporal or sergeant 2 privates, a lieutenant 4, a captain 6, a colonel 15, a general 60); a
paroled man may not fight until he is exchanged. **Service**: in the Thirty Years' War and under
Napoleon a captured man of the ranks might join his captors' regiment, and desert back when he
could (at Stadtlohn in 1623 a thousand of four thousand prisoners joined Tilly, and most deserted).

**The team's own captives.** A man the team takes is kept on its list. Each End of season each
captive eats {{cap_keep}} Days a day from the leader's purse, and each not on parole tries to escape:
his Stealth {{cap_stealth}} against the team's best Watch, {{cap_crowded}} worse for each captive more
than the team's own number. He may be **ransomed** (his people pay the leader his era's ransom, an
officer's at {{cap_officer}} times a soldier's pay), **exchanged** for one of the team held, put on
**parole** (he gives his word, does not try to escape, and keeps himself), or **let go**.
**Questioning** him is words across tongues with the team's best speaker of his tongue, or its
interpreter: a critical, he tells all he knows; a success, something useful; a failure, little; a
fumble, a wrong answer believed. A captive's ransom is shared as loot is: the medieval third
goes to the leader's lord (a gift to a patron who takes thirds), the Swedish hospitals take their
tenth. When the team's side chases a beaten enemy on the field map, the prisoners taken in the
pursuit are offered to the team, as many as the GM will put in its charge. (A postwar report credited the US Army's German-speaking
interrogators, the Ritchie Boys, with nearly 60% of the credible intelligence gathered in Europe.)

## By night {#night}

A force may **march on by night**, up to {{nt_hours}} hours after the day's marching hours, at about
two-thirds of the pace (a 1979 US Army study timed night navigation about 40% slower). Its guide,
the best Fieldcraft among its named men, rolls once a night to keep the way: Ordinary on a road,
Hard off it. Failing, the night's march goes slower still; on a fumble the column spends
{{nt_lost}} hours on the wrong road, as the guides did at Stormberg in 1899, wandering the veld until
dawn. A night march of {{nt_min}} hours or more costs a step more fatigue.

## Boots and clothing {#kit}

Each named man's **boots** wear with every day he marches, faster on wet or muddy ground, and his
**clothing and blankets** with every day, twice as fast on a hard night. Lives in days:

{{table:kit_life}}

Worn boots raise his chance of blistered feet (half again; worn out, three times); worn clothing
makes his nights a step colder and wetter (worn out, two), for his recovery and for frostbite.
On a day his force does not march, its best Craft mends the most worn boots or clothing among its
named men (Mend kit: a success takes half the wear off, a fumble ruins them). He may buy new from
the price list (boots, shoes, a coat, a blanket), and a force its army feeds is issued new at its
depot. In October 1862 the 48th North Carolina returned 51 of its 619 men barefoot and 194 more
nearly so; at Valley Forge 2,898 men were unfit for duty, "barefoot and otherwise naked".

## Supplies {#supplies}

A force carries what it needs between fights: **ammunition** (a share of its full loads),
**rations** (days a man carries), **water** (litres a man) and **medical stores**:

{{table:supplies}}

**Rations.** A day its army does not feed it, and it has not foraged or hunted its food, it eats a
day's rations; with none left it goes hungry (desertion and straggling run higher, and its named
men have a hard night). A Union quartermaster reckoned that of eight days issued, hardly five could
be counted on. **Water.** By a river, lake, ford, marsh, bridge, village or town, or in rain, it
fills up; elsewhere each man needs {{sp_cool}} L on a cool day, {{sp_warm}} L on a warm one, {{sp_hot}} L in
heat and {{sp_vhot}} L in great heat; short of it, the force is thirsty: a step more fatigue and a
hard night. **Medical stores** serve {{sp_cases}} wounded or sick for each hundred men; each new
wounded man draws one and each sick man a seventh a day; with none left its sick and wounded are
cared for a step worse in camp. **Ammunition** goes into a battle on the field map as its share of a
load, and what is left comes back.

It refills everything at its army's **depot** (where it started on the map, or a place marked a
depot of its side, flagged ⚑ on the map: Harfleur and Calais for Henry V, Balaklava and Kamiesh
before Sevastopol, Hagaru-ri and Koto-ri at Chosin), draws from its **baggage train** in the same hex (the train carries
{{sp_train_rations}} days' rations, {{sp_train_ammo}} loads of ammunition and {{sp_train_med}} fills of medical
stores for {{sp_serves}} men, and a larger force takes more of it), and where its army does not feed it
its named men may **buy** a day's rations for every man in a village or town at their army's keep.

## Camps {#camps}

At the end of the march a force makes camp:

{{table:camps}}

Digging takes the work of the camp's ditches, banks, palisades and stakes, laid out as the
battle map would lay them, divided among the men ({{t_share}} of them work at once; the rest
guard and cook). Camp work can use what is left of the marching day and then {{t_evening}}
evening hours; beyond that it runs on into the next day.

**Surprise.** When a fight is set up from the travel map, the defenders may be caught
unawares: {{s_base}}%, plus the camp's figure above ({{s_none}} more with no camp at all),
+{{s_dark}} in the dark, +{{s_fog}} in fog, plus the attackers' best Stealth ÷ {{s_per}}, less the
defenders' best Watch ÷ {{s_per}}, and never under {{s_lo}}% or over {{s_hi}}%. Surprised men
cannot attack or move in the first round.

## Skills on the march {#march-skills}

The best named man in the force rolls; a skill used well is ticked.

**Billets.** A force its army does not feed must find its own quarters in a village or town: his
Persuade (no better than his tongue there). Fail, and no one will take them in. Quartered, its
named men pay no bed; in the Thirty Years' War the host fed them too (a Leipzig ordinance of
1639: soup, a vegetable, cheese, bread and beer). A force its army feeds is billeted by authority.

**Hunting for the pot.** In open country, heath, forest, hills or marsh, Fieldcraft at nightfall: a
deer feeds {{hunt_deer}} men for a day (Lewis and Clark's party ate 9 lb of meat a man), a buffalo
{{hunt_buffalo}} where they ran; a critical, twice that. Meat for the whole force feeds it however
short of food, and it is fresh.

**Finding the way.** Off the road, or in fog, snow, a storm or a sandstorm, the guide's Fieldcraft
once a day (Hard in fog, a blizzard or a sandstorm). Fail, and the day's march goes {{way_slow}}
times as long (night navigation ran about 40% slower in a 1979 US Army study); fumble, and an hour
is lost going the wrong way first.

## Finding work {#work}

Between fights a man may hire himself out. Where the team is (a town, city, village, camp, the
road, the frontier or at sea) decides which jobs are on offer; he may take any of them that his
career allows and his skill reaches (the least skill to be hired). Pay is in Days for each day
worked, or a sum for the whole contract, a horse or a body.

A job is worked in **terms**: a day, a week ({{w_week}} days), a month ({{w_month}} days) or a
contract of so many days. Each term he makes **one check** on the job's skill. At or under it,
he is paid in full; over it, {{w_fail}} of the pay; {{w_fumble}} or more and over, a fumble,
and nothing. A job with no skill pays in full.

A job with a **risk** takes one d100 a term as well: at or under the risk, trouble. The GM uses
the job's hook, or rolls a d10 on the era's trouble table. Jobs marked † are against the law,
and their trouble is the law's.

**Keep.** A soldier with his army is fed by it. A man who is not (off on his own, on leave, or
working for someone else) pays his **keep** from his purse each day: a day's food
({{keep_food}} for an English archer), and a bed ({{keep_bed}}) where there is one to be had:
in a town, city or village, or at an inn on the road (on the travel map, a night in a village or
town out of camp). A job marked (fed) feeds him while he works:
his army's own service, or wages paid with board. A day he cannot pay for his food he goes
hungry: it counts as a day without fresh food, and tires him a step. A night he cannot pay for
a bed he sleeps rough. The medieval jobs:

## Rank, pay and loot {#rank}

**Rank.** Each era has its ladder of ranks; a man starts at the bottom unless his career puts him
higher (a sergeant, an officer). The medieval ladder, from the Calais muster of 1346 (in truth a
man's place was his contract, and rises like an archer's to man-at-arms were rare):

{{table:ranks}}

**Promotion.** A rank falls **vacant** when a man of it is lost, or when the GM opens one for the
wider company's losses. At the end of a season each vacancy is offered to the team's men one
rank below who have served its years in that rank (or did a **deed** in battle, which waives the
time and adds +{{deed_bonus}}) and, where the army demanded it, can read and write (Letters
{{literate}} or more: British sergeants in the Napoleonic wars, officers in the Civil War). The most
senior rolls Command first, at the rank's difficulty; the first to succeed is promoted, and his
old rank falls vacant in turn. A man can be **reduced** to a lower rank at the GM's word. The roller suggests a deed for a man
who put down two or more enemies, or their leader, in a fight; the GM confirms it.

**Buying a commission.** In the British army of 1700 to 1871, most officers bought their rank. A
British officer (or a man about to become one) may buy the next rank when it falls vacant, at the
regulation price ({{buy_ensign}} for an ensign, {{buy_captain}} for a captain of the line), less the
price of the commission he already holds; the unofficial price ran half again to double, which the
GM may ask. On leaving the army he may **sell out** and take his commission's price with him.
Free commissions still came by death in action and to sergeants promoted from the ranks (the
ordinary roll above).

**Pay.** Each day his force is fed by its army, a man earns his rank's pay, less what the army
stopped for his rations and necessaries, paid into his purse at the end of the season. Days
when his force's pay is **in arrears** are owed instead, until the GM says the arrears are paid.
Horsemen and surgeons may draw more; a sutler draws nothing. Nurses and auxiliaries draw their
own army's rates: a Union nurse 40 cents a day and a ration, a Confederate one up to $25 a month
(more than twice a private's), a QAIMNS nurse of 1914 £30 to £35 a year, the ATS two-thirds of a
private's pay, and from 1947 an American army nurse a second lieutenant's; a vivandière, a camp
woman and a soldier's wife draw none.

**Loot.** After a won fight (the roller sees when one side alone is still standing, or the GM
names the winner, or the field map says who held the field), the field: each man rolls d100 (a stormed town's sack adds
{{sack_bonus}}, though only a 100 is a windfall):

{{table:finds}}

His era takes its share (the medieval captain a third, the Swedish hospitals a tenth). The
medieval third went to his lord: given to a patron who takes thirds, it is a gift and raises his
Standing. Where
plunder is forbidden (the Crimea's provost, the Lieber Code of 1863, the Hague rules of 1907),
or past what a man may keep as a trophy, the **provost** rolls his Watch against the man's
Stealth: if the provost wins, the loot is taken from him, and on the provost's critical or the
man's fumble the era's punishment follows (hanging, flogging, a court-martial). In the eras of
prize money each man draws his rank's shares (at Waterloo, a private one share of about 51
days' pay, a captain 35). A medieval or Thirty Years' War prisoner may be ransomed (with leave).

**Honours.** The Plains nations had no ranks and no pay. A man's honours come from coups
counted (the first to strike counts most: the roller counts it when his blow lands in close
combat) and horses taken from an enemy camp; his horses are
his wealth, and giving them away turns wealth into standing.

## Sickness {#sickness}

Before antibiotics, disease emptied armies faster than battle. Once a week every well man
may catch what the camp breeds, at a chance set by its **hygiene** (a bivouac is poor,
quarters good, other camps fair), by staying four weeks or more in one place, by the
ground (marsh, a town) and by the week's weather:

{{table:diseases}}

A case climbs to its peak after it shows, then gets better or worse each night on the
track of [Recovery](#recovery), with rest, a camp and shelter helping, and marching, cold
wet nights and filth hurting. Survivors of typhus and plague cannot catch it again; ague comes
back in marsh country.

- **What it costs.** Men at **{{d_unfit}}** or worse cannot dig, fight or march on their own;
  at **{{d_carried}}** or worse they are carried on litters. A force carrying litters marches at
  {{d_march}}%, or {{d_short}}% with fewer than {{d_bearers}} fit men a litter.
- **Scurvy.** After {{sc_weeks}} weeks without fresh food (fruit, greens, fresh meat, onions),
  each week every well man may fall ill: {{sc_chance}}% the first week, {{sc_per}} more each week
  after, against his Endurance. Fresh food heals it {{sc_heal}} steps a week. A scurvied man's
  old wounds open again.
- **Frostbite.** On a night of {{fb_below_f}} °F ({{fb_below}} °C) or colder, men without
  enough shelter may be frostbitten, a sickness on the same track.

!!! example "The retinue: on the road"
    The retinue marches north on foot: {{t_foot_day}} a day on the road when the days are long,
    less in March, when Towton was fought, and less again in the snow that fell that day. It
    makes a watched camp each night, with a sentry roster and fires; no tools are needed. In
    a fair camp, moving on every day, the flux is the only real danger; were it to sit
    in a filthy siege camp for a month, typhus would come for it.

!!! gm "For the GM"
    The travel map counts all of this for whole forces, and runs each named character's own
    case with his own Endurance when you put him **With** a force from the roller. Your job
    is the choices: how hard to march, where to camp, whether to wait for the sick.
